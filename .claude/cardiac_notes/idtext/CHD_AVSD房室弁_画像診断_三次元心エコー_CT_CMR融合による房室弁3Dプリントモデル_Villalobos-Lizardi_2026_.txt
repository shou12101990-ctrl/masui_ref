Abstract
Background Given the established and reproducible benefit of 3D-printed models in complex congenital heart 
disease (CHD) surgical planning, the focus has shifted from validating their utility to refining anatomical fidelity 
through enhanced imaging integration. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the perceived clinical utility of 
multimodality fusion 3D-printed cardiac models and to determine whether inclusion of valve structures confers 
incremental benefit over conventional single-modality 3D models in enhancing anatomical understanding and 
preoperative surgical planning among pediatric cardiac surgeons and imaging cardiologists in complex CHD.
Methods In this feasibility study, multimodality fusion 3D models were successfully generated by integrating cross-
sectional imaging (cardiac computed tomography and magnetic resonance) with 3D echocardiographic datasets to 
reproduce atrioventricular valve apparatus and subvalvular structures in 10 pediatric patients with complex CHD. Ten 
faculty members (7 pediatric cardiologists with advanced imaging expertise and 3 pediatric cardiothoracic surgeons) 
evaluated 10 patient-specific models using a structured Likert questionnaire.
Results Surgeons assigned significantly higher ratings than imagers for anatomical understanding (median 5 vs 
4; p = 0.002) and surgical planning (p < 0.001). Participants agreed that multimodality models are most valuable in 
complex congenital heart disease, particularly in cases requiring ventricular septal defect patching or intraventricular 
baffle repair involving the subvalvular apparatus.

 Background
The application of three-dimensional (3D) models in 
congenital heart disease (CHD) has moved beyond 
innovation to become an established component of care 
in highly complex cases. A robust and growing body of 
evidence has consistently demonstrated their clinical 
value in several key areas: enhancing the understanding 
of complex anatomies [ 1, 2], reducing the risk of misin -
terpretation and miscommunication [ 3–5], and most 
critically, optimizing surgical strategy [ 6]. By enabling 
procedural planning [ 7] and hands-on surgical train -
ing [ 8, 9], these models allow refinement of operative 
approaches and have been associated with improved 
surgical outcomes [ 10–12]. Given the consistency and 
reproducibility of these findings across centers and 
pathologies, the benefit of 3D models in complex CHD 
surgical planning is no longer in question [13, 14].
The focus is now shifting from demonstrating the value 
of 3D models to refining and perfecting their anatomi -
cal representation. Current 3D-printed cardiac models 
are frequently derived from a single imaging modality, 
which may limit the representation of complex ana -
tomical structures when image quality is suboptimal 
or incomplete. Most 3D models are derived from com -
puted tomography (CT) or cardiac magnetic resonance 
(CMR), both of which provide excellent delineation of 
the blood pool, great arteries, and extracardiac spatial 
relationships. However, these modalities offer limited 
characterization of valve leaflets, chordal structures, and 
annular morphology—critical anatomical components 
that are better defined by 3D echocardiography and often 
determine surgical strategy in complex congenital heart 
disease.
While single-modality approaches remain clinically 
valuable, emerging evidence suggests that the integration 
of multimodality imaging datasets may provide comple -
mentary anatomical information and improve model 
completeness in selected cases, particularly in the field of 
congenital heart disease [ 15–17]. By integrating comple -
mentary datasets—combining the volumetric and spatial 
resolution of CT/CMR with the superior valve definition 
provided by 3D echocardiography—a more anatomically 
Conclusion Multimodality imaging fusion for 3D printing is technically feasible and produces high-quality models 
with strong intraoperative correlation. Incorporation of atrioventricular valve anatomy provides meaningful 
incremental benefit—particularly for surgeons—enhancing operative planning in complex CHD.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the 
feasibility of integrating 3D echocardiography with CT/
CMR datasets to generate patient-specific 3D printed 
heart models incorporating atrioventricular valves in 
complex congenital heart disease. The secondary objec -
tive was to determine whether inclusion of atrioventricu-
lar valve anatomy confers incremental perceived benefit 
over conventional single-modality 3D models in enhanc -
ing anatomical understanding and preoperative surgical 
planning among surgeons and cardiac imagers.
Methods
The process to create a multimodality image fusion and 
3D printed model involved six sequential stages: Patient 
recruitment, acquisition of the medical imaging, imag -
ing segmentation, computer-aided design (CAD), image 
fusion with co-registration, and 3D printing (Fig. 1).
Patient recruitment
Eligible patients were those for whom cross-sectional 
imaging (CT or CMR), 3D echocardiography, and a 3D 
printed model had been requested as part of routine sur -
gical decision-making during multidisciplinary surgical 
conference meetings. At our institution, 3D models are 
typically requested in cases where there is uncertainty 
regarding the optimal surgical strategy, particularly when 
deliberation involves biventricular repair, staged univen -
tricular palliation, or potential biventricular conversion 
from a univentricular pathway. The clinical decision to 
generate a 3D model was made independently of study 
participation. Exclusion criteria included: inadequate 
image quality precluding accurate segmentation or mul -
timodality fusion; incomplete acquisition of the required 
imaging datasets (CT or CMR in conjunction with 3D 
echocardiography); contraindications to clinically indi -
cated imaging; and refusal or withdrawal of informed 
consent by parents or legal guardians.
Demographic and clinical variables—including age, 
sex, weight, primary cardiac diagnosis, and prior surgical 
history—were collected for all included patients.
Medical imaging
The choice of imaging modality—including CMR, CT, 
transthoracic echocardiography, or transesophageal 
echocardiography—as well as the requirement for gen -
eral anesthesia, was determined strictly according to clin-
ical indications and independent of study participation. 
For the purpose of multimodality fusion, patients under -
went either CT or CMR in conjunction with 3D echocar -
diography, with studies coordinated to be performed on 
the same day whenever feasible to minimize anatomical 
variation between datasets.
CT images were acquired using a 256-slice CT scan -
ner (Revolution CT, GE HealthCare, USA), following 
standard premedication protocols without beta-blockers. 
Imaging parameters were optimized for low radiation 
dose, blood pool visualization and imaging prospectively 
triggered by the patient’s ECG. Contrast agent (Visipaque 
320, GE Healthcare, USA) was administered through a 
peripheral vein, followed by 20–30% diluted contrast and 
saline tracers.
CMR was performed on a 1.5 Tesla scanner (MAGNE -
TOM Avanto Fit, Siemens Healthineers, Germany) using 
a cardiac-gated, respiratory-navigated 3D IR FLASH 
(Inversion Recovery Fast Low Angle Shot) sequence and 
intravenous injection of an extracellular gadolinium-
based contrast agent using a dual phase bolus injection 
and slow infusion technique [18].
3D echocardiograms were acquired on a Vivid TM E95 
Ultrasound System (GE HealthCare, USA), using 6VC-D

Statistical analysis
Continuous variables are described as median and inter -
quartile range. Evaluation of annulus ring geometry 
discrepancy between cross-sectional and 3D-Echocar -

responses between surgeons and imagers was performed 
using the Mann–Whitney U test. For all analyses, P val -
ues < 0.05 were considered significant. The statistical 
analyses were performed using Stata software, version 
11.2 (StataCorp, USA).

Computer aided design
Computer aided design (CAD) was performed using 
3-Matic Version 18.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium), a 
commercially available design software (Fig. 1). The ves -
sels, atria and annulus were expanded outward with a 
wall thickness of 1.2 mm using the hollow tool function 
[21]. The myocardium was kept as a full thickness model.
Geometry co-registration
CAD geometries from CT or CMR and 3D echocar -
diograms were imported into Autodesk Meshmixer 3.5 
(Autodesk Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA), (Fig. 2). A semi-
automated approach was used to align 3D echocar -
diograms with CT or CMR. The “ Align To Target” tool 
provided initial alignment, which was refined iteratively 
using the “Transform” tool to ensure a precise match (Fig. 
2). Once alignment was achieved, the final heart models, 
including valves, were saved as STL files.
3D printing
The J750 Digital Anatomy Printer (Stratasys Ltd, Eden 
Prairie, MN, USA) was used to create 3D models using 
VeroUltraWhite and VeroUltraBlack resins for high-
quality detail, and the Polyjet Vivid color set for vibrant 
surface anatomy representation [ 21]. Estimated material 
cost per model ranged from $300 to $500 CAD. Labor 
and overhead costs were not included due to significant 
variability across institutions.

Assessment of clinical utility of the 3D model
A total of 10 faculty members assessed the clinical utility 
of the 10 models. 7 pediatric cardiologists with imaging 
training and 3 pediatric cardiothoracic surgeons. Signifi -
cant intergroup differences among surgeons and imagers 
were observed in several domains. Overall, surgeons con-
sistently assigned higher usefulness ratings than imagers, 
see Fig. 5.
For anatomical understanding, surgeons assigned 
significantly higher ratings than imagers (median 5 
extremely useful vs 4 very useful; U = 1435.0, p = 0.002). 
Surgeons reported that the integrated models improved 
spatial perception and clarified complex anatomical 
relationships beyond conventional imaging and single-
modality 3D models. Surgeons rated the fidelity of the 
models with intraoperative findings predominantly in the 
higher usefulness categories, with most responses fall -
ing within “very useful” ( n = 11) and “extremely useful” 
(n = 8). Overall, these results indicate a generally strong 
perception of anatomical accuracy when compared with 
direct intraoperative inspection.
The most pronounced difference was observed in sur -
gical planning ( U = 1615.5, p < 0.001). Surgeons more 
frequently rated the models in the highest usefulness 
categories, emphasizing that incorporation of atrioven -
tricular valve anatomy facilitated operative strategy for -
mulation. Specifically, surgeons noted that having valve 
anatomy integrated within the 3D model simplified 
procedural planning by eliminating the need for mental 
reconstruction from separate imaging datasets. In con -
trast, several cardiologist imagers indicated that con -
ventional imaging modalities already provided sufficient 
anatomical detail, and that inclusion of valve anatomy 
within the 3D model did not substantially alter their 
planning approach.
In the domain of decision-making confidence, median 
scores were identical between groups (median 4 in 
both); however, the distribution of responses differed 
significantly ( U = 1305.5, p = 0.039), reflecting a greater 
tendency among surgeons to assign higher confidence 
ratings.

Discussion
Over the past decade, the clinical utility of 3D-printed 
cardiac models in complex CHD has been consistently 
validated across institutions, particularly in enhancing 
spatial understanding, facilitating multidisciplinary dis -
cussion, and supporting surgical planning [ 1, 3, 4, 6–11]. 
Given this established evidence base, further random -
ized trials aimed solely at reconfirming general utility 
are unlikely to provide meaningful additional insight; the 
field has therefore shifted from validation toward refine -
ment of 3D model design and application.
Traditionally, 3D-printed cardiac models have relied on 
single-modality imaging datasets, most commonly CT or 
CMR, which, although anatomically robust, may incom -
pletely represent dynamic structures such as the atrio -
ventricular valves and subvalvular apparatus. Previous 
studies have demonstrated the feasibility of CT-based 
3D modeling of atrioventricular valves [ 22], particu -
larly in adult populations undergoing structural heart 
interventions [ 23, 24]. However, in pediatric patients 
with congenital heart disease, valve segmentation from 
CT remains technically challenging due to smaller ana -
tomical structures and higher heart rates. In this setting, 
three-dimensional echocardiography provides superior 
characterization of valve morphology and subvalvular 
structures, with high accuracy for 3D printing and reli -
able reproduction of valves in CHD, albeit at the cost of 
increased processing time [ 25], Integration of multimo -
dality imaging, combining cross-sectional datasets with 
high-resolution 3D echocardiography, may enable more 
comprehensive and anatomically faithful cardiac repli -
cas. However, this approach requires advanced technical 
expertise, dedicated software, and significant time invest-
ment for segmentation and image fusion, which may 
limit its widespread adoption in centers with constrained 
resources.
Accordingly, this study was not designed to reassess the 
established role of 3D printing in complex CHD—now 
routinely integrated into surgical decision-making at our 
institution and many centers worldwide—but to evalu -
ate the feasibility of multimodality fusion incorporating 
atrioventricular valve anatomy and to determine whether 
this integration provides incremental value beyond con -
ventional single-modality 3D modeling approaches.
We demonstrated that generation of multimodality 
3D-printed models is feasible and provided a step-by-
step workflow to facilitate replication at other centers. 
However, feasibility comes with technical challenges.

The main limitation is temporal and spatial alignment 
between cross sectional imaging and echocardiography.
Accurate multimodality fusion requires ECG-gated or 
synchronized image acquisition to ensure datasets cor -
respond to closely matched phases of the cardiac cycle. 
Motion artifacts must be minimized: breath-holding 
under general anesthesia was used for 3D echocar -
diography, while respiratory navigation was applied for 
CMR angiography. Breath-holding was not required 
for CT angiography given its short acquisition time 
(typically < 2 seconds). Despite aiming for the highest 
possible spatial resolution, inherent differences exist 
between imaging modalities in both spatial and tem -
poral resolution. Echocardiography provided the high -
est spatial and temporal resolution (0.31 × 0.37 mm; 
8 ± 4 ms), compared with CT (0.33 × 0.62 mm; 120 ms) 
and CMR (0.57 × 1.1 mm; 140 ms). Consequently, minor 
inaccuracies are unavoidable when fusing datasets from 
different modalities due to these intrinsic resolution 
and timing disparities. As CT technology evolves, with 
improvements in spatial and temporal resolution and 
reductions in radiation dose, future studies may explore 
direct modality-to-modality comparisons and assess the 
feasibility of incorporating CT-derived valve anatomy in 
pediatric populations
We utilized up to 4 different software programs for 
segmentation and computer-aided design to maximize 
the efficiency of post-processing. Segmentation of the 
valvular leaflets from 3D echocardiograms is most diffi -
cult as the visualized valve leaflets show regional varia -
tion of the thickness or signal drop-out depending on 
the sonic beam angle to the parts of the object. Emerg -
ing techniques including machine learning for automatic 
heart segmentation of cross sectional imaging [ 26, 27]

Spatial alignment was a semi-automatic process, which 
required careful evaluation and expertise. As there are 
no easy landmarks to exactly coregister exact points 
between cross sectional and echo, manual adjustments 
are required. With expertise, we demonstrated that there 
were minimal differences between annular diameters in 
terms of diameter, cross sectional area, angulations. This 
was latter confirmed by intraoperative inspection where 
surgeons validated the fidelity of the models anatomy 
compared to intraoperative inspection.
Temporal synchronization can be solved by interpolat -
ing images and choosing the end diastolic phase in both 
imaging modalities. Differences in temporal resolution 
among imaging modalities is not a main problem when 
only one cardiac phase is to be used for 3D printing. 
The future will be using imaging fusion over one cardiac 
cycle, allowing visualization of heart contractility. For 
this functional evaluation, virtual reality and holograms 
would be an attractive complementary technology to 3D 
printing for a more comprehensive evaluation.
The secondary objective of this study was to deter -
mine whether multimodality 3D-printed models pro -
vide incremental perceived benefit over conventional 
single-modality models among cardiologists and sur -
geons. Although evaluations were performed follow -
ing structured case discussions with dedicated time for 
assessment, the conference-based setting may still dif -
fer from independent or prolonged model review, which 
could influence perception of incremental value.
In this study, surgeons consistently assigned higher use-
fulness ratings to multimodality 3D models than imagers, 
particularly in domains directly related to anatomical 
understanding, surgical planning, and decision-making 
confidence. While both groups generally considered the 
models “very useful, ” surgeons more frequently rated 
them as “extremely useful, ” and these distributional dif -
ferences reached statistical significance in all planning-
related domains. In contrast, perceptions of fidelity with 
intraoperative findings were comparable between groups, 
with no significant difference observed.
These findings may be explained, at least in part, by dif-
ferences in professional training and cognitive workflow. 
Cardiologists and cardiac imagers undergo extensive 
training in echocardiography and cross-sectional imag -
ing, developing the ability to integrate information from 
multiple, separate datasets—including two-dimensional 
and three-dimensional echocardiography, computed 
tomography, and magnetic resonance imaging—into a

This expertise may reduce their reliance on external

In contrast, surgeons may experience this integrative 
process differently. Although highly skilled in anatomi -
cal interpretation, their daily workflow does not routinely 
involve reconstructing anatomy from multiple imaging 
planes or modalities. For them, having all relevant ana -
tomical and valvular information consolidated into a 
single, tangible 3D model may reduce cognitive load and 
streamline preoperative planning. Rather than mentally 
integrating separate imaging datasets and echocardio -
graphic views, surgeons can directly visualize and manip -
ulate a unified representation of the anatomy. This may 
explain why surgeons more frequently rated the models 
as “extremely useful, ” particularly for surgical planning 
and confidence.
As with other advanced imaging technologies, their use 
is most appropriate in selected, high-complexity cases 
to ensure cost-effectiveness. Participants agreed that the 
greatest value lies in cases requiring patch or baffle repair 
of a ventricular septal defect (VSD), particularly when the 
subvalvular apparatus is involved or straddling. Complex 
anatomies, such as double outlet right ventricle (DORV), 
were specifically identified as scenarios in which detailed 
spatial understanding of valve–VSD–arterial relation -
ships is critical to surgical strategy.
Taken together, these results highlight that the clinical 
utility of multimodality 3D models may be profession-
dependent. Their greatest added value may lie in support-
ing specialists whose routine workflow does not involve 
complex multimodal image integration. From a transla -
tional perspective, this reinforces the importance of mul -
tidisciplinary collaboration, where imaging specialists 
and surgeons contribute complementary expertise.
Conclusions
In conclusion, this feasibility study demonstrates that 
multimodality imaging–derived 3D-printed models 
incorporating atrioventricular valve anatomy are achiev -
able and reproducible through fusion of cross-sectional 
imaging and high-resolution 3D echocardiography. By 
integrating complementary datasets from different imag -
ing modalities, these models provide comprehensive 
and anatomically detailed representations of cardiac 
structures, incorporating information that may not be 
captured by a single modality, and demonstrating good 
agreement with intraoperative findings based on sur -
geons evaluation. Multimodality 3D models provide sig -
nificant added value in preoperative assessment, with the 
greatest perceived benefit among surgeons, highlight -
ing their role as an integrative tool that bridges imaging 
interpretation and surgical planning. Clinical application 
may be particularly valuable for complex congenital heart 
disease cases—particularly in anatomies such as DORV 
and TGA, where intraventricular baffle repair or septa -
tion requires precise delineation of the atrioventricular 
valve apparatus and chordal attachments.
