AUTHOR'S SUMMARY
Complex transposition of the great arteries (TGA) with significant left ventricular outflow 
tract obstruction is a rare and technically demanding condition. Although several surgical 
techniques have been developed, each carries notable challenges. Recently, aortic root 
translocation has been revisited and increasingly adopted due to refinements that have 
improved early and long-term outcomes. Mastery of this procedure not only enhances 
the management of complex TGA, including congenitally corrected TGA, but also broadens 
the possibilities for achieving safe and reliable biventricular repair in patients with even more 
intricate congenital heart defects.
ABSTRACT
Transposition of the great arteries (TGA) is a congenital heart defect (CHD) characterized by 
discordance between the ventricles and great arteries. The arterial switch operation generally 
provides excellent early and long-term outcomes in cases in which both great arteries are 
well developed. Furthermore, various surgical techniques, including the Rastelli procedure, 
réparation à l’étage ventriculaire (REV) procedure, and pulmonary root translocation have 
been introduced to improve the outcomes in patients with complex TGA accompanied by 
significant left ventricular (LV) outflow tract obstruction. A common feature of these 3 
techniques is the intraventricular tunneling of the LV to the aorta through the ventricular 
septal defect while maintaining the original position of the aortic root. Recently, aortic root 
translocation (ART), which reconstructs the biventricular outflow tracts by repositioning 
the aortic root toward the pulmonary side, has been reevaluated as an alternative to 
conventional techniques. Early experiences with ART have reported technical challenges, 
in addition to high surgical mortality and morbidity rates. Nevertheless, with an increase 
in surgical experience, early outcomes comparable to those of non-ART procedures have 
been reported, along with improved long-term results. A key principle of ART is to preserve 
the integrity of the aortic root geometry. Various modifications have been introduced to 
achieve this. As a potential solution, we propose an aortic root reconstruction technique, 
which we briefly introduce here. Furthermore, ART has been applied in patients with

INTRODUCTION
Transposition of the great arteries (TGA) is a congenital heart defect (CHD) characterized 
by discordance between the ventricles and great arteries (Figure 1A-D).1) This discordance 
contrasts with the ventricle-to-great artery concordance seen in the normal heart, in which 
the aorta arises from the left ventricle (LV) and the pulmonary artery (PA) arises from 
the right ventricle (RV). Herein, “RV” and “LV” refer not merely to anatomical position but 
to their morphological characteristics. Morphologically, the RV and LV are distinguished 
by not only their structural features but also their functional and hemodynamic properties. 
Therefore, TGA is defined solely by the relationship between the ventricles and great 
arteries, in which the aorta arises from the morphological RV and the PA arises from 
the morphological LV , irrespective of the physical position of the ventricles.
This strict definition is necessary because this review article covers both complete TGA and 
congenitally corrected TGA. In congenitally corrected TGA, the LV is typically positioned on 
the right side of the patient, whereas the RV is located on the left side.
TGA can be further categorized based on the associated anomalies, particularly in the context 
of surgical considerations. In the presence of pulmonary stenosis or LV outflow tract 
obstruction (LVOTO), it can be classified as complex TGA, irrespective of the presence or 
absence of a ventricular septal defect (VSD) (Figure 1D). Distinguishing complex TGA is 
important because of its surgical implications. In patients with well-developed great arteries 
and without aortic or pulmonary obstruction, the condition can often be corrected by 
performing an arterial switch operation (ASO), which involves transposing the aorta and 
PA along with coronary artery transfer. Nevertheless, the condition is generally categorized as 
a complex TGA in cases where ASO is technically challenging or unfeasible.

SURGICAL STRATEGIES FOR COMPLEX TRANSPOSITION 
OF THE GREAT ARTERIES WHEN ARTERIAL SWITCH 
OPERATION IS NOT FEASIBLE
If ASO is not feasible, the PV cannot serve as an aortic valve, necessitating alternative surgical 
approaches. The most traditional option is the Rastelli procedure, whereas other strategies 
include the réparation à l’étage ventriculaire (REV) procedure, pulmonary root translocation 
(PRT), and aortic root translocation (ART) (Figure 1E-H).
The Rastelli procedure is performed in patients with complex TGA accompanied by severe 
LVOTO (Figure 1E). This procedure involves intraventricular tunneling to direct the blood 
flow from the LV through the VSD to the aorta, while a RV outflow tract (RVOT) valved 
conduit is placed from the right ventriculotomy to the main PA.8-10)
The REV procedure shares a concept similar to that of the Rastelli procedure, but differs in 
RVOT reconstruction (Figure 1F).11)12) In the REV procedure, the PA is sufficiently mobilized 
and either repositioned anterior to the aorta using the LeCompte maneuver or left in its 
native position.13) Instead of using a valved conduit, the PA is directly connected to the right 
ventriculotomy in a tissue-to-tissue manner.
PRT follows a similar intraventricular strategy, directing the LV outflow through the VSD to 
the aorta. However, in PRT, the pulmonary root is harvested and directly anastomosed to 
the right ventriculotomy without performing the LeCompte maneuver.14) The key advantage 
of PRT over the Rastelli or REV procedure is that it involves harvesting at the pulmonary root 
level rather than at the proximal main PA (Figure 1G). This provides a longer pulmonary trunk 
and facilitates RV-to-PA reconstruction.15)
A shared feature of these 3 methods is the intraventricular tunneling of the LV to 
the aorta through the VSD while maintaining the aortic root in its original position (Figure 1E-G).
Tateishi et al.16) reported that, unlike the Rastelli-type repair, PRT resulted in a more 
natural LV outflow on postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) compared 
to the preoperative state. Although PRT differs technically from the Rastelli and REV 
procedures, all 3 approaches share the same intracardiac limitation: they do not modify 
the native relationship between the VSD and the aorta. As a result, the LVOT is routed 
through a similarly positioned intraventricular baffle. As the patient grows, significant 
changes occur in the VSD, aortic root and valve, and baffle itself. Consequently, the likelihood 
of developing LVOTO in the long-term is inevitable.
In contrast, ART involves harvesting the aortic root and repositioning it posteriorly toward 
the pulmonary root area to complete LVOT reconstruction (Figure 1H).17) The anterior space 
was used for RVOT reconstruction.18) Unlike the other 3 approaches, collectively referred to as 
non-ART surgeries, ART enables more direct VSD tunneling for LVOT reconstruction, thereby 
establishing a direct connection from the LV to the aorta. This technique allows a more 
anatomically natural reconstruction of both outflow tracts within the heart. Externally, by 
relocating the aortic root from its anterior position to a more posterior location, ART creates 
an additional space between the heart and sternum. This spatial adjustment helps minimize 
RVOT compression even as the patient grows.

AORTIC ROOT TRANSLOCATION: SURGICAL PRINCIPLES 
AND MODIFICATIONS
In this review, the term ‘aortic root translocation (ART)’ refers broadly to all surgical 
techniques that involve harvesting and posteriorly translocating the aortic root into 
the pulmonary root region. This includes both the original Nikaidoh operation,17) which 
utilizes only the aortic root, and modifications involving both the aortic and pulmonary 
roots, such as the half-turned truncal switch (HTTS) and double root translocation (DRT) 
techniques.19)20)
The fundamental concept of ART was introduced by Nikaidoh in 1984.17) He developed 
this surgical technique after encountering a patient who died from bilateral outflow tract 
obstruction as a late complication of the Rastelli procedure.21) The Nikaidoh operation 
involves harvesting the aortic root and shifting it toward the pulmonary root to achieve 
a more natural alignment of the 2 outflow tracts, while minimizing compression of the RVOT 
by the sternum. Since the 2000s, this technique, with its excellent mid- to long-term 
outcomes, has been recognized as an important surgical option for patients with complex 
TGA.22) In the original Nikaidoh procedure, the aortic root with the attached coronary 
arteries is translocated posteriorly toward the pulmonary root. 17) However, this approach 
can lead to coronary artery kinking during posterior translocation, resulting in impaired 
coronary perfusion. To address this issue, a modified technique was introduced, in which 
the right coronary artery (RCA) button was separated from the aortic root. Instead of fully 
mobilizing the aortic root, partial mobilization was performed by pivoting it around the left 
main coronary artery, while keeping it connected to the ventricle on a pedicled myocardial 
attachment.22) The aortic root was then rotated posteriorly and reattached, and the RCA was 
subsequently reimplanted, thereby completing the modified Nikaidoh procedure.
Three major technical modifications were developed starting from the basic concept of 
the Nikaidoh procedure.
The first modification involves abandoning the PV and root, followed by partial or complete 
mobilization of the aortic root ( Figure 2).23)24) This allows for the posterior translocation 
of the aortic root to the pulmonary root, thereby creating the LVOT. Subsequently, anterior 
reconstruction of the RV-to-PA continuity is performed using various techniques such as 
a transannular patch or an RV-to-PA conduit.25)
The second modification, reported by Dr. Yamagishi in 200319) and later by Mair et al. in 2006,26) 
involves harvesting both the aortic and pulmonary roots en bloc, rotating them 180° and 
reattaching them to reconstruct the outflow tracts for both ventricles. This technique is known 
as the “half-turned truncal switch (HTTS)” or “en bloc rotation of the outflow tracts (EBR).”
The final modification introduced by Hu et al. in 200720) involves harvesting the aortic and 
pulmonary roots separately and exchanging their positions to reconstruct the outflow tracts 
of both ventricles. This technique is referred to as the “double root translocation (DRT).”

DISCUSSION
Applicability of aortic root translocation in complex transposition of 
the great arteries with pulmonary atresia or intact ventricular septum
For patients with complex TGA, ART requires sufficient space in the pulmonary root to allow 
posterior translocation of the aortic root. Yamagishi38) proposed the criteria for the surgical 
indications of ART and non-ART methods based on the size of this space, specifically 
the degree of LVOTO, as measured by the pulmonary-to-aortic annular diameter ratio. For 
example, the Rastelli procedure is considered suitable for severe pulmonary stenosis or 
pulmonary atresia. Yamagishi38) stated that ART was contraindicated in cases of pulmonary 
atresia. However, Kramer et al.39) reported that ART was performed in 14% of the patients 
with pulmonary atresia. Similarly, Lee et al.40) successfully applied the modified Nikaidoh 
procedure in patients with pulmonary atresia. Marcelletti et al.41) described 2 types of 
pulmonary atresia in complex TGA cases: approximately one-third of the patients exhibited 
subvalvular atresia, characterized by a completely undeveloped subpulmonary infundibulum, 
whereas the remaining two-thirds presented with valvular atresia, where the infundibulum 
was partially developed. In cases of valvular atresia, a subvalvular infundibulum develops, 
and this space can be utilized to perform ART.
Various surgical approaches have been proposed for the treatment of complex TGA of an 
intact ventricular septum. Esmaeil et al.42) reported that while ASO with concurrent LVOTO 
relief would be ideal, it may not be feasible when addressing pulmonary valvular and/or 
subvalvular obstruction, which is particularly challenging. ART is an alternative treatment 
option in such cases. A non-ART approach involves creating a VSD to facilitate surgery. 
However, Jex et al.43) noted that these procedures are technically demanding. ART, which 
involves harvesting the aortic root followed by a Konno incision into the intact ventricular 
septum to enlarge the LVOT, can be performed to overcome these challenges. In our 
experience, we performed surgery on a patient with congenitally corrected TGA with an 
intact ventricular septum using a Konno incision to expand the LVOT.44) This patient has been 
under follow-up for 16 years with no evidence of LVOTO to date.
Rationale for considering aortic root translocation in anatomical repair of 
complex congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries
Several surgical options, including biventricular and single-ventricle repairs, are available 
for patients with complex congenitally corrected TGA. If a biventricular repair is selected,

https://doi.org/10.4070/kcj.2025.0164https://e-kcj.org
[[p12]]
the decision lies between conventional and anatomical repairs. When opting for anatomical 
repair, ART combined with an atrial switch should be considered for 2 key reasons.
The first reason pertains to abnormal cardiac positioning and the resulting spatial 
constraints for RVOT reconstruction in non-ART repairs. Unlike complete TGA, congenitally 
corrected TGA was more frequently associated with mesocardia and dextrocardia (Figure 3). 
A recent fetal echocardiography study45) reported that one-third of fetuses with congenitally 
corrected TGA exhibited abnormal cardiac positions. In these cases, the anteriorly located RV 
limited the space available for RV-to-PA reconstruction in non-ART repair. Performing ART 
in such cases can effectively address the issue of limited space limitations. In our experience, 
among the 8 patients with congenitally corrected TGA, 6 had either dextrocardia (n=4) or 
mesocardia (n=2), whereas the remaining 2 had levocardia.
The second reason is related to the characteristic VSD associated with AV discordance. Unlike 
complete TGA, congenitally corrected TGA exhibits AV discordance, which significantly 
affects the AV junction as well as the morphology and position of VSDs.46) In congenitally 
corrected TGA, the PV is wedged between the atrial septum and the right mitral valve. 
This results in the atrial septum being displaced from the ventricular septum, leading to 
a misalignment between the two. Anderson et al.46) described how malalignment altered 
the arrangement of the AV conduction axis, whereas Hosseinpour et al.47) highlighted 
the abnormal positioning of the conduction system in complex congenitally corrected 
TGA. Additionally, the conduction system in hearts with situs solitus (SLL) and situs 
inversus lacked a consistent anatomical position. Although it is generally anterior to 
the typical SLL hearts, Shin’oka et al.48) reported that 5 of 48 patients exhibited a dominant 
posterior conduction system. This variability increases the risk of a heart block during 
VSD enlargement and closure. Most patients with congenitally corrected TGA have 
perimembranous VSDs located beneath the PV , with fibrous continuity between the PV

Addressing aortic regurgitation in aortic root translocation procedures
The most significant long-term concern after ART is the development of AR.21)32)37) Unlike 
non-ART procedures, ART involves the posterior translocation of the aortic root, which may 
contribute to a higher risk of AR. Reported incidence reaches up to 25%, and the risk appears 
to increase over time.23)50) Preventing AR depends largely on preserving the integrity of aortic 
root geometry. Even subtle distortions during root reconstruction can lead to progressive 
aortic valve dysfunction. These findings underscore the importance of meticulous surgical 
technique and anatomic precision in performing ART, and further highlight the need for 
careful patient selection, refined surgical expertise, and long-term echocardiographic 
surveillance to mitigate the risk of AR and ensure favorable outcomes. In light of these risks, 
recent studies have selectively applied the Rastelli-type procedure and ART in patients with 
complex TGA, comparing their respective outcomes and proposing anatomical criteria more 
appropriate for ART.28)51) In our practice, ART has consistently been the preferred strategy for 
complex TGA provided that adequate subpulmonary space is present. In contrast, 3 patients 
since 2005 underwent the Rastelli procedure due to a complete absence of subpulmonary 
space, rendering ART technically unfeasible and unlikely to provide benefit.
In our experience with ART, AR became a problem in 3 patients (21%). One case involved 
a patient with complete TGA who underwent ART at the age of 2 years following 2 BT shunts. 
Eleven years after ART, the patient underwent RV-to-PA conduit replacement and aortic valve 
repair. Intraoperative findings revealed severe AR due to deformity of the aortic valve leaflet at 
the anterior commissure where the VSD patch was attached. Bicuspidization of the aortic valve 
was performed by suturing the paracommissural leaflets with severe deformities. The patient 
was followed up for 6 years post-repair without recurrence of aortic stenoinsufficiency. Two 
other patients, one with complete TGA (operated on in 2009) and the other with congenitally 
corrected TGA (operated on in 2016), are currently being closely followed up for moderate AR.
Since the 2016 congenitally corrected TGA case, we have implemented surgical modifications 
in 6 patients—one with complete TGA and 5 with congenitally corrected TGA—to preserve 
aortic root integrity. This approach addresses the challenges of reconstructing the aortic root 
as a freestanding graft similar to those encountered during the Ross procedure. Securing 
the aortic root to the LVOT without distortion is particularly critical because even minor 
geometric distortions can lead to significant long-term complications. These challenges 
cannot be resolved solely by adjusting the VSD patch size or ensuring proper alignment of 
the aortic sinuses with the LVOT.34)38)
To fundamentally overcome these issues, we propose the “aortic root reconstruction-first 
(ARRF) technique,” which involves securely attaching a fully reconstructed aortic root to

the ascending aorta while ensuring proper aortic root and valve geometry during LVOT 
reconstruction ( Figure 4). In the Ross procedure, this technique entails first attaching 
the pulmonary autograft directly to the ascending aorta, then filling the aortic root with 
blood, and anastomosing it to the LVOT while directly assessing the autograft geometry and 
valve competency. In ART, this approach involves harvesting the coronary arteries, patching 
the coronary button defects, and connecting the reconstructed aortic root to the ascending 
aorta before proceeding with LVOT reconstruction. This method underscores the critical 
role of sutures between the aortic root and LVOT in influencing the aortic root geometry and 
valve function.
The ARRF technique enhances a surgeon’s technical competency, which is crucial for lengthy 
ART procedures, especially in complex cases such as congenitally corrected TGA. However, 
optimizing the ARRF technique for ART necessitates efforts to minimize the operative time 
associated with handling the coronary artery buttons. Learning from the original Nikaidoh 
procedure and its modifications may help to identify potential solutions. For instance, 
in patients with complete TGA, leaving the left coronary artery in situ, harvesting only 
the RCA button, mobilizing the aortic root, and repairing the RCA defect with a patch before 
implementing the ARRF technique could significantly reduce CPB time and simplify ART. 
All 6 patients who underwent this modified technique had well-functioning aortic valves. 
However, because the median follow-up period was 31 months (range, 5–44 months), a long-
term follow-up was necessary.

Expanding the indications of aortic root translocation for complex congenital 
heart defects
Patients with complex CHDs, particularly those with intricate intracardiac anatomy and 
complex ventricle–great artery relationships, often face considerable challenges in achieving 
biventricular repair through conventional surgical approaches.52) Traditional methods, 
including transatrial access, standard right ventriculotomy, and approaches via the great 
arteries, can be technically demanding. The major CHDs under this category include 
complex TGA, intricate double-outlet RV , criss-cross heart, and double-inlet LV . Notably, 
ART has been successfully applied to achieve biventricular repair in complex TGA cases 
with complete AV septal defect in heterotaxy syndrome and TGA or double-outlet RV with 
remote VSD.53-56) As experience with ART continues to grow, it is expected to facilitate safer 
and more competent biventricular repair for several complex CHDs previously managed with 
univentricular strategies.
Conclusion
The optimal surgical approach for complex TGA remains unclear. However, with growing 
experience in ART, it has become evident that despite its technical challenges, ART provides 
more natural ventricular outflow tracts and demonstrates excellent long-term outcomes, 
particularly for both the RVOT and LVOT. Consequently, the threshold for ART adoption 
appears to be lower. Although further refinement is necessary to enhance the safety and 
long-term outcomes, these challenges are surmountable. If successfully refined, ART has 
the potential to pave the way for safer and more reliable biventricular repair in a broader 
spectrum of complex CHDs.
