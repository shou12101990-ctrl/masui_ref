with Williams Syndrome Based on the Risk Stratification

Original Article
Williams syndrome (WS) is a congenital and multisystem 
genetic disorder due to a recurrent microdeletion on 
chromosome 7q11.23. It is an autosomal dominant disorder 
attributed to an elastin coding gene mutation. The deletions 
in the chromosome are responsible for the hemizygosity of  
the elastin gene. This genetic aberration is the basis for the 
disease manifestations.[1] This leads to elastin arteriopathy, 
resulting in widespread vascular abnormalities and other 
connective tissue impairment. Most notable is supravalvular 
aortic stenosis (SVAS) and present in up to 75% of 
cases.[2] Peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis (PPAS) is also 
reported in these patients, but it usually improves as age 
progresses and severity resolves by itself. [2] Biventricular 
hypertrophy is noted in patients with combined SVAS and 
PPAS, risking patients to myocardial ischemia, arrhythmias, 
and sudden arrest. [2,3] Coronary artery abnormalities are 
found in 10%–30% of  cases, with stenosis occurring

ABSTRACT
Introduction: Williams syndrome (WS) is associated with morbidity and mortality during surgical and nonsurgical procedures. The primary 
objective of the study was to assess the incidence of perioperative mortality in patients with WS among the risk groups. The secondary objectives 
were to analyze the mortality cause and study the various perioperative outcomes among the WS risk groups.
Materials and Methods: In this retrospective observational study, we included patients with WS who underwent anesthesia for elective 
corrective surgical repair and nonoperative procedures like radiological imaging and cardiac catheterization procedures in our institute from January 
2009 to April 2024. The patients were stratified into high‑, medium‑, and low‑risk groups based on the risk factors recorded from the hospital 
medical records. We analyzed the incidence of perioperative mortality and associated outcomes among the three risk groups of patients with WS.
Results: Medical records of 30 patients with WS were studied. The incidence of mortality in the high‑risk group was 11.8%, whereas there 
was no mortality in the other risk groups. The mortality occurred in high‑risk group patients who underwent sedation with inhalational anesthesia 
for a radiological imaging procedure. There was no significant difference in the duration of mechanical ventilation, vasoactive inotropic score, 
or length of intensive care unit and hospital stay among the three risk groups.
Conclusion: Mortalities were observed in the high‑risk group who underwent sedation with inhalational anesthesia for radiological imaging. 
Preoperative risk stratification is needed to identify the risk category and alleviate the major perioperative cardiac events in patients with WS.
Keywords: Congenital heart defect, risk stratification, sudden cardiac death, supravalvar aortic stenosis, Williams 
syndrome

The primary objective of  the study was to examine the 
incidence of  perioperative mortality in patients with WS 
undergoing various surgical and nonsurgical procedures 
under sedation or general anesthesia (GA) based on the risk 
stratification groups. The secondary objectives were: (1) to 
compare the incidence of  perioperative mortality between (a) 
GA and sedation, (b) intravenous and inhalational induction, 
and (c) operative and nonoperative procedures, and (2) to 
study the incidence of  malignant arrhythmias, duration of  
mechanical ventilation, vasoactive inotropic score, stroke, 
renal failure and, length of  intensive care unit (ICU) and, 
hospital stay among the three risk groups.
METHODS
Study design and patient population
The Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) approved 
the study (SCT/IEC/2063/MAY/2023), and the waiver 
requirement for written informed consent was obtained 
from the IEC, as this was a retrospective observational 
study conducted from our hospital medical records. We 
studied the past medical records of  patients with WS 
who underwent anesthesia for various operative and 
nonoperative procedures in our tertiary care hospital 
from January 2009 to April 2024. Patients’ medical 
records were studied for preoperative, intraoperative, 
and postoperative parameters, and the patients were 
risk‑stratified into low‑, medium‑, and high‑risk groups. 
The type of  anesthesia technique  (sedation or GA), 
mode of  induction (inhalational or intravenous), and 
the type of  procedure (operative or nonoperative) were 
noted among the risk groups. Preoperative transthoracic 
echocardiography details were evaluated for ventricular 
hypertrophy and function, aortic and pulmonary artery 
abnormalities, and coronary artery anomalies. The incidence 
of  mortality, cardiac arrest, malignant arrhythmias, duration 
of  mechanical ventilation, vasoactive inotropic score, ICU 
stay, and hospital stay were noted among the three risk 
groups. Patients with incomplete medical records were 
excluded from the study. This manuscript adheres to the 
Strengthening the Reporting of  Observational Studies in 
Epidemiology guidelines.

RESULTS
A total of  38 patients with WS had undergone anesthesia 
for operative and nonoperative procedures in our 
hospital during January 2009 to April 2024. Eight patients 
were excluded due to incomplete medical records, and 
30 patients were included in our study. The demographic 
data, preoperative clinical data, and preoperative 
echocardiographic data are given in Table 2 . The risk 
categories of  the study groups are shown in Figure 1 . 
The procedure details, technique of  anesthesia, mode 
of  anesthetic induction, and the need for vasopressors 
before induction are given in Table 3. The incidence of  
perioperative mortality was 11.8% in the high‑risk category, 
whereas there were no mortalities in the medium and the 
low‑risk groups. The two mortalities in high‑risk group 
occurred in patients who had inhalational induction for the 
computed tomography imaging. There was no significant 
difference in the incidence of  mortality among the types 
of  anesthesia technique, mode of  anesthesia induction, 
and kind of  procedure undergone [Table 4]. The incidence 
of  malignant arrhythmias was 41.2% (7 patients) in the 
high‑risk group and 18.2% (2 patients) in the moderate‑risk 
group. There were no incidences of  arrhythmias in the 
low‑risk group. In the high‑risk group, two patients had 
malignant arrhythmias during inhalational induction for 
computed tomography imaging and progressed to fatal 
cardiac arrest. The other five patients in the high‑risk 
group and two patients in the moderate‑risk group had 
malignant arrhythmias during corrective surgery and were 
cardioverted to sinus rhythm. Even though the duration 
of  mechanical ventilation, vasoactive inotropic score, 
and the length of  ICU and hospital stay were higher in 
the high‑risk group, the difference was not statistically 
significant [Table 5]. There was no incidence of  stroke or 
renal failure among the study cohort.
DISCUSSION
In our single‑centre, retrospective observational study, 
we observed that patients with WS of  high‑risk category, 
who had undergone sedation with inhalational induction 
for nonoperative procedures, had a greater risk of  major 
adverse cardiac events. Also, the occurrence of  malignant 
arrhythmia requiring intervention was higher in the 
high‑risk group. The cause of  sudden cardiac death in 
patients with WS was not completely understood. Still, we 
noticed that all the patients had supravalvar component 
of  aortic stenosis, out of  which 19 (63%) cases had SVAS 
mean gradient more than 40 mm Hg and left ventricular 
hypertrophy was found to be present in all patients. The 
high SVAS gradient and left ventricular hypertrophy might 
have caused left ventricular outflow tract obstruction due 
to a decrease in preload and afterload during the induction 
of  anesthesia. Similarly, in a study by Pham et al.,[7] patients 
with WS undergoing cardiac catheterization had a higher 
risk of  mortality, which was attributed to bilateral outflow 
tract hypertrophy and obstruction.

occurrence in WS patients with an incidence of  40%. In

WS patients with PPAS, there is a reduction in the elastin 
component of  the pulmonary vessels as age progresses, 
and there will be a reduction in the symptoms and gradient 
across the stenotic segment in late childhood.[8] However, 
we observed that our study cohorts had symptomatic 
PPAS (40%) and isolated supravalvar pulmonary 
stenosis (16%). There was coronary ostial narrowing in 
two patients (6.6%) of  the high‑risk category who had 
cardiac arrest during an aesthesia induction, which could 
have contributed to the mortality. [8] Studies had shown 
that there is about an 11% risk of  adverse cardiac events 
while providing anesthesia for WS patients. [9] Anesthesia 
induction is the critical phase for the perianesthetic cardiac 
arrest in WS patients.[9]
The patients with WS have anxiety disorders and 
neurodevelopmental issues, which make them uncooperative 
for anesthesia induction. [1] This leads to anesthesia 
induction by high concentrations of  inhalational agents to 
secure the intravenous access. In our study, we observed 
that the incidence of  cardiac arrest occurred during the 
inhalational induction due to the myocardial depressant 
effect of  high concentrations of  volatile anesthetics and 
a fall in coronary perfusion pressure. [10] The reduced 
poststenotic aortic pressures during anesthetic induction 
reduce the diastolic flow in the coronaries, leading to 
myocardial ischemia. Also, the reduced coronary perfusion 
coupled with left ventricular hypertrophy aggravates the 
microvascular coronary ischemia, leading to refractory 
cardiac arrest.[11] The high prestenotic aortic pressure in 
WS leads to coronary artery dysplasia, resulting in coronary 
artery musculature hypertrophy and luminal narrowing.[12] 
Therefore, WS patients of  the high‑risk category should 
be monitored with extreme caution during inhalational 
induction. A multimodal approach must be utilized using 
nonpharmacological measures and oral anxiolytics to calm 
the child before an aesthesia induction. Preprocedural 
anxiety can be triggered in the WS patients by noise, needle 
injection, medical equipment, and the operation theatre 
environment, which makes them extremely aggressive 
during anesthesia induction. Also, the sympathetic 
activation and tachycardia can increase the myocardial 
oxygen supply‑demand mismatch, leading to myocardial 
ischemia.[13] In our study, inhalational induction was done in 
patients undergoing nonoperative procedures. In contrast, 
the patients undergoing operative surgical procedures 
had intravenous induction through the intravenous 
cannula, which was secured in the hospital ward on the 
morning of  surgery. Studies had recommended using 
intravenous induction with boluses of  vasoactive agents 
like phenylephrine to counteract the hypotension episodes 
during anesthesia induction in patients with WS.[6,14]

Conduct of  anesthesia is a critical transition and is subject 
to the occurrence of  adverse cardiac events in patients 
with WS. A preoperative understanding and stratification 
of  risk factors can help healthcare providers to counsel the 
patient or the child’s parents regarding the perioperative 
anesthetic concerns involved in the procedure. Risk 
assessment is a helpful tool based on the available medical 
evidence, which is used to identify the preoperative risk 
factors and assess the procedure’s outcomes. In this study, 
we attempted to retrospectively risk‑stratify the patients 
with WS from the data of  hospital medical records (based 
on the risk stratification criteria by Matisoff  et al.[6]). We 
studied the factors contributing to perioperative mortality 
and poor postoperative outcomes. In our study, most 
patients in the low‑risk and moderate‑risk groups were 
planned for radiological imaging, whereas corrective 
surgeries were higher among high‑risk patients. The 
incidence of  arrhythmias was 47.2% in patients of  the 
high‑risk group, among whom two patients had fatal 
cardiac arrest. The incidence of  arrhythmias was due to 
pre‑existing QTc prolongation and associated ischemic 
changes in the preoperative electrocardiograph. Studies 
have recommended electrocardiograph screening, 
arrhythmia evaluation, and intermittent rhythm monitoring 
in WS patients in the high‑risk category during the entire 
perioperative period.[15] Avoiding drugs that prolong QTc 
during the perioperative period may reduce incidence of  
malignant arrhythmias in high‑risk groups.
The incidence of  mortality observed in our study 
was 6.6% comprising patients who were planned for 
presurgical radiological workup. These observations 
were more in comparison to the study by Zinyandu 
et al.,[16] where the observed mortality was 2.5%. The two 
deceased patients had coronary ostial stenosis and QTc 
prolongation in preoperative electrocardiograph, which 
was considered to be a high‑risk factor for mortality. [17] 
Both mortalities occurred in the nonoperating room area, 
highlighting the importance of  nonoperating room 
cardiac anesthesia (NORCA) care in the radiology suite or 
cardiac catheterization laboratory. Corrective surgery was 
performed in 30% of  the study cohorts. The postoperative 
requirement of  vasoactive medications and duration of  
mechanical ventilatory support were higher in high‑risk 
patients who underwent surgical correction. All the patients 
who underwent corrective surgery had an uneventful 
recovery, and there was no need for mechanical circulatory 
support in the postoperative period.
We acknowledge that our study had a few limitations. 
First, being a retrospective and single‑centre study, there 
were limitations in the available data, diagnostic tests, 
and incompleteness of  specific parameters that could 
be used for evaluation. Second, the risk stratification 
model used in the study was not validated across different 
ethnic study populations. Third, although our hospital is a 
high‑volume cardiac surgery centre, our study’s sample size 
was small. This was due to the low prevalence of  WS in 
the population. Fourth, long‑term outcomes after hospital 
discharge were not assessed. However, our study is the 
first to evaluate the WS patients’ perioperative outcomes 
based on the risk stratification criteria. Future prospective 
multicentric studies were needed to develop and validate 
the risk prediction models for the perioperative outcome 
in patients with WS.
CONCLUSION
In conclusion, WS patients of  the high‑risk category who 
were planned for sedation with inhalational induction 
for nonoperative procedures had a higher incidence of  
perioperative major adverse cardiac events. Since early 
identification of  high‑risk patients is crucial and anesthesia 
induction is a big challenge, intravenous induction 
is preferred over inhalational for better control of 
hemodynamics and to resuscitate the patients during major 
adverse cardiac events. A special emphasis should be placed 
on whether exposing the patients to NORCA procedures 
may be beneficial in changing the disease outcome. A risk 
stratification model and perioperative guidelines are the need 
of  the hour to prepare, plan, and mitigate the perioperative 
major adverse cardiac events in patients with WS.
