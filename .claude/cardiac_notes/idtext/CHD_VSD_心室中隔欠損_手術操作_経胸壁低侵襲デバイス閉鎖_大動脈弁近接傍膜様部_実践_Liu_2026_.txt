Background: T ransthoracic minimally invasive device closure (TDC) via the transthoracic approach can 
be used to treat ventricular septal defect (VSD). However, studies investigating transthoracic closure in 
pediatric patients with perimembranous ventricular septal defects (PmVSDs) located close to the aortic valve 
and without preoperative aortic regurgitation (AR) are still lacking. The aim of this study was to compare the 
efficacy and safety of TDC and conventional surgical repair (CSR) for PmVSDs measuring ≤2 mm from the 
aortic valve in pediatric patients without preexisting AR.
Methods:  A retrospective cohort study involving 119 pediatric patients treated between March 2017 
and April 2024, including 51 who underwent TDC and 68 who received CSR, was conducted. Baseline 
characteristics were balanced using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Primary outcomes 
included operation time, duration of ventilator support, chest tube drainage volume, postoperative hospital 
length of stay, and complications at 6 months postprocedure.
Results: The TDC group had significantly shorter operating times (70.95 vs. 146.59 minutes), shorter 
ventilation duration (97.12 vs. 219.98 minutes), less blood loss (30.54 vs. 115.15 mL), and shorter postoperative 
hospital stays (4.45 vs. 6.13 days; all P<0.001). The residual shunt (0% vs. 2.9%) and atrioventricular block (AVB) 
(0% vs. 5.9%) rates were lower in the TDC group, whereas new-onset mild valvular regurgitation occurred in 
3.9% of TDC patients compared with 0% in the CSR group. Overall morbidity was 3.9% in the TDC group 
vs. 8.8% in the CSR group (P=0.46). No mortality or late reinterventions were observed during follow-up.
Conclusions: T ransthoracic device closure under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance is a 
safe and effective alternative to CSR for selected cases of PmVSDs adjacent to the aortic valve, resulting in 
faster recovery and favorable short-term outcomes.

Background
VSDs are common congenital heart defects; approximately 
160,000 new cases are reported in China every year, and the 
prevalence of VSD is estimated to be 20% (5).
Rationale and knowledge gap
Minimally invasive transthoracic device closure performed 
under echocardiographic guidance is a method used to 
treat VSDs (1). Approximately 1.5% to 10.6% of patients 
with PmVSDs have AVP or AR (2). The proximity of the 
PmVSD to the aortic valve increases the likelihood of AVP/
AR, and AR develops or worsens more frequently after 
surgery in patients with PmVSDs with AVP and AR than in 
those without AVP (3). The Guidelines for Interventional 
T reatment of Common Congenital Heart Disease in 
Children [2025] note that a VSD combined with mild to 
moderate AVP , in the absence of moderate or severe aortic 
valve regurgitation, constitutes a relative indication for 
interventional procedures (4).
Objective
In this study, we aimed to compare the efficacy and safety 
of TDC vs. CSR for PmVSDs measuring ≤2 mm from the 
aortic valve in pediatric patients without preexisting AR. 
We present this article in accordance with the STROBE 
reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/
article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2511/rc).
Methods
Clinical data
The clinical data of 803 patients with simple PmVSDs 
admitted to Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital between 
March 2017 and April 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. 
One hundred nineteen patients with PmVSDs within 2 
mm of the aortic valve underwent surgical treatment. The 
study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of 
Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was 
approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Provincial 
Children’s Hospital (No. EYLL-2025-084). Informed 
consent was waived in this retrospective study.
The inclusion criteria were patients aged 1 to 11 years 
with a confirmed diagnosis of constricted VSD on the basis 
of preoperative chest X-ray, 12-lead electrocardiogram 
(ECG), and transthoracic echocardiography. T ransthoracic 
echocardiography confirmed the VSD as a PmVSD, with 
the defect located less than 2 mm from the upper margin 
of the aortic valve. No other malformations that required 
concurrent treatment were present, and no aortic valve 
regurgitation was observed (Figure 1A).

Key findings 
• T ransthoracic minimally invasive device closure (TDC) for 
pediatric perimembranous ventricular septal defects (PmVSDs)  
≤2 mm from the aortic valve [no preexisting aortic regurgitation 
(AR)] is safer and more effective than conventional surgical repair 
(CSR): shorter operation time (68 vs. 147 min), shorter ventilation 
duration (1.6 vs. 4.1 hours), less blood loss, shorter hospital stay (4.4 
vs. 6.2 days), lower residual shunt (0% vs. 2.9%) and atrioventricular 
block (0% vs. 5.9%), similar overall morbidity (3.9% vs. 8.8%), and 
no mortality or late reinterventions. TDC was associated with 3.9% 
new mild valvular regurgitation (CSR: 0%).  
What is known and what is new? 
• Ventricular septal defects are common congenital heart defects; 
PmVSDs near the aortic valve increase aortic valve prolapse/AR 
risk; CSR is a traditional therapy; and TDC is a minimally invasive 
option.
• TDC under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) guidance is 
a safe alternative for selected pediatric PmVSDs ( ≤2 mm from the 
aortic valve, no AR), with faster recovery and favorable short-term 
outcomes.
What are the implications, and what should change now?
• TDC should be prioritized for eligible pediatric patients. 
Clinicians may adopt TEE-guided TDC to improve recovery. 
Future studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up 
are needed to confirm the durability of these findings. Guidelines 
should be updated to include TDC as a preferred option for such 
cases.

TDC group
In the TDC group, transesophageal echocardiography 
(TEE) (Philips 5C ultrasonic diagnostic equipment, 
Washington State, USA; the probe was a s7-3t esophageal 
probe with a frequency of 3–7 MHz) was performed before 
the operation. This step was repeated to further clarify the 
intracardiac structure and determine the size of the defect, 
the direction of the shunt bundle blood flow, the size and 
number of the diverging mouth, the distance between the 
upper margin of the defect and the aortic valve, and the 
presence of prolapse or regurgitation of the aortic valve. If 
the TEE results suggested that TDC was contraindicated 
(e.g., for moderate-to-severe AR or nonperimembranous 
VSDs), CSR of the VSD was performed. T wo paths were 
chosen according to the direction of the VSD shunt beam 
flow. If the shunt flow through the VSD was directed away 
from the apex of the heart, a 2–3 cm parasternal incision 
was made between the 4th and 5th costal spaces on the 
right side of the sternum ( Figure 1B), the pericardium was 
incised, puncture was performed through the right atrium 
near the atrioventricular groove, and a purse-string suture 
was placed. Using an “S”-shaped metal catheter with 
a hollow (lumen-containing) structure ( Figure 1C ), the 
catheter tip was introduced into the left ventricle under 
TEE guidance through the right atrium, tricuspid valve, 
right ventricle, and VSD ( Figure 1D). The guide wire was 
placed through the metal catheter, the metal catheter was 
replaced with the occluder delivery sheath tube, and the 
occlusive device was placed at the defect site of the VSD 
(Figure 1E,1F ). The occlusive device was released after 
confirming that any residual shunt, new valve reflux or 
intracardiac conduction block was absent. A drainage tube 
was not placed postoperatively. If the shunt flow through 
the VSD was toward the apex of the heart, a surgical skin 
incision was created in the lower part of the sternum. A 3–  
4 cm incision was made on the skin of the lower sternum, 
and the puncture point was selected on the surface of the 
right ventricle. The occluder was implanted and released via 
the right ventricular surface using the VSD route, and no 
residual shunt, new valve regurgitant or conduction block 
was detected. A pericardial drainage tube was inserted after 
surgery (Figure 1G). A total of 14 children underwent device 
closure through a lower sternal incision.
On the basis of the VSD diameter measured via TEE, 
a symmetrical or eccentric occluder was selected by 
adding 2 mm. A symmetrical occluder (VSD Occlude, 
Shanghai Shape Memory Alloy Co., Ltd.,  Shanghai, 
China) was preferred in cases without AVP; this occluder 
is made of nickel-titanium memory alloy wire woven with 
a symmetrical double disc sheet structure. An eccentric 
occluder was preferred in patients with AVP if a new 
AR occurred after the implantation of the occluder  
(Figure 1H,1I ). This occluder is made of nickel-titanium 
memory alloy wire woven, with a double-disc sheet 
structure, a single-sided design of the left ventricular 
surface, zero upper edge to leave space for the aortic valve, 
and a lower edge is inlaid with metal raised marks (“Mark” 
marks). The occluder delivery system was equipped with a 
guide wire, push rod, and sheath tube. Surgical repair of the 
VSD was performed if the AR did not improve.
After the operation, patients in both groups were transferred 
to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) while still intubated. 
After their condition stabilized, they were transferred from 
the CICU to the general ward for follow-up treatment. The 
surgery was successful for all the patients in both groups.
Six months after the surgery, the patient visited 
our hospital’s outpatient department for a follow-up 
echocardiogram and ECG.
Perioperative observation indexes
The perioperative observation indexes included the 
following:
(I) Recording the operation time, ventilator support 
duration, CICU stay, chest tube drainage volume, 
and postoperative hospital length of stay of both 
groups;
( I I ) Recording the incidence of in-hospital  
postoperative complications (arrhythmia, new-
onset valve regurgitation, infection, residual shunt, 
and unplanned reoperation);
(III) Recording the transthoracic echocardiography 
findings of both groups in a quiet state before 
surgery and at 6 months postoperatively. 
Residual shunt: postoperative transthoracic  
echocardiography revealed a left-to-right shunt at the 
ventricular septal level. Arrhythmia: atrioventricular block 
(AVB), bundle branch block.

linear regression for continuous outcomes, with the survey

required reoperation because of excessive drainage fluid

but echocardiography revealed that they had resolved by  
6 months postsurgery. Four patients in the CSR group had 
new complete right bundle branch blocks, whereas no new 
ECG abnormalities were found in the TDC group. 
After regression analysis with the IPTW treatment, the 
following results were observed:
(I) Postoperative shunt and postoperative AVB were 
not observed in any patients in the device closure 
group, whereas numerous cases occurred in the 
CSR group. These results suggest that device 
closure may serve as a strong protective factor 
against these complications.
(II) New-onset AR, however, was absent in the CSR 
group but was observed in two patients in the 
device closure group. These findings highlight a 
potential safety signal and underscore the need to 
further evaluate the possible relationship between 
device closure and new-onset AR.
During the procedure, substantial sheath retraction 
may entangle the surrounding tricuspid chordae tendineae 
with the guide wire on rotation, causing chordal injury and 
varying valvular regurgitation. For eccentric occluders, 
intraoperative TEE must accurately visualize the long 
axis of the ventricular septum at the largest cross-section 
of the defect. This criterion ensures that the occluder’s 
marker faces the cardiac apex and that the prolapsed aortic 
valve leaflet lies as much as possible on the occluder’s left 
ventricular surface to prevent abrasion.
Challenges and technical considerations.
Although minimally invasive VSD device closure achieves 
reliable results in children with AVP , challenges still  
exist (6). VSDs combined with noncoronary valve prolapse 
may be risk factors for AR (7). It was previously believed 
that the treatment of PmVSDs with AVP device closure 
was difficult, and surgical repair was considered the best 
treatment.
Owing to gradual developments in TDC of VSDs under 
the guidance of TEE, minimally invasive TDC of PmVSDs 
within 2 mm of the aortic valve has been performed 
at our hospital. TDC under the guidance of TEE is 
suitable for all types of VSDs, including those classified 
as perimembranous, and it is not associated with strict 
peripheral vascular requirements. It is suitable for young 
children with low body weights, is associated with easier 
establishment of tracks, and is convenient to perform (8).
This study was a retrospective analysis. Surgical repair 
has long been the main treatment for such VSDs. As 
surgeons gain experience with TDC, attempts have been 
made to close VSDs with smaller edge distances, and good 
therapeutic results have been achieved.

defect may become tangled on the cable when the latter 
is rotated, resulting in damage to the chordae tendineae 
and varying degrees of regurgitation (9). When the VSD is 
close to the aortic valve, an ADO-II occluder or eccentric

aortic valve leaflet is placed on the left ventricular surface

aortic valve leaflet. Before the device is released, TEE is 
necessary to repeatedly observe the defect and aortic valve 
and identify any residual shunt at the ventricular level 
and new-onset AR. In the absence of obvious aortic valve 
regurgitation before the operation, the small occluder may 
need to be replaced after initial placement. If significant 
reflux persists, closure treatment should be abandoned, and 
surgical repair should be performed. In a study of the TDC 
of subarterial VSDs, no cases of AR were reported at the 
3- or 5-year follow-up (12). A residual shunt is a common 
complication after treatment for PmVSDs. After surgical 
repair, residual shunts are prone to occur at the upper and 
lower edges of the patch, which are not easily exposed. The 
occurrence of residual shunts after interventional closure is 
mostly related to an occluder that is either too small or too 
large, and the morphology of the VSD is also related to the 
formation of a shunt (13).
Four patients in the CSR group in this study had new 
complete right bundle branch blocks, whereas no new ECG 
abnormalities were found in the TDC group. Surgical 
treatment involves the use of a patch to repair the defect. If 
an inappropriate suture method, traction device, or clamp is 
used to repair a right VSD, the risk of a right bundle branch 
block is greater than that of a left bundle branch block (14). 
Although new-onset arrhythmia did not occur in the TDC 
group in this study, some studies revealed that young age, 
low weight, proximity of the defect to the atrioventricular 
valve and aortic valve, presence of membranous aneurysms, 
occluder type, and intraoperative technique are key factors 
influencing the occurrence of arrhythmia after closure (15). 
In this study, compared with percutaneous defect closure, 
TDC features a shorter surgical path, eliminates the need 
for guidewire exchange maneuvers inside the heart, and 
reduces injury to the tissues surrounding the VSD, thereby 
lowering the incidence of arrhythmias.
Recommendations for future studies and guidelines
With the continuous maturity of the technique of 
transthoracic closure and the wide promotion of its clinical 
application, the existing guidelines for the treatment of 
heart disease have gradually developed. These guidelines 
help doctors consider the specific situation of patients more 
comprehensively and scientifically for formulating more 
reasonable and effective treatment plans.
Compared with traditional surgery, minimally invasive 
surgical methods reduce patient pain and recovery time, 
enabling patients to return to normal life and work more 
quickly. This approach will also improve patient satisfaction 
and compliance with treatment, further promoting the 
improvement and development of guidelines for the 
treatment of heart disease. We hope to establish standard 
guidelines in the future.
Despite its advantages, transcatheter closure still has 
certain limitations. First, the procedure demands precise 
anatomical positioning and sizing of the defect. For 
instance, large VSDs or those with suboptimal anatomical 
locations may compromise the function of the aortic or 
atrioventricular valves following occluder placement, 
potentially causing valve regurgitation. Second, the 
surgical technique is highly complex and must be guided by 
advanced imaging modalities such as TEE. The occluder 
device adopted in this study is made of nickel-titanium 
alloy, which has the risk of metal ion leaching into the blood 
and may induce adverse reactions such as human allergies. 
If ceramic occluder devices or absorbable material occluder 
devices are used in the future, this problem is expected 
to be avoided. Even minor procedural errors can lead to 
occluder displacement, dislodgement, or residual shunting. 
Moreover, there remains a risk of complications, including 
conduction disturbances, arrhythmias, and thrombus 
formation.
Given the retrospective design of this study, unmeasured 
confounding could not be fully eliminated despite IPTW 
adjustment. Additionally, as a single-center investigation, 
the findings have limited generalizability, with observed 
outcomes potentially influenced by institutional expertise 
and local clinical practices—an effect that is particularly 
pronounced for technically complex procedures such as 
TDC. The small sample size further compromised the 
statistical power, whereas the short follow-up duration 
precluded definitive conclusions regarding long-term 
efficacy.
Conclusions
In the TDC group, all the children were discharged 
uneventfully, with no severe complications and no deaths.
