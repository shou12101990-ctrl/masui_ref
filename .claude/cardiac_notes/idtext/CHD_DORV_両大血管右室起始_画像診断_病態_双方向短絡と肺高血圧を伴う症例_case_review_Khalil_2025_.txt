a b s t r a c t 
Double Outlet Right Ventricle (DORV) is a rare congenital heart defect characterized by both 
the aorta and pulmonary artery originating from the right ventricle. It accounts for 1%-3% 
of all congenital heart defects and is often associated with additional structural anomalies 
such as ventricular septal defects (VSDs), which exacerbate hemodynamic complications. 
We present a case of a 16-year-old female with DORV and 2 VSDs, leading to bidirectional 
shunting and subsequent pulmonary hypertension. The patient presented with progres- 
sive exertional dyspnea, central cyanosis, and clubbing of the fingers, indicative of chronic 
hypoxia. Echocardiography and Doppler studies confirmed the diagnosis, revealing signifi- 
cant bidirectional shunting and moderate pulmonary hypertension. Two distinct VSDs were 
identified in the interventricular septum (IVS): the first, a larger perimembranous VSD in- 
volving the inlet part with extensions toward the subaortic region, measured 11 mm × 16 
mm; the second, a smaller VSD in the mid-part of the trabecular septum, measured 4 mm ×4 
mm. Both VSDs permitted bidirectional shunting, predominantly left-to-right, contributing 
to the patient’s cyanosis and pulmonary hypertension, with the larger VSD being routable 
to the aorta. Treatment involved stabilization with medical management to address pul- 
monary hypertension and preparation for corrective surgery. Timely surgical correction, in- 
cluding the closure of VSDs and rerouting of the ventriculo-arterial connections, is crucial 
for improving outcomes. Ongoing follow-up is essential to monitor for complications such 
as arrhythmias, right heart failure, and progression of pulmonary hypertension. The case 
underscores the complexity of DORV and the need for a multidisciplinary approach in its 
management.

Introduction 
Double outlet right ventricle (DORV) is a rare and complex con- 
genital heart defect in which both the aorta and pulmonary 
artery arise from the right ventricle, leading to significant 
hemodynamic alterations. This anomaly is often associated 
with other defects, notably the ventricular septal defect (VSD), 
pulmonary stenosis, and a variety of transposition and arch 
anomalies. While the incidence of DORV is low, accounting for 
approximately 1%-3% of all congenital heart defects, its clin- 
ical significance is profound, especially when compounded 
by additional structural abnormalities like VSDs, which can 
worsen symptoms by allowing abnormal shunting of blood be- 
tween the ventricles. Early diagnosis is critical to determining 
the most appropriate therapeutic interventions, including sur- 
gical correction, which remains the cornerstone of managing 
this congenital anomaly [ 1 ,2 ]. The presence of bidirectional 
shunting across the VSDs complicates the clinical picture fur- 
ther, contributing to poor oxygen saturation and cyanosis, 
often resulting in the need for urgent clinical management. 
In this context, a comprehensive diagnostic and therapeutic 
strategy is required to address both the structural defect and 
its hemodynamic consequences [ 3 ]. 
DORV presents a multifaceted challenge for clinicians due 
to the variability in its anatomic presentation and the asso- 
ciated risks of both systemic and pulmonary circulatory is- 
sues. Although early detection through imaging techniques 
such as echocardiography and Doppler studies has improved, 
the complex nature of DORV requires a multidisciplinary ap- 
proach to management, ranging from stabilization of the pa- 
tient’s condition to corrective surgery. Surgical intervention 
options include the Rastelli procedure, the arterial switch op- 
eration, and the Nikaidoh procedure, each of which addresses 
the distinct hemodynamic issues resulting from the abnormal 
ventriculo-arterial connections and VSDs [ 4 ,5 ]. However, the 
timing of surgery is crucial, as delayed interventions are of- 
ten associated with poorer outcomes due to the progressive 
nature of pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure. 
In the past few decades, advancements in diagnostic tools, 
particularly echocardiography and cardiac magnetic reso- 
nance imaging (MRI), have revolutionized the ability to diag- 
nose DORV and assess its severity. These imaging techniques 
provide a detailed view of the heart’s structural abnormalities, 
which are essential for both surgical planning and prognosti- 
cation. Doppler echocardiography, for instance, allows clini- 
cians to evaluate the degree of shunting across the VSD and 
assess the pressures within the pulmonary vasculature, which 
directly influence the management approach [ 6 ,7 ]. Moreover, 
advancements in the management of pulmonary hyperten- 
sion and heart failure have improved the overall prognosis 
for patients with complex congenital heart defects like DORV, 
although the risk of complications such as arrhythmias and 
right ventricular dysfunction remains a concern throughout 
the patient’s life. 
This case report presents a rare and interesting case of 
DORV with bidirectional shunting, associated with 2 VSDs in 
a 16-year-old female patient. The clinical features, diagnos- 
tic workup, and subsequent management are discussed, high- 
lighting the complex nature of the disease and the thera- 
peutic approach required for optimal outcomes. The patient’s 
clinical course is characterized by a progressive worsening 
of symptoms, including exertional dyspnea, central cyanosis, 
and signs of right heart failure. Diagnostic imaging, includ- 
ing echocardiography, Doppler studies, and electrocardiogram 
(ECG), provided critical insights into the structural and hemo- 
dynamic alterations in the patient’s heart, leading to the di- 
agnosis of DORV and associated pulmonary hypertension. 
The therapeutic approach involved stabilization with medical 
management and preparation for corrective surgical interven- 
tion. 
Case presentation 
The patient, a 16-year-old female, presented to the tertiary 
hospital center with a 2-month history of exertional dyspnea. 
This symptom had progressively worsened over the course 
of her illness, and it prompted further investigation, eventu- 
ally leading to the diagnosis of Double Outlet Right Ventri- 
cle (DORV) with 2 associated ventricular septal defects (VSDs). 
The patient was admitted for further management, includ- 
ing cardiac catheterization to evaluate the reversibility of the 
defect. Upon admission, her clinical condition was marked 
by central cyanosis, indicating reduced oxygen levels in the 
blood, and clubbing of the fingers, which is often associated 
with chronic hypoxia and congenital heart disease. Addition- 
ally, she exhibited tachycardia, with a pulse rate of 120 beats 
per minute. A thorough physical examination revealed the 
presence of a pan-systolic murmur, best heard over the left 
parasternal area, without any added heart sounds or signs of 
edema in the extremities, suggesting that her heart failure had 
not progressed to the point of severe fluid retention. 
Laboratory investigations were extensive and revealed sev- 
eral abnormal findings. A complete blood count showed 
that the patient had microcytic, hypochromic anemia with a 
hemoglobin level of 9.5 g/dl, which is significantly below the 
normal reference range for her age. This suggests the pres- 
ence of iron deficiency anemia, which is common in chil- 
dren and adolescents with chronic illness. Her white blood cell 
count was also low, at 3.68 × 103 / μl, indicative of leukopenia, 
which can result from either chronic illness or the body’s re- 
sponse to stress. Mild thrombocytopenia was also observed 
with a slightly reduced platelet count, suggestive of stress 
platelets. The hematology report indicated anisocytosis (vari- 
ation in red blood cell size), microcytosis (smaller than normal 
red blood cells), and hypochromia (pale red blood cells due to 
a lack of hemoglobin). Furthermore, a few tear-drop-shaped 
cells, macrocytes, and polychromatic cells were present, all of 
which are typical of iron deficiency anemia. This was further 
corroborated by the clinical findings of central cyanosis and 
clubbing, which are often associated with chronic low oxygen 
levels in the blood due to congenital heart defects. 
Biochemical analysis revealed that the patient’s serum glu- 
cose levels were within normal limits at 5.3 mmol/L, and her 
serum creatinine level was also normal at 0.59 mg/dl, indicat- 
ing adequate renal function. Electrolyte studies showed that 
sodium was 136 mmol/L, potassium was 4.5 mmol/L, and chlo- 
ride was 101 mmol/L, all of which were within normal ranges,

Discussion 
Double Outlet Right Ventricle (DORV) is a congenital cardiac 
anomaly in which both the aorta and pulmonary artery origi- 
nate from the right ventricle. It represents a rare and complex 
form of congenital heart disease, with an incidence of approx- 
imately 1%-3% of all congenital heart defects [ 6 ,7 ]. DORV can 
present with significant hemodynamic consequences, partic- 
ularly when it is associated with other defects, such as ventric- 
ular septal defects (VSDs). This discussion explores the mul- 
tifactorial nature of DORV, highlighting its diagnosis, clinical 
presentation, management strategies, and the challenges that 
arise from its treatment. 
Anatomy and variability of DORV 
DORV is a malformation characterized by an abnormality in 
the ventriculo-arterial connection, where the aorta and pul- 
monary artery both arise from the right ventricle. This con- 
dition can occur in various forms, depending on the align- 
ment of the outflow tracts and the presence of associated 
defects such as VSDs, pulmonary stenosis, or transposition 
of the great arteries. The anatomic variety includes several 
subtypes, including those with the aorta originating directly 
from the right ventricle and others with an arterial switch or 
a double-outlet artery. These variations in anatomy influence 
the clinical presentation and the surgical approach [ 8 ,9 ]. 
The complexity of DORV often lies in its associated struc- 
tural defects. In our case, the patient presented with 2 VSDs, 
which contributed to a bidirectional shunt, exacerbating her 
cyanosis and worsening the oxygenation status. The clinical 
presentation of cyanosis, central and peripheral clubbing, and 
exertional dyspnea is frequently seen in patients with sig- 
nificant shunting through the VSD. The bidirectional shunt- 
ing observed in this case significantly complicates the clinical 
course by increasing the volume of blood in the right ventricle 
and contributing to pulmonary hypertension. 
Diagnosis of DORV 
The diagnosis of DORV requires both clinical and radiologi- 
cal evaluation, including echocardiography, Doppler studies, 
and electrocardiogram (ECG). The use of color Doppler and 2- 
dimensional echocardiography allows for the clear visualiza- 
tion of the anatomical defects and the assessment of hemo- 
dynamic consequences such as shunt direction, pulmonary 
pressures, and the condition of the heart valves [ 10 ]. 
In our case, the patient’s echocardiographic findings re- 
vealed 2 distinct VSDs in the interventricular septum (IVS): 
the first, a larger perimembranous VSD involving the inlet 
part with extensions toward the subaortic region, measured 
11 mm ×16 mm; the second, a smaller VSD in the mid-part of 
the trabecular septum, measured 4 mm x 4 mm. The presence 
of bidirectional shunting, as evidenced by Doppler imaging, is 
a significant factor in the pathophysiology of DORV, leading to 
a reduction in oxygen saturation and systemic hypoxia. The 
Doppler study in our case also showed elevated pulmonary 
arterial pressure (PAP) and mild tricuspid regurgitation (TR), 
both of which are common complications in patients with 
DORV, especially those with significant VSDs [ 11 ,12 ]. The right 
atrium and right ventricle dilation observed in the patient’s 
echocardiogram further supports the hemodynamic burden 
placed on the right heart as a result of the increased blood 
flow through the right ventricle. 
The electrocardiogram (ECG) of the patient revealed non- 
specific changes, including a short PR interval, right axis de- 
viation, and possible lateral infarction. These findings are of- 
ten seen in patients with congenital heart defects and may 
indicate underlying conduction disturbances or arrhythmias. 
While the ECG findings in DORV are not always pathog- 
nomonic, they help provide additional information in the con- 
text of a detailed diagnostic workup. 
Pulmonary hypertension in DORV 
Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a common and significant 
concern for patients with DORV, particularly when associated 
with VSDs. The bidirectional shunt observed in our patient 
contributed to increased blood flow to the pulmonary vascu- 
lature, leading to elevated pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP). 
Moderate pulmonary hypertension with a mean PAP of 28 
mmHg was observed in this patient, which is consistent with 
the hemodynamic findings reported in other studies of DORV 
patients with VSDs. 
The presence of PH significantly affects the prognosis of 
patients with DORV. If left untreated, PH can lead to pro- 
gressive deterioration in exercise capacity and eventually re- 
sult in life-threatening complications. Management of PH in 
DORV patients typically involves optimizing heart function

pulmonary hypertension and reduce the strain on the right 
ventricle. These medications aim to improve circulation and 
decrease the workload on the heart, which is crucial for stabi-

Surgical options and timing 
Surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment 
for patients with DORV and associated defects. The timing of 
surgery is critical, as early interventions are associated with 
better outcomes, while delayed surgeries are often compli- 
cated by irreversible damage to the right ventricle and pul- 
monary vasculature. Surgical interventions in DORV typically 
focus on correcting the abnormal ventriculo-arterial connec- 
tion and repairing the VSDs. The Rastelli procedure, arterial 
switch operation, and Nikaidoh procedure are commonly em- 
ployed techniques, each tailored to the specific anatomical 
features of the DORV defect [ 13 ,14 ]. 
While transthoracic echocardiography and Doppler ultra- 
sound remain the cornerstone for the initial diagnosis of Dou- 
ble Outlet Right Ventricle (DORV), providing critical anatom- 
ical and physiological insights such as the identification of 
ventricular septal defects (VSDs), assessment of shunting, and 
estimation of pulmonary pressures, they have limitations in 
delineating complex 3-dimensional relationships and extrac- 
ardiac structures. Cardiac CT and Cardiac MRI offer significant 
advantages in this regard, particularly for surgical planning in 
DORV cases. Cardiac CT provides high-resolution imaging of 
the coronary arteries, great vessels, and extracardiac anatomy, 
enabling precise visualization of the malposed great arterial 
relationship and any associated anomalies such as pulmonary 
artery dilation or arch abnormalities, as observed in our pa- 
tient. Cardiac MRI, on the other hand, excels in assessing ven- 
tricular volumes, myocardial function, and tissue characteri- 
zation, which are crucial for evaluating the extent of right ven- 
tricular hypertrophy and pressure overload. Additionally, MRI 
can quantify the degree of shunting and assess pulmonary 
vascular resistance (PVR) noninvasively, aiding in the deter- 
mination of surgical candidacy and the optimal timing of in- 
tervention. These advanced imaging modalities complement 
echocardiography by providing a comprehensive roadmap for 
surgical correction, such as the Rastelli procedure or arterial 
switch operation, ensuring that the approach is tailored to 
the patient’s specific anatomy and hemodynamic profile, ul- 
timately improving surgical outcomes and reducing perioper- 
ative risks [ 15 ,16 ]. 
In our patient, the management plan included preparation 
for corrective surgery, including cardiac catheterization to as- 
sess the reversibility of decisions regarding surgery. The goal 
of surgery is to re-establish normal blood flow patterns and 
alleviate the hemodynamic burden on the heart. The surgical 
correction involves rerouting the aorta and pulmonary arter- 
ies to the left and right ventricles, respectively, and closing the 
VSDs. However, the decision regarding the optimal surgical ap- 
proach must take into account the patient’s age, the severity 
of the defect, and the associated complications such as PH. 
Long-term prognosis and management 
The prognosis for patients with DORV depends on several fac- 
tors, including the timing of surgery, the presence of other 
associated defects, and the response to medical interven- 
tions. Long-term outcomes after corrective surgery have sig- 
nificantly improved with advances in surgical techniques, pe- 
rioperative care, and postoperative monitoring. However, pa- 
tients with DORV remain at risk for complications such as ar- 
rhythmias, pulmonary hypertension, and right heart failure, 
which require ongoing follow-up and management. 
For our patient, the comprehensive management approach 
involves regular follow-up with echocardiograms, ECGs, and 
laboratory tests to monitor for complications and ensure sta- 
ble heart function. The patient will require close monitoring 
for any signs of worsening right heart failure, arrhythmias, or 
progressive PH. The role of advanced imaging techniques like 
echocardiography, MRI, and Doppler studies will continue to 
be crucial in assessing long-term outcomes and guiding post- 
operative care. 
Conclusion 
This case of DORV with bidirectional shunting in a 16-year- 
old female highlights the complexity of managing congenital 
heart defects and underscores the importance of early inter- 
vention. The combination of clinical, radiological, and hemo- 
dynamic data provided a comprehensive understanding of the 
patient’s condition, leading to a tailored treatment approach. 
Although the patient remains at risk for complications, the 
prognosis can be significantly improved with timely surgi- 
cal intervention and ongoing medical management. The use 
of advanced imaging techniques and a multidisciplinary ap- 
proach is essential for optimizing outcomes in patients with 
complex congenital heart defects like DORV.
