Abstract
Background Advances in aortic coarctation (CoA) repairs now focus on reducing restenosis and hypertension. There 
is debate overusing median sternotomy, which may reduce re-coarctation, versus lateral thoracotomy, which avoids 
bypass; anatomical factors influence the method chosen. Re-coarctation rates vary (5–30%), impacted by arch size, 
low weight, and age. While balloon angioplasty is less invasive, it has high restenosis risk in neonates; high-pressure 
balloons may help. Evidence from prior studies is limited due to short follow-up, small cohorts, and retrospective 
design.
Aim To identify risk factors contributing to recoarctation following surgical repair in a cohort of one hundred patients 
with coarctation, and to examine the role of catheter-based interventions in the management of restenosis.
Methods This single center experience retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2017 to 
December 2023 and involved 100 patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) and CoA who underwent surgical 
treatment. The study included patients aged from 1 to 30 days at the time of surgery who exhibited significant post-
surgical stenosis, as confirmed by dynamic or echocardiographic findings. However, patients with hypoplastic left 
heart syndrome who had undergone Norwood surgery were excluded from the analysis.
Results Postoperative assessments demonstrated a substantial improvement in left ventricular function, with 84% 
of patients exhibiting good outcomes. The incidence of postoperative complications (with or without recoarctation) 
was elevated at 64%, predominantly due to cardiac-related issues. Notably, lower operative weight was identified as 
a significant predictor of surgical failure with recoarctation (gradient more than 20 mmHg with or without impaired 
LV function) (p = 0.002). During the follow-up period ranging from three months to 1.5 years, 16 patients (16%) 
underwent catheter intervention for management of recoarctation, there were no reported mortalities, although one 
patient required surgical reintervention.

Introduction
Early surgical correction of aortic coarctation, a con -
genital narrowing near the ductus arteriosus, was associ -
ated with risks like paraplegia, mesenteric ischemia, and 
death. With most complications resolved, the surgical 
approach must prevent re-coarctation and postoperative 
hypertension to be deemed successful [1, 2].
For a long time, the focus has been on finding the best 
surgical approach to minimize such complications, yet 
no consensus has been established. Some authors advo -
cate for median sternotomy based on less incidence of 
re-coarctation [ 3, 4], others prefer lateral thoracotomy 
to avoid the need for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) [ 5], 
while others devised new classification systems, based 
on anatomical variables, to properly assign patients to 
the surgical approach best suited to their condition [ 6]. 
However, the vast range in incidence of re-coarctation 
(5–30%) merits more nuanced exploration of other pos -
sible factors [2, 7].
Other factors have been identified as potential deter -
minants of re-coarctation, such as the size of the aortic 
arch, a weight < 3 kg at surgery, and age [ 8–10]. Balloon 
angioplasty is of significant utility and offers satisfactory 
results. However, studies have shown that in extremely 
young, such as neonates and very young infants, the risk 
of restenosis is high [ 11]. Nevertheless, BA was still rec -
ommended as it is effective and obviates the need for 
open surgery in this vulnerable population [ 12]. To miti-
gate the incidence of recoarctation, it has been suggested 
that a high-pressure low-profile balloon in infants with 
CoA improved outcomes, resulting in a lower gradient as 
well as reducing the rate of recoarctation [13].
Several studies evaluated the various surgical 
approaches in relation to the incidence of recoarctation; 
however, limitations persisted. Follow-up periods were 
not long enough to account for late recoarctation [ 14], 
unreliable sample sizes and large numbers of dropouts 
[15].
Methods
Study design, settings, and ethical considerations
The Ethics Committee of Madinah Cardiac Cen -
ter approved the study, and written informed consent 
was obtained from patients’ parents or guardians. This 
retrospective cross-sectional analysis, study was con -
ducted at the Department of Pediatric Cardiac Surgery 
and Cardiology, Madinah Cardiac Center MCC from 
January 2017 to December 2023, and involved 100 infants 
with congenital heart disease (CHD) who had surgical 
repair for coarctation of the aorta (CoA), was designed 
to investigate the risk factors linked to surgical failure 
in treating aortic recoarctation in infants and to evalu -
ate the effectiveness of catheter-based interventions for 
managing post-surgical stenosis.
Ethical considerations
Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional 
review board at the Madinah Cardiac Centre, ensuring 
compliance with the principles outlined in the Declara -
tion of Helsinki. Given the retrospective nature of the 
study, informed consent was waived, as all data were col -
lected anonymously from existing medical records with -
out direct patient contact.
Data collection
Data collection involved a thorough review of medical 
records, operative reports, echocardiographic findings, 
and catheterization data. Demographic variables such as 
birth weight, age at surgery, and gender were analyzed. 
Preoperative clinical data were recorded, including the 
presence of associated cardiac or extracardiac anomalies, 
the necessity for preoperative interventions, and the type 
of surgical procedure performed. The surgical techniques 
utilized were categorized into three main approaches: 
resection with end-to-end anastomosis, extended end-
to-end anastomosis, and complex arch reconstruction. 
Postoperative outcomes, such as left ventricular function, 
complications, and mortality, were carefully documented.
Inclusion criteria
Patient post-surgical coarctation repair.
Exclusion criteria
Infants with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) 
who underwent Norwood surgery were excluded due 
to their distinct hemodynamic profile and surgical 
approach, which could influence risk factor analysis and 
treatment outcomes.
Conclusion This study endorsed the significance of patient weight at the time of operation as a robust predictor of 
surgical failure and the need for postoperative catheterization in infants with recoarctation of the aorta. While cardiac 
and noncardiac anomalies also contribute to the likelihood of requiring reintervention, weight remains the most 
consistent and statistically significant factor. The role of refined endovascular modalities, such as balloon angioplasty, 
continues to be vital in managing recoarctation, particularly in low-weight infants. Overall, these insights contribute 
to a deeper understanding of the risk factors and management approaches for Recoarctation in infants, aiming to 
improve long-term outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Outcomes
*The primary endpoints of the study were the incidence 
of post-surgical stenosis, the need for catheter-based 
interventions, recoarctation is defined as persistent or 
recurrent stenosis (a Z-score of −2 or lower for the aorta 
is a significant finding with pressure gradient more than 
20 mm hg) requiring reintervention [2].
*Secondary outcomes included intraoperative and 
postoperative complications, which were classified into 
categories such as bleeding, cardiac complications, Spe -
cial focus was given to the relationship between low 
birth weight and restenosis, as well as the effectiveness of 
catheter-based interventions in alleviating post-surgical 
stenosis.
Common surgical techniques
There are more than 2 surgeons with different outcomes 
involved in this study.
  • Resection and End-to-End Anastomosis (EEA):
This involves removing the narrowed portion of the aorta 
and then surgically sewing the two healthy ends of the 
vessel together. For longer areas of narrowing or a hypo -
plastic aortic arch, an extended end-to-end anastomosis 
(EEEA) is often used, providing a larger connection and 
better long-term results.
  • Subclavian Flap Repair:
In this technique, the left subclavian artery is used to 
enlarge the coarctation segment.
Key Aspects of the Procedure.
  • Incision:
The surgery is often approached through a left thoracot -
omy, though other approaches exist.
  • Mobilization:
The surgeon must mobilize and disconnect the ductus 
arteriosus and carefully dissect the aorta and its branches 
to achieve a tension-free connection.
  • Anastomosis:
The two ends of the aorta are sewn together using 
sutures. In neonates and young children, the procedure is 
performed without bypass [16].
In our center we used other techniques in hypoplastic 
arch like patch augmentation using autologous pericar -
dium 7(7%) and end to side anastomosis technique 2(2%).
Ballon angioplasty
Balloon angioplasty is a catheter-based intervention 
employed to address recoarctation of the aorta in infants. 
Under general anesthesia, and after heparinization with 
heparin sulfate 100IU/kg, access to the vascular route 
is typically through the femoral artery by the Seldinger 
technique, the ballon used mostly Sterling ballon (size 
10+/−4 mm).
Caution is warranted not to over-distend the balloon 
to avoid injury to the vessel or the development of aneu -
rysms. After the balloon is deflated, the effectiveness of 
the dilation is assessed by performing angiography again 
and remeasuring the pressure gradient. If the gradient is 
less than 10 mmHg, the dilation is considered successful 
[17].
Careful follow-up is planned for the patient to monitor 
potential complications like vascular injury, stenosis, or 
hypertension [18].
Balloon angioplasty is highly effective in treating block-
ages, although some patients may require repeated inter -
vention to address recurring stenosis.
Statistical analysis
Continuous variables were reported as means and stan -
dard deviations, while categorical variables were pre -
sented as frequencies and percentages. For comparison 
of continuous outcomes between two groups, the stu -
dent t test or Mann Whitney test were used according to 
either passing normality test or not. For comparison of 
categorical values, Fisher exact test was used. In addition, 
a logistic regression analysis was performed to identify 
independent predictors of surgical failure and the need 
for post-surgical catheterization.
Results
This retrospective study included 100 patients with sig -
nificant native CoA (discrete or associated with aortic 
arch hypoplasia) who underwent surgery. The median 
age was 8 days and ranged from 1 to 30 days at the time 
of surgery. Females were more predominant (58%) than 
males (42%). Most of them were presented with respi -
ratory distress, shock and or feeding difficulties. Most 
patients had left side arch (94%). Thoracotomy alone was

Discussion
With refined endovascular modalities, such as balloon 
angioplasty, outcomes have improved for neonates and 
infants with CoA [ 19, 20]. However, survival rates and 
the risk of recoarctation remain points of debate in these 
patients compared to other age groups [21, 22].
In this retrospective study, we aimed to evaluate the 
risk factors associated with surgical failure in the treat -
ment of CoA in infants and to assess the role of catheter-
based interventions in managing postsurgical restenosis

After stratifying our subjects based on the need for 
postoperative diagnostic and interventional catheteriza -
tion, we observed a statistically significant difference in 
patient weight, with those requiring postoperative cath -
eterization having a substantially lower mean weight 
compared to those who did not (2.4 vs. 3.1 kg, p = 0.002). 
Other variables did not significantly vary among our 
subjects. This was subsequently confirmed on multivari -
ate analysis, as we identified weight at operation as the 
only significant factor that could robustly predict surgical 
failure (OR 0.023, 95% CI: 0.913–1.035; p = 0.002). Other 
factors such as age, cardiac anomalies, and noncardiac 
anomalies exhibited no plausible predictivity of operative 
outcomes. Abdelrehim et al. corroborated our findings as 
they found that reintervention due to recoarctation was 
undertaken in 57.1% of patients who weighed less than 
2.5 kg during the primary operation (p = 0.029) [23].
Similarly, Simsek et al. inferred that 58.3% of patients 
who developed recoarctation underwent balloon angio -
plasty. Of those, the number of patients who weighed 
between 0 and 3 kg had a considerably higher rate of 
recoarctation compared to those who were between 3 
and 10 kg or more (p = 0.013) [24].
In alignment with our findings, Soynov et al. indicated 
that weight was a significant predictor of recoarctation, 
i.e. failure of the primary surgery, in both the univariate 
analysis (OR 0.08, 95% CI: 0.0001:0.55; p = 0.024), as well 
as the multivariate analysis (OR 0.03, 95% CI: 0.0007:0.93; 
p = 0.048) (4). Krylova et al. concluded that weight had 
no statistically meaningful association with recoarctation 
after aortic coarctation surgery [25].
Patients with multiple cardiac anomalies had a higher 
rate of postoperative catheterization (81.2% vs. 69%, p = 
0.017). Likewise, those with noncardiac anomalies (GI, 
respiratory, renal, or sepsis) were more likely to require 
catheterization postoperatively (50% vs. 34.5%, p = 
0.007). Although data by Simsek et al. were in line with 
ours, showing that 58.6% of patients with coarctation 
associated with other cardiac anomalies developed coarc-
tation as opposed to 41.4% who had simple coarctation, 
the significance of this was not reported [ 24]. Abdel -
rehim et al. and Krylova et al. both refuted the correla -
tion between associated cardiac anomalies and the risk 
of recoarctation [ 23, 25]. Weismann et al. reported that 
when other cardiac surgeries were simultaneously per -
formed with primary coarctation surgery, the odds of 
developing recoarctation were more than two-fold (OR 
2.37, 95% CI: 1.12:5.02; p = 0.025) [26].
Postoperative catheterization patients had a higher 
complication rate than those without catheterization 
(81.25% vs. 60.7%, p = 0.030). Surgery type ( p = 0.121) 
and intraoperative complications ( p = 0.430) did not sig -
nificantly affect catheterization need. Abdelrehim et al. 
found that 42.9% of patients with postoperative compli -
cations required reintervention compared to 5% without 
complications (p = 0.019) [ 23]. In contrast, Weismann et 
al. reported a higher risk of recoarctation after patch aug-
mentation by sternotomy (OR 3.15, 95% CI: 1.45–6.86; p 
= 0.004) (26), while our study found no statistical associa-
tion between recoarctation and incision type or surgical 
technique.
In our study, catheter-based intervention for recoarcta-
tion resulted in significant improvement in echocardio -
graphic parameters, including pressure gradient and left 
ventricular ejection fraction, as well as clinical outcomes, 
facilitating successful ventilator weaning and hospital 
discharge.
Similarly, Vasile et al. found that catheter interven -
tions like balloon angioplasty are an effective, less inva -
sive option for treating recurrent coarctation (Re-CoA) 
in infants, allowing growth before more durable surgical 
repair. However, restenosis rates are higher after initial 
angioplasty, particularly in younger infants or those with 
arch hypoplasia [27].
Also, Saad and coworkers mention that Balloon angio -
plasty is considered a safe procedure for the management 
of CoA, but its efficacy remains questionable especially 
for young infants with native CoA type. However, it is 
a reliable option for managing Re-CoA children, with a 
lower rate of future re-intervention [28].
According to Rao et al. despite being associated with a 
considerable incidence of recoarctation in neonates and 
infants, balloon angioplasty is a viable option for both 
coarctation and repeat intervention in case of recoarcta -
tion [29].
Rao PS observed that although balloon angioplasty was 
once used for neonatal aortic coarctation, high recur -
rence rates have led most centers to favor surgery. More 
research is needed on stent use in neonates and infants. 
Balloon angioplasty is still preferred for post-surgical 
cases [30].

Limitations
This study is limited by its retrospective and observa -
tional nature. Additionally, the small number of patients 
available for study with short time of follow up maximum 
1.5 years.
Conclusion
This study finds that patient weight at surgery is the main 
predictor of surgical failure and the need for postopera -
tive catheterization in infants with recoarctation of the 
aorta. While other anomalies affect reintervention risk, 
weight remains critical. Balloon angioplasty is effec -
tive for treating recoarctation in low-weight infants. The 
future development of expandable stents may address 
restenosis as children grow.
