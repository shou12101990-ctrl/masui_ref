Background
A univentricular heart is a rare congenital heart defect, 
accounting for approximately 3% of all congenital heart 
diseases (CHD) [ 1]. It includes several conditions [ 2]: 
double inlet atrioventricular (AV) connections (either 
double inlet left ventricle [DILV] or double inlet right 
ventricle), the absence of one AV connection (such as

Abstract
Background Anesthesia management for patients with uncorrected univentricular heart and ectopic pregnancy 
undergoing emergency laparotomy presents significant challenges. Close collaboration among a multidisciplinary 
team, a keen understanding of anatomy and pathophysiology, and optimization of intraoperative hemodynamic 
targets are crucial for successful outcomes.
Case presentation We report the anesthetic challenges encountered in a case of an adult female with uncorrected 
univentricular heart and ectopic pregnancy who required urgent exploratory laparotomy. We summarize critical 
anesthetic considerations for patients with univentricular heart physiology undergoing non-cardiac surgery. The 
patient successfully underwent unilateral salpingectomy under general anesthesia.
Conclusions Multidisciplinary coordination and tailored hemodynamic management were pivotal to successful 
outcomes, providing valuable reference for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of similar cases in the future.

The mortality for uncorrected univentricular hearts is 
very high. A retrospective study in Norway [4, 5] revealed 
that, between 1990 and 2015, the mortality within the 
first month of life for patients with uncorrected univen -
tricular heart was as high as 83.9% (125/149), with only 
2 patients surviving beyond this period (2/151). Survival 
into adulthood without surgical correction has only been 
reported in a few case studies [ 6, 7]. Reports of non-car -
diac surgery in adults with uncorrected univentricular 
hearts are rare. Thus, more clinical experience is needed 
to provide reference for future clinical diagnosis and 
treatment processes. Accordingly, we present a case of 
a complex uncorrected univentricular heart associated 
with an ectopic pregnancy, which underwent a salpingec-
tomy. We also review previously published cases of non-
cardiac surgeries performed in patients with uncorrected 
univentricular hearts. Additionally, we discuss impor -
tant considerations for anesthetic management in these 
patients, emphasizing the significance of understanding 
the pathophysiology of univentricular hearts and the role 
of a multidisciplinary team in ensuring optimal hemody -
namic stability during the perioperative period.
Case presentation
Patient information
A 28-year-old female (40 kg) with uncorrected complex 
CHD presented with 52 days of amenorrhea accompanied 
by progressive abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding. She 
had a history of receiving general anesthesia for an abor -
tion, which she tolerated without major complications. 
However, detailed records from that procedure were 
unavailable. She was not under long-term cardiology fol -
low-up, nor was she taking any cardiac medications prior 
to admission. Her uncorrected CHD (echocardiography 
findings below) resulted in lifelong cyanosis, a baseline 
oxygen saturation (SpO₂) of 78%, and a New York Heart 
Association (NYHA) class III functional status, consis -
tent with a functional capacity of fewer than four MET 
tasks. Surgical correction had been deferred in childhood 
due to parental concerns regarding the perceived high 
risk of surgery and financial constraints.
On admission, elevated beta-human chorionic gonado -
tropin (β-HCG) (3194 mIU/L) and transvaginal ultra -
sound revealed a ruptured left adnexal mass, consistent 
with ectopic pregnancy. Multidisciplinary consensus rec -
ommended emergency laparotomy. Ethics approval from 
the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hos -
pital of Jining Medical University (2024-03-C018) was 
obtained for the publication of this case report.
Clinical and laboratory findings
The patient exhibited marked cyanosis (lips, nail beds), 
digital clubbing, and tachycardia (HR 95 bpm). Trans -
thoracic echocardiography demonstrated situs inversus, 
dextrocardia, univentricular heart, complete atrioven -
tricular canal defect (Fig. 1 A), transposition of the great 
arteries, double aortic arch, and bilateral superior vena 
cava (Fig. 1 C and Fig. 1 D). The univentricular heart was 
anatomically a left ventricle with underdevelopment of 
the right ventricle. Moderate regurgitation of the com -
mon atrioventricular valve (Fig. 1 B) was observed, with 
a peak regurgitant velocity of 4.8 m/s. Pulmonary valve 
stenosis was also noted, with a peak velocity of 3.8 m/s. 
Coagulopathy (prothrombin time (PT) 16.4 s, interna -
tional normalized ratio (INR) 1.33) and polycythemia 
(hemoglobin (Hb) 221 g/L, hematocrit (Hct) 66.1%) were 
noted. Arterial blood gas revealed hypoxemia (partial 
pressure of oxygen (PaO₂) 46 mmHg) and compensated 
metabolic acidosis (pH 7.30, lactate 0.6 mmol/L).

Operative and anesthetic management
Comprehensive invasive hemodynamic monitoring 
was established before general anesthesia was induced. 
Prior to induction, a left radial arterial line and a cen -
tral venous line were placed. A manual defibrillator with 
paddles was confirmed to be immediately available in 
the operating room as the primary backup for arrhyth -
mia management. General anesthesia was selected due 
to hemodynamic instability and coagulopathy. Induction 
with sufentanil (20 µg), midazolam (3 mg), rocuronium 
(40 mg), and etomidate (4 mg) achieved rapid loss of 
consciousness. Mechanical ventilation was initiated with 
an FiO₂ of 1.0. Our hemodynamic management strat -
egy aimed to maintain oxygen saturation at the patient’s 
preoperative baseline level (78%−85%), while avoiding a 
significant increase that could indicate pulmonary over 
circulation at the expense of systemic output. Her SpO₂ 
remained within this target range (79%−85%) throughout 
the procedure.
Upon entering the abdominal cavity, approximately 800 
mL of clotted blood was evacuated, confirming signifi -
cant intra-abdominal hemorrhage. Immediately follow -
ing peritoneal incision and decompression, the patient 
developed profound hypotension (82/40 mmHg) with 
tachycardia (HR 160 bpm). This was consistent with 
an acute reduction in preload due to the sudden loss of 
intra-abdominal pressure.
The initial management targeted hemodynamic stabili -
zation. A phenylephrine bolus (40 µg) was administered 
to rapidly restore systemic vascular resistance and coro -
nary perfusion pressure. This stabilized the blood pres -
sure at 90/60 mmHg and reduced the heart rate to 120 
bpm. Concurrently, fluid resuscitation was initiated with 
a 200 mL bolus of compound sodium chloride solution, 
which increased the CVP from 12 cmH₂O to 14 cmH₂O.

However, the patient experienced a recurrence of 
hypotension, indicating a persistent volume deficit and 
limited preload reserve. An additional 600 mL of fluid 
was administered, raising the CVP further to 16 cmH₂O. 
The observed dissociation between the rising CVP and 
the persistent hemodynamic instability highlighted the 
ventricle’s preload intolerance and diastolic dysfunction. 
The patient’s response underscored the importance of 
afterload support with phenylephrine in this setting, with 
fluid administration playing a secondary, supportive role.
Nevertheless, the persistence of hypotension despite a 
rising CVP indicated a complex hemodynamic state in 
which traditional indicators of volume status were unre -
liable. This likely reflected the limited preload reserve 
and diastolic dysfunction of the single ventricle. In this 
condition, further fluid administration increased intra -
cardiac pressures without improving effective circulating 
volume or cardiac output.
Although the patient was asymptomatic preopera -
tively, a double aortic arch carries lifelong risks of tra -
cheoesophageal compression. Potential manifestations 
include stridor, dysphagia, or recurrent pneumonia [ 8]. 
Perioperatively, airway edema or hematoma could acutely 
exacerbate compression.

Follow-up
During the seven-day postoperative follow-up, the 
patient reported no adverse events. Six months post-dis -
charge, the patient presented with hemoptysis. Given the 
patient’s history of chronic cyanosis, the hemoptysis was 
presumed to be secondary to pulmonary arteriovenous 
malformations (PAVMs). The patient was treated conser-
vatively with supplemental oxygen, systemic tranexamic 
acid, and avoidance of anticoagulants, resulting in par -
tial resolution of symptoms. A definitive diagnostic pro -
cedure (e.g., CT angiography or cardiac catheterization) 
was not performed during the acute episode. Twelve 
months postoperatively, progressive cyanosis (resting 
SpO₂ of 76%) prompted surgical intervention. She under-
went a bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis (Glenn 
procedure). Intraoperative direct measurement revealed 
a critically low pulmonary artery pressure of 8 mmHg, 
indicating favorably low pulmonary vascular resistance. 
Postoperatively, she experienced sustained clinical 
improvement, with oxygen saturation typically ranging 
from the mid-80s to approximately 90% on room air. The 
patient was discharged with stable hemodynamics and no 
perioperative complications.
Review of previous studies
We searched for reports on uncorrected univentricular 
heart patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery (Table 
2). We identified 6 case reports involving a total of 8 
patients with uncorrected univentricular hearts who 
underwent various non-cardiac surgeries [ 9–14]. Among 
them, 5 patients underwent cesarean Sects [ 10, 12, 13]., 
including both emergency and twin pregnancy cesar -
ean deliveries. One patient had an abortion procedure 
[11], one underwent an appendectomy [ 9], another had 
emergency craniotomy for brain abscess removal [ 14]. 
All surviving patients with uncorrected univentricular 
heart were female, aged between 20 and 40 years, with 
NYHA classifications ranging from II to III. Except for 
one patient [ 13], the remaining 7 patients had varying 
degrees of pulmonary hypertension. All 8 patients exhib -
ited some degree of cyanosis, with preoperative oxygen 
saturation levels ranging from 68% to 92%. The anesthetic 
approaches for non-cardiac surgeries in these patients 
varied: Among the 5 cesarean sections, 4 were performed 
under combined spinal-epidural anesthesia [ 10, 12, 13], 
while 1 was performed under general anesthesia [ 12]. 
The patient with uncorrected univentricular heart who 
had an appendectomy received intravenous anesthesia 
combined with regional nerve block [ 9]. The patient who 
underwent craniotomy for intracranial abscess removal 
was managed under general anesthesia [ 14]. Multidisci -
plinary teams (MDT) were involved in 90% of obstetric 
cases, including cardiologists, obstetricians, anesthesiol -
ogists, and neonatologists. MDT planning was critical for 
optimizing hemodynamics and neonatal outcomes. The 
majority of cases (75%) involved a univentricular left ven-
tricle morphology, often accompanied by transposition 
of the great arteries (58%) and pulmonary stenosis (PS, 
67%). Pulmonary stenosis emerged as a critical compen -
satory mechanism, limiting pulmonary blood flow and 
reducing ventricular volume overload, thereby prolong -
ing survival. None of the patients underwent transesoph -
ageal echocardiography (TEE). No perioperative deaths 
were reported in these patients, and all survived.
Discussion
The anesthetic management of patients with uncorrected 
univentricular heart physiology presents formidable 
challenges, necessitating meticulous multidisciplinary 
coordination to address their unique hemodynamic vul -
nerabilities. These individuals exhibit complex circula -
tory adaptations, including limited cardiac reserve and 
altered preload-afterload relationships, which predispose 
them to profound intraoperative instability. Our case 
highlights these challenges in a 28-year-old woman with 
situs inversus, dextrocardia, and univentricular left ven -
tricle physiology undergoing emergency laparotomy for 
ruptured ectopic pregnancy.
Patients with single-ventricle physiology rarely survive 
into adulthood without palliative surgery. However, cer -
tain anatomical configurations—such as a dominant left 
ventricle with moderate pulmonary stenosis (PS)—may 
permit compensated circulation by balancing systemic 
and pulmonary blood flow. In our patient, PS (peak gra -
dient: 59 mmHg) served as a protective mechanism, 
preventing pulmonary over circulation while maintain -
ing adequate systemic perfusion. This aligns with prior 
observations that adults with balanced circulation and

preserved ventricular function (EF 59% in our case) may 
experience delayed symptom onset despite uncorrected 
anatomy [ 7, 15, 16]. The absence of Fontan palliation 
further underscores the precarious equilibrium in such 
patients, where even minor hemodynamic perturbations 
can precipitate decompensation.
The choice of open laparotomy over laparoscopic tech -
niques was driven by the imperative for rapid hemorrhage 
control and avoidance of pneumoperitoneum-induced 
hemodynamic compromise. Increased intra-abdominal 
pressure during laparoscopy could exacerbate preexisting 
venous return limitations in single-ventricle physiology, 
potentially triggering catastrophic hypotension [ 3]. Gen-
eral anesthesia was prioritized over neuraxial techniques 
due to coagulopathy (INR 1.33) and the need for precise 
hemodynamic control.
Anesthetic induction with etomidate and sufentanil 
minimized myocardial depression while suppressing 
sympathetic responses to laryngoscopy. Maintenance 
with sevoflurane permitted rapid titration to surgical 
stimuli, though its negative inotropic effects necessitated 
cautious use. Hemodynamic stability was further chal -
lenged post-peritoneal incision, with hypotension (82/40 
mmHg) and tachycardia (HR 160 bpm) attributable to 
multifactorial mechanisms:
Preload Reduction: Abdominal decompression 
abruptly decreased intra-abdominal pressure, causing 
venous pooling and reduced cardiac filling—a phenom -
enon analogous to post-decompression physiology in 
abdominal compartment syndrome [17].
Sympathetic Activation: Surgical nociception trig -
gered adrenergic surges, exacerbating myocardial oxy -
gen demand in a heart already compromised by chronic 
hypoxemia.
Fluid-Responsive Limitations: Despite aggressive 
resuscitation with 800 mL compound sodium chloride 
(CVP increased from 12 to 16 cmH₂O), single-ventricle 
anatomy restricted preload tolerance, as elevated CVP 
reflected ventricular diastolic dysfunction rather than 
effective volume expansion.
Interpreting the hemodynamic changes in this case was 
challenging. The occurrence of refractory hypotension 
alongside an elevated CVP following fluid administration 
highlights the limitations of conventional monitoring in 
single-ventricle physiology. This phenomenon could be 
attributable to several mechanisms, including diastolic 
dysfunction, volume overload, worsening atrioventricu -
lar valve regurgitation, or myocardial stunning. With -
out TEE, it was impossible to definitively discriminate 
between these possibilities. TEE would have provided 
an invaluable, real-time assessment of ventricular fill -
ing, contractility, and valvular function. This would have 
allowed for more precise guidance of fluid therapy, ino -
tropic support, and vasopressor selection. This case 
strongly underscores the recommendation to use TEE in 
all high-risk non-cardiac surgeries involving patients with 
complex CHD to optimize hemodynamic management.
Although the use of 100% inspired oxygen during 
induction is theoretically risky in single-ventricle physi -
ology due to its potential to reduce pulmonary vascular 
resistance and increase pulmonary blood flow, it was 
deemed necessary in this emergency context. The need to 
maximize oxygen reserve during rapid sequence induc -
tion in a hypovolemic, cyanotic patient took precedence. 
The patient’s fixed pulmonary stenosis had a protective 
effect, as evidenced by the stability of her intraoperative 
oxygen saturation within her baseline range.
Notably, the absence of TEE hindered real-time 
assessment of ventricular filling and contractility. In 
similar high-risk cases, TEE has proven invaluable for 
guiding fluid therapy and inotropic support, particularly 
in patients with complex cardiac anatomy [ 3]. Therefore, 
we strongly recommend using TEE to optimize hemody -
namic management in such high-risk scenarios.
This case underscores the critical role of interdisci -
plinary collaboration. Cardiologists provided essen -
tial insights into ventricular tolerance thresholds, while 
obstetricians prioritized expeditious surgical intervention 
to mitigate hemorrhagic shock. The patient’s subsequent 
cavopulmonary anastomosis at 12 months postopera -
tively exemplifies the longitudinal care required for sin -
gle-ventricle patients, as progressive cyanosis ultimately 
necessitated definitive palliation.
Conclusion
In the perioperative management of adults with an 
uncorrected univentricular heart undergoing non-car -
diac surgery, the underlying pathophysiology is crucial. 
This includes the fragile equilibrium of the pulmonary-
to-systemic flow ratio (Qp: Qs) and protective compen -
satory mechanisms such as pulmonary stenosis. For 
these high-risk patients, TEE is strongly recommended 
to assess hemodynamics in real time. Standardized, mul -
tidisciplinary protocols that integrate cardiology, anes -
thesiology, and surgical expertise must be implemented 
throughout the perioperative duration.
