Functionally Single-Ventricle Complications After Fontan
Palliation—A Narrative Review
Małgorzata Kowalczyk * and Mirosław Kowalski
Department of Adult Congenital Heart Disease, National Institute of Cardiology in Warsaw,
04-628 Warsaw, Poland

Abstract
Functionally single-ventricle (FSV) defects are complex congenital heart anomalies that
require Fontan palliation, a surgical procedure redirecting systemic venous blood directly to
the pulmonary arteries, bypassing the heart. Despite improvements in surgical techniques
and perioperative care leading to enhanced survival rates, patients remain vulnerable to
significant long-term complications, due to the unique Fontan circulation physiology. This
circulation relies on low pulmonary vascular resistance and preserved single-ventricle func-
tion but predisposes patients to venous congestion and reduced cardiac output, resulting in
multi-organ dysfunction. Key cardiovascular complications include systolic and diastolic
dysfunction of the single ventricle, atrioventricular valve regurgitation, arrhythmias, pul-
monary vascular disease, and thromboembolic events. Systemic complications encompass
Fontan-associated liver disease (FALD), protein-losing enteropathy (PLE), plastic bronchi-
tis (PB), renal impairment, and endocrine and psychosocial burdens. All the problems
induce frequent hospitalizations, psychological challenges, and impaired educational and
employment opportunities. Comprehensive management requires multidisciplinary ap-
proaches addressing the complex interplay of hemodynamic, organ-specific problems, and
psychosocial factors inherent to Fontan physiology.
Keywords: Fontan circulation; functionally single ventricle; long-term complications of

Functionally single-ventricle defects are a group of congenital heart defects that are
not amenable for biventricular repair due to abnormal anatomy of one of the ventricles [1].
Defects classified as single-ventricle include mainly tricuspid atresia (TA), right heart
hypoplasia syndrome, including pulmonary valve atresia with an atrioventricular septal
defect, Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS), double-inlet right ventricle, double-
inlet left ventricle (DILV), unbalanced atrioventricular canal (AVC) and morphologically
undifferentiated, functionally single ventricle [2].
A common feature of various morphologically different FSV-type heart defects is the
surgical treatment approach, which involves creating a shunt between the venous flow
and the pulmonary arteries, bypassing the functionally single ventricle, regardless of its
morphology [3]. Patients disqualified from Fontan palliation or refusing surgery have a
poor prognosis [4].
In 1968, Francis Fontan from Bordeaux performed remarkable surgery on patients
with tricuspid atresia [5]. He created a connection between the right atrium and pulmonary

artery in a patient with a previously performed Glenn connection. The systemic venous
return in Fontan circulation goes directly to the pulmonary circulation, bypassing the heart.
In the following years, many surgeons improved the Fontan technique. In 1987, Deleval
proposed a modification with an intra-atrial tunnel with a right atrial posterior wall that
directed venous flow to the pulmonary artery [ 6]. In 1990, Mareceletti and colleagues
reported the use of an extracardiac tunnel, with inferior vena cava flow directed to the
pulmonary artery via an external conduit (TCPC—Total cavopulmonary connection) [7].
Improving surgical techniques and peri-operative care resulted in very good outcomes in
short-term morbidity and mortality [8,9]. Long-term outcomes are also improving. A 2015
study found 10-, 20-, and 30-year survival rates are 74%, 61%, and 43%, respectively [10].
However, patients are prone to severe complications, which are due to the palliative
character of the surgery and the pathophysiology of created circulation.
2. Methodology
This narrative review synthesizes anatomical, physiological, surgical, and failure-
management aspects of the Fontan circulation using a purposive, topic-driven selection of
key historical reports, guideline documents and contemporary interventional and pharma-
cological series. The data derives from long-term outcomes and heart failure phenotype
studies of Fontan circulation in pediatric and adult cohorts, guidelines and consensus
documents on adult congenital heart disease and practical management principles, and
research on targeted treatment and rescue strategies in Fontan patients. Articles were
identified from reference lists of key reviews and guidelines, and by targeted searches
around predefined themes.
3. Pathophysiology of Fontan Circulation
The direct connection between the venous system and the pulmonary arteries means
that blood flow from the systemic veins to the pulmonary capillaries functions in a portal-
circulation-like manner (Figure 1).

The factors determining the efficiency of Fontan circulation are low pulmonary vascu-
lar resistance, preserved systolic and diastolic function of the functionally single ventricle,
normal systemic vascular resistance, and unobstructed venous return. The effectiveness of
venous return bypassing the heart depends on the pressure gradient between the venous
system and the pressure in the pulmonary capillaries [ 11]. The adverse hemodynamic
consequences of Fontan circulation include venous congestion and reduced output of
the functionally single ventricle [12]. Their consequences may include failure of multiple
organs and systems: liver disease secondary to failing Fontan circulation (FALD—Fontan-
associated liver disease), chronic kidney disease (CKD), protein-losing enteropathy (PLE),
plastic bronchitis (PB), and others.
Fontan Failure Phenotypes
Fontan circulatory failure may manifest through several distinct pathways, depending
on the underlying etiology (Figure 2). Four clinical phenotypes have been described:
failure predominantly related to systolic dysfunction (group 1), to diastolic dysfunction
(group 2), failure despite apparently normal hemodynamics (group 3), and failure primarily
attributable to lymphatic abnormalities (group 4) [13].

4. Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Complications
4.1. Single Ventricle Systolic and Diastolic Dysfunction
Remodeling of the functional single ventricle is a key contributor to systolic dysfunc-
tion. At the molecular level, univentricular heart development is associated with altered
expression of genes involved in myocardial contractility, including downregulation of genes
encoding contractile proteins and calcium-handling/adrenergic signaling components (e.g.,
α-myosin heavy chain, sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+-ATPase [SERCA], and β-adrenergic
receptors) [15,16].
Additional FSV cavity remodeling results from chronic volume overload. Following
a systemic-to-pulmonary shunt, FSV preload may increase to approximately 250–350%
of the expected value for a left ventricle [17]. This increased preload promotes eccentric
hypertrophy, progressive dilation, and spherical remodeling. In contrast, the bidirectional
Glenn procedure and subsequent Fontan completion reduce preload (reported as ~80%
of normal value for body size), thereby favoring concentric hypertrophy [ 18]. A further
reduction in preload secondary to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance is associated
with decreased ventricular compliance and increased filling pressures. Moreover, myocar-
dial architectural remodeling and geometric distortion of the FSV contribute to impaired
ventricular contraction [19].
Ventricular interdependence represents an additional determinant of systolic per-
formance, as ventricular contractility depends on interactions between the right and
left ventricles. Ventricular hypoplasia may reduce the efficiency of the dominant ven-
tricle [20]. Myocardial perfusion abnormalities also appear to contribute to functional
deterioration [21]. Before the Fontan procedure, reduced arterial oxygen saturation may
limit oxygen delivery to the subendocardium of the single ventricle [ 22]. After creating
a total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC), a reduction in aortic diastolic pressure—and,
consequently, coronary perfusion—has been observed [23]. Studies in complex congenital
heart disease populations indicate that right ventricular hypertrophy secondary to systemic
pressure loading can impair myocardial perfusion [ 24–26]. Ischemia may occur in the
hypertrophied ventricle because of increased metabolic demand (e.g., during exercise),
analogous to perfusion–demand mismatch in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or severe aor-
tic stenosis. Coronary fistulas, which may reduce effective myocardial oxygen delivery, are

The Fontan procedure is also predisposed to diastolic dysfunction through concentric
myocardial remodeling [28]. Age-related progression of ventricular structural changes,
increased wall stiffness, and a higher proportion of connective tissue are the main con-
tributors to diastolic dysfunction of the FSV . Similar mechanisms (including suboptimal
myocyte stretch) have been implicated in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in
older individuals with anatomically normal hearts. The concept of “accelerated aging” of
the FSV has been supported by findings reported by Oner et al. [29]. In their study, an FSV
ejection fraction <50% was observed in 8% of children during 10 years of follow-up after
the Fontan procedure.
4.2. Atrioventricular Valve Dysfunction
Atrioventricular valve regurgitation (AVVR) is a common complication in patients
with Fontan circulation, leading to increased pulmonary wedge pressure and systemic ve-
nous congestion (Figure 5). Large regurgitant volume decreases cardiac output, activating
the sympathetic nervous system and augmenting vascular resistance. In long-term obser-
vation, AVVR increases the risk of heart failure and arrhythmia, as well as systemic organ
dysfunction, and early mortality [30]. AVVR can be caused by structural and functional
abnormalities in the valve apparatus and ventricular dysfunction. Patients with FSV of
right-ventricular morphology and those with an atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) have
https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15093538
[[p6]]
J. Clin. Med. 2026, 15, 3538 6 of 18
the highest risk of developing AVVR and gradual decline [31]. However, regardless of valve
morphology (mitral vs. tricuspid vs. common valve), AVVR is progressive, and linked to
poor outcomes. Precise diagnostics should always include various imaging techniques.
Transthoracic 2D and 3D echocardiography remains the first-line modality, but followed by
transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), and cardiac
catheterization, if needed. Surgical treatment of AVVR is rarely performed, due to high
risk and uncertain prognosis after the operation. Surgical valve repair is preferred to valve
replacement, unless there is severe valve dysplasia. Unfortunately, surgical treatment does
not guarantee a favorable prognosis. Patients with single-ventricle right morphology and a
history of tricuspid operation have an 8-year cumulative incidence of death or transplan-
tation of 40% [32]. Patients with ventricular dysfunction, protein-losing enteropathy, and
other secondary complications, are at the highest risk of poor outcomes [33].

4.3. Arrhythmia
Atrial arrhythmia is a common complication after a Fontan procedure, with prevalence
varying by study and surgical technique, in approximately 15% to 60% of patients [32]. The
incidence is higher in patients with atriopulmonary Fontan connection compared to lateral
or extracardiac tunnel [33]. The core causes of arrhythmia incidence are chronically elevated
intra-atrial pressure and intracardiac scars after surgical treatment [34]. Most common atrial
tachyarrhythmias in patients with Fontan palliation are intra-atrial re-entrant tachycardia
(IART), focal atrial tachycardia, and atrial fibrillation (AF) (Figure 5). Atrial fibrillation is
becoming increasingly common in adult patients with Fontan palliation, due to the higher
lifespan of Fontan patients [32].
Ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) in patients with a Fontan circulation are less frequent
(approximately 11% of patients), but associated with a higher risk of death or heart
transplant [35]. Non-sustained ventricular arrhythmias are typically discovered during

rhythm monitoring diagnostics. Risk factors of VA include older age at the time of the
Fontan procedure and a history of right ventricular-to-pulmonary artery conduit. Treat-
ment can be challenging, due to complex anatomy. Pharmacological treatment is limited,
as it is often ineffective or has serious side effects. Rapid hemodynamic decline during
arrhythmia requires urgent cardioversion. Despite complex anatomy, catheter ablation
should be considered. In selected patients, the implantation of a defibrillator (ICD) is
required. A new onset of arrhythmia or decline of a recurrent one should always prompt
overall evaluation of the circulatory hemodynamics (Figure 6).

4.4. Pulmonary Vascular Disease
The direct dependence of pulmonary vascular resistance on single-ventricle cardiac
output has been confirmed in invasive studies [ 36]. Abnormalities in the embryonic
development of the pulmonary vasculature in patients with single-ventricle physiology
are thought to determine abnormal structure and function of the pulmonary arteries after
birth [37]. The nature of blood flow is also a factor leading to loss of autoregulatory capacity
of the pulmonary vascular bed and abnormal vascular tissue architecture. Pulsatile flow
through the pulmonary arteries regulates endothelial function, maintains the balance of
vasoactive mediator release, influences smooth-muscle remodeling, and promotes uniform
recruitment of pulmonary arterioles [38,39]. After completion of the Fontan connection, the
protective effects of pulsatile flow on the pulmonary vasculature are absent, having been
replaced by an unfavorable continuous flow pattern [40].
Collectively, these factors contribute to a distinct pattern of adverse pulmonary vascu-
lar remodeling in patients with long-standing Fontan circulation [41]. The histopathologic
changes include eccentric intimal thickening characterized predominantly by acellular
fibrosis with collagen deposition [42]. These alterations are associated with reduced pul-
monary blood flow and the consequent limitation of cardiac output. Accordingly, the

pulmonary vasculature has become a therapeutic target for pharmacologic vasodilation
strategies [43].
4.5. Thromboembolic Complications
After Fontan palliation, patients have an increased risk of thromboembolism (TE) [36].
All elements of Virchow’s triad are present in Fontan circulation: abnormal non-laminal
blood flow, vascular wall injury, and hypercoagulability [44].
Other factors contributing to this elevated risk include the presence of prosthetic
materials and arrhythmias [37]. The most severe clinical manifestations of TE are pulmonary
embolism and stroke. Thrombus formation occurs mostly in the “right side” of circulation,
presenting as venous thrombosis, intra-atrial or Fontan tunnel thrombi, or pulmonary
embolism [45] (Figures 7 and 8).

In patients without a subpulmonary pump, pulmonary embolism obstructs the pul-
monary vasculature—a primary regulator of cardiac output. Stroke is less common than
pulmonary embolism, though recent studies have proved it is often clinically silent and
underdiagnosed [46]. Asymptomatic strokes were diagnosed in 39% patients in cerebral
magnetic resonance imaging. Both pulmonary embolism and stroke are associated with
substantial mortality and morbidity, underscoring the critical importance of thrombopro-
phylaxis. The serious complications associated with TE have led to the recommendation
that anticoagulation therapy should be initiated earlier than in the general population.
Thromboprophylaxis is indicated mostly in secondary prevention, in patients with a history
of TE [2]. However, there is currently no clinical consensus on primary prevention nor the
use of non-vitamin K oral anticoagulants.

5. Systemic Complications
5.1. Fontan-Associated Liver Disease
Fontan-associated liver disease (FALD) represents a major systemic complication in
patients who have undergone the Fontan procedure, with a prevalence exceeding 50% in
this population [47]. The pathophysiology of FALD is primarily driven by chronic hepatic
venous congestion and persistently elevated central venous pressure, which together in-
duce progressive liver injury characterized by fibrosis and cirrhosis [48]. Over time, these
pathological changes significantly elevate the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma, necessitat-
ing vigilant long-term surveillance [49]. Clinically, FALD may remain asymptomatic for
extended periods, complicating timely diagnosis and management. When symptoms do
manifest, they often include severe complications such as ascites, variceal bleeding, and
hepatic encephalopathy [50] (Figure 9). Accurate diagnosis requires a multimodal approach
combining thorough clinical examination with laboratory tests assessing liver function and
hematologic status, advanced imaging techniques to evaluate liver architecture and blood
flow, non-invasive fibrosis scoring systems, and, in select cases, liver biopsy to confirm

histopathological changes [50]. There is no pharmacological treatment of FALD. The use
of agents decreasing pulmonary vascular resistance may ameliorate hemodynamics and
delay progression of FALD [51]. In many cases, severe FALD is rather an outcome of ineffi-
cient Fontan circulation, and it is a presentation of Fontan failure. Patients with end-stage
cirrhosis or portal hypertension due to Fontan failure should be considered for combined
heart and liver transplant [52].

5.2. Protein-Losing Enteropathy
Protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) is another serious systemic complication seen in
5–12% of patients post-Fontan surgery, and is associated with a substantial increase in
morbidity and mortality [53]. PLE results from excessive loss of serum proteins into the
intestinal lumen, leading to hypoalbuminemia. PLE is related to clinical sequelae such as
edema and immunodeficiency [54]. Several risk factors contribute to the development of
PLE, including anatomical variations like non-left ventricular morphology, elevated ven-
tricular end-diastolic pressure, and perioperative factors such as renal failure, prolonged
cardiopulmonary bypass duration, and extended hospitalization after Fontan surgery [55].
The complex interplay of these factors highlights the need for comprehensive assessment
and tailored management strategies to mitigate the incidence and impact of PLE in this vul-
nerable cohort. The first step in PLE diagnostics is to search for characteristic symptoms and
signs (peripheral edema and prolonged infections) along with serum hypoalbuminemia [56].
High alpha-antitrypsin in stool samples confirms a diagnosis of PLE [57]. Multimodality
imaging should be implemented for ruling out reversible causes of PLE. In some cases,
cardiac catheterization is required to exclude elevated pressure in Fontan connection or
to perform catheter interventions [58]. Etiology treatment is important, due to the limited
possibility of pharmacotherapy.

5.3. Plastic Bronchitis
Plastic bronchitis (PB) presents with a constellation of non-specific respiratory symp-
toms such as chronic cough, wheezing, and progressively worsening dyspnea, which can
easily be mistaken for more common pulmonary conditions. The severity of symptoms of-
ten correlates with the degree of airway obstruction caused by the casts, and, in severe cases,
respiratory failure may occur. The lifetime cumulative incidence in survivors of the Fontan
surgery is about 4% [59]. The increased venous pressure, dysfunction of the bronchial mu-
cosa, and increased pressure in the lymphatic vessels, sometimes with broncho-lymphatic
fistulas, are considered to be the main factors involved in the formation of bronchial casts.
Due to the rarity and non-specific presentation of the disease, diagnostic vigilance is neces-
sary for timely diagnosis. Evaluation typically involves imaging techniques such as chest
X-rays and computed tomography (CT) scans, which may reveal airway obstruction or
atelectasis. Ref. [60] states that bronchoscopy plays a crucial role in both diagnosis and man-
agement by allowing direct visualization and removal of bronchial casts [61]. Additionally,
lymphatic imaging techniques, including dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance
lymphangiography, can identify abnormal lymphatic flow and broncho-lymphatic fistulas,
guiding targeted therapeutic interventions [62]. However, patients with a history of PB are
at high risk of early death and heart transplant [63]. The onset of PB should prompt further
evaluation for hemodynamic alterations or lymphatic abnormalities.
6. Other Concerns
Altered hemodynamics and organ-specific comorbidities predispose patients to re-
duced exercise tolerance [64]. Exercise unmasks a limited single-ventricle output reserve.
The absence of a subpulmonary ventricle is responsible for the inappropriately small in-
crease in cardiac output during exertion. Direct connection of the systemic veins to the
pulmonary arteries prevents an increase in pulmonary arterial pressure in response to exer-
cise. Impaired regulation of pulmonary vascular resistance contributes to a reduced flow
reserve. In individuals with a normal circulation, exercise decreases pulmonary vascular
resistance through pulmonary arteriolar dilation and recruitment of additional vessels. In
patients with single-ventricle physiology, regulation of pulmonary vascular resistance via
these mechanisms is minimal or entirely absent [65].
Venous return is driven by the pressure gradient between the systemic veins and the
pulmonary capillaries. The absence of a subpulmonary pump results in chronic venous
congestion. Venous pressures below 18–20 mmHg are generally well tolerated. Low
pulmonary capillary wedge pressure is also required to maintain venous blood flow. Factors
that increase pulmonary capillary pressure (e.g., atrioventricular valve regurgitation, and
systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction of the single ventricle) reduce venous return and
lead to failure of the Fontan circulation. During exercise, venous pressure and pulmonary
blood flow increase only modestly, via the “muscle pump.” Contraction of the lower-
extremity muscles and changes in intrathoracic pressure during respiration provide an
additional driving force for venous return during exertion, but they do not compensate for
the increased preload demand imposed by exercise [66].
Fontan surgery results in stress on renal function. Fontan circulation is associated with
both acute kidney injury (AKI) immediately after the operation and progressive, long-term
kidney function impairment resulting in chronic kidney disease (CKD). Pathophysiological
changes result from incomplete histologic repair after the initial damage during surgery,
nephron loss, compensatory glomerular hypertrophy, and chronic interstitial inflammation
and fibrosis [67]. AKI is common directly after Fontan surgery, occurring in 16% of patients
within 48 h post-operation. The absence of a fenestration and longer duration of bypass are

associated with AKI lasting more than 48 h. Prolonged AKI increases hospital length of
stay and the risk of sternal wound infection [68].
The long-term impact of Fontan palliation on kidney function is primarily caused by
chronically increased pressure in the renal veins and decreased cardiac output, resulting
in low blood volume in the renal arteries. This causes renal impairment, measured by
GFR < 90 mL/min/1.72 m2, in about 10% of patients years after surgery. Chronic kidney
disease is associated with higher mortality long after Fontan surgery [ 69]. Treatment
and prophylaxis of renal failure are especially challenging in this population, as most
nephroprotective drugs decrease cardiac output through vasodilation and volume loss.
Altered hemodynamics after Fontan surgery also impact the endocrine system. Thy-
roid, parathormone, and adrenal hormone levels are often outside normal ranges. Growth
hormone deficiency and malnutrition contribute to growth retardation. Children with
Fontan circulation have mild deficits in height, with greater deficits in those with protein-
losing enteropathy (PLE) [70].
All these problems make Fontan patients more prone to psychiatric and socioeconomic
issues. Frequent hospital stays, psychological difficulties, and challenges in education make
it hard to find and maintain well-paid employment.
7. Future Directions
A high risk of complications raises concerns regarding patients’ quality of life. Most
adverse effects following surgical repair result from specific hemodynamic alterations, and
cannot be fully resolved. Ongoing scientific and clinical efforts aim to mitigate the impact of
venous congestion and reduced cardiac output. Physical exercise remains the most effective
strategy for maintaining overall health in patients with cardiovascular disease [71]. This
approach is similarly beneficial for Fontan patients. Despite their reduced exercise tolerance,
physical activity facilitates the adaptation of Fontan circulation to physiological stress and
enhances the function of the muscle pump [72]. Improved venous return, supported by
well-conditioned leg muscles, can contribute to increased cardiac output.
Recent studies have focused on optimizing physical exercise protocols for Fontan
patients. A recent meta-analysis assessed the impact of exercise on peak oxygen con-
sumption in this population, highlighting exercise performance as a modifiable factor [72].
Cardiac rehabilitation should be routinely implemented, introduced early, and tailored
individually, based on clinical status and mobility. Numerous study findings challenge the
traditional notion that, particularly pediatric patients, require excessive protection from
physical activity. Exercise is also employed as a tool to evaluate Fontan circulation, with
novel diagnostic methods being integrated. Invasive venous-pressure measurement during
cardiopulmonary exercise testing and exercise-induced pulmonary vascular resistance
have been demonstrated to be reliable markers for comprehensive assessment of Fontan
circulation [73,74].
Recent years have seen increased awareness and considerable efforts toward the earlier
diagnosis of Fontan-associated liver disease (FALD). However, the altered pathophysiology
of liver congestion and fibrosis in Fontan patients has rendered classic diagnostic schemes
and scoring systems less reliable. Non-invasive predictors of severe fibrosis, such as
transient elastography combined with platelet count (as utilized in the FonLiver risk
score), have demonstrated better predictive accuracy for severe liver fibrosis compared
to conventional models [ 75]. The diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) within
the specific context of FALD is critically important. A recent review emphasized the
significance of early detection of carcinogenesis, as it is a major factor contributing to
improved survival outcomes [ 76,77]. Furthermore, new insights into micromolecular
changes in the livers of Fontan patients have emerged. A recent study revealed significant
https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15093538
[[p13]]

metabolic abnormalities, particularly in amino acid metabolism, as well as disruptions in
other metabolic pathways [78].
Future research will undoubtedly focus on improving the diagnostics of high pul-
monary vascular resistance (PVR) and developing therapeutic interventions aimed at
preventing pulmonary vascular disease in this population. The utility of PVR measurement
has proven to be a valuable prognostic factor during the subsequent stages of Fontan
palliation [79–81] Scientists are actively seeking novel pharmacotherapeutic agents that
may prevent Fontan circulation failure. The pulmonary vasculature remains the main
therapeutic target. Pulmonary vasodilators have been increasingly utilized worldwide,
and new agents are being administered in patients with single-ventricle physiology [82,83].
Modern devices, such as left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) or atrial flow regulators,
are widely used in patients with normal cardiac anatomy. An increasing number of studies
are being published on the use of these devices in patients with congenital heart defects,
including those after the Fontan procedure [ 84–86] The atrial flow regulator has been
successfully used to create a fenestration between the pulmonary artery and the left atrium,
leading to clinical improvement in patients with failing Fontan circulation [87]. Growing
experience is being gained with the broader use of LVADs in Fontan patients, where the
device is primarily employed as a bridge to transplantation [88,89].
8. Conclusions
Fontan surgery remains the sole technique to extend the lifespan of patients with a
single-ventricle heart. Nevertheless, it is a palliative procedure associated with numerous
short- and long-term complications. During the initial era of Fontan palliation, only patients
meeting the so-called “10 commandments” were eligible for the surgery [90]. Currently,
a broader patient population undergoes this procedure. This expanded approach offers
hope to the majority of patients but concurrently increases the risk of comorbidities and
diminished quality of life. Most long-term complications arise from specific circulatory
characteristics, namely chronic systemic venous congestion and reduced cardiac output.
Advances in diagnostic methods allow for the early detection of cardiovascular and sys-
temic organ failure. While some complications can be managed pharmacologically or
through interventional procedures, optimal hemodynamic conditions can only be achieved
via heart transplantation. End-organ damage frequently necessitates simultaneous kidney
or liver transplantation. Ongoing advances in diagnostics, pharmacotherapy, and mechani-
cal circulatory support offer promising opportunities to improve long-term outcomes and
quality of life in this growing patient population.
