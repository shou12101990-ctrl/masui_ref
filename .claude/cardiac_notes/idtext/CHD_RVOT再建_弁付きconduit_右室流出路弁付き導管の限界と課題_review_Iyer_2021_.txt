INVITED EDITORIAL
Valved conduits in the right ventricular outflow — the Achilles heel
of congenital heart surgery!
Krishna Subramony Iyer1
Received: 16 August 2020 / Revised: 28 August 2020 / Accepted: 5 September 2020 / Published online: 9 October 2020
# Indian Association of Cardiovascular-Thoracic Surgeons 2020
Abstract
Valved conduits play an important role in the reconstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract, but their limited longevity
remains an unsolved problem for the congenital heart surgeon.
Keywords Valved conduits . Right ventricular outflow . Longevity
A right ventricle to pulmonary artery (RV-PA) conduit is a
necessity in many congenital heart surgeries, where a natural
right ventricle to pulmonary pathway does not exist or is in-
adequate. Included in this subset of patients are discordant
ventriculo-arterial connections, pulmonary atresia, truncus
arteriosus, and variants of tetralogy of Fallot. The RV-PA
connection has remained the Achilles heel for congenital heart
surgeons, simply because an ideal conduit still remains elusive
and every child receiving a valve conduit irrespective of the
type is doomed to a lifetime of reoperations.
Homograft conduits have had a chequered history since their
first use in the 1960s and 1970s [1]. Initial results were suboptimal
due to early calcific degeneration. Improvements in techniques of
harvesting, sterilization, and cryo-preservation substantially im-
proved their performance and led to renewed interest in their
use. In the 1970s and 1980s, xenograft valved conduits came in
vogue— porcine valves seated within Dacron or pericardial tubes
[2]. Most popular in this category were the Carpentier-Edwards
and the Hancock valved conduits. Their popularity was enhanced
by the ready off-the-shelf availability and their wide range of
sizes. In more recent times, the bovine jugular vein conduit,
marketed as the Medtronic Contegra valved conduit, has gained
widespread popularity [3]. In addition to homografts and commer-
cially available conduits, surgeons often resort to making conduits
in the operating room— the so-called handsewn conduits, using
different materials for thetube and valve leaflets [4].
A valved conduit serves two purposes — (a) it provides an
unobstructed extra-cardiac passage for blood to flow from the
right ventricle to the pulmonary artery and (b) it prevents
blood from regurgitating into the right ventricle during ven-
tricular systole. The choice of a conduit is a decision based on
many factors including importantly availability, ease of im-
plantation, and expected longevity. In resource-limited coun-
tries, cost of the conduit also plays an important role.
In this issue of IJTCVS, Raja et al. [ 5] publish their single-
institution experience with the use of homografts, which were
banked in their own institution, in the right ventricular out-
flow. Given the paucity of such data from this country, this
publication is certainly most welcome. The early- and
intermediate-term outcomes have been stated and certain con-
clusions drawn. By their own admission, the authors have
accepted that the numbers and the wide spread of the patient
groups makes it difficult for any categorical recommendations
to be made. Consistent with world experience, the authors
have shown a better performance with pulmonary homografts
as compared with aortic homografts [6]. It is also a given that
longevity of smaller sized grafts would be shorter than larger
grafts. Early and late mortality cannot realistically be blamed
on the type of conduit, unless it is shown to be directly as a
result of structural failure of the conduit. The relatively high
early and late mortality presented in the authors ’series there-
fore needs to be overlooked, while evaluating the manuscript.
The manuscript, however, highlights certain issues that are
relevant to our country. It is clear that the setting up of an in-
house homograft bank makes great sense for any active pediatric
cardiac unit [7]. Some of the compelling advantages of homo-
grafts are the low cost, ease of implantation, and the relative
resistance to infection. It definitely helps to reduce the

dependence on commercially available conduits that are gener-
ally prohibitively expensive and subject to manipulation by mar-
ket forces. For example, the larger sizes of the Medtronic
Contegra conduit went off the shelves as the demand increased
for the percutaneous Melody valve marketed by the same com-

smaller sizes required for neonates and infants (necessitating a
suboptimal downsizing of larger homografts as described by the
authors) and the tendency for dense calcification over time,
which makes the reoperation for replacement a challenge.
Nevertheless, the pulmonary homograft has proven to be the best
substitute for the right ventricular outflow in the Ross operation
[8], and in the older patients, a large-sized homograft can provide
many many years of event-free survival.
In some of the more affluent countries, homografts are com-
mercially prepared and marketedwith a substantial increase in
cost, which takes away the price advantage. Nevertheless, the
commercial availability has substantially increased its popularity
and widespread use. It is pertinent to highlight here that the
explosive rise in heart transplants has made available a large pool
of homografts, which are harvested from the explanted hearts.
However, in affluent countries like Japan, cultural issues prevent
the harvesting of homografts and this has spurred the develop-
ment of their own handsewn polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)
conduit, which has delivered impressive results [9].
The most important attribute of a conduit, by which it is
judged, is the longevity in the patient after implantation. This is
usually measured by the replacement or intervention-free surviv-
al. The longevity of the conduit determines the number of
reoperations that a patient has to undergo during his or her life-
time. Most conduits fail because of calcific degeneration of the
conduit valve or development of excessive“peel,” resulting in
progressive conduit stenosis. Much of this is related to the inher-
ent biological response of the body to the conduit material.
However, an important element contributing to longevity is
how streamlined and turbulence free the blood flow within the
conduit is after implantation. There are therefore many less talked
about factors relating to surgical technique that can contribute to
exaggerated failure. It is important to choose the right size of
conduit, which is appropriate for the size of the patient and the
size of the pulmonary arteries. An ambitious oversizing with a
view to improving longevity may actually prove counterproduc-
tive. The lie and course of the conduit are also a key factor.
Compression of the conduit between the heart and the overlying
sternum is one of the commonest causes of early conduit failure,
especially when the valve is unstented, as it is in homografts.
Acute angulation at either ends of the conduit has to be
prevented. Lastly, the technique of anastomosis of the distal
end plays an important role in reducing troublesome distal anas-
tomotic narrowing. It is therefore difficult to dissect out these
various elements that contribute to conduit failure, while evalu-
ating a manuscript that presents outcomes of conduit surgery.
Less than ideal surgical technique may therefore be a disservice
to the reputation of a conduit!
Given the fickle nature of valved conduits, many surgeons
would prefer surgical alternatives that avoid their use and rely
more on the patients ’ tissues, which have growth potential
[10]. Examples of these are the Barbero-Marciel procedure
for repair of truncus arteriosus and the REV (réparation à
l’ètage ventriculaire) procedure for transposition of great ar-
teries with ventricular septal defect and pulmonary stenosis.
However, in many cases, the use of a valved conduit is
absolutely unavoidable. As stated before, the search for a
valved conduit that can function lifelong without degeneration
and also grow along with the patient continues, but such an
ideal conduit remains a“chimera.”For the present, we have to
make the best use of what is available and ensure that the
patient gets the best, which he or she deserves.
