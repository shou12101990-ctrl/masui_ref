Introduction
Single ventricle (SV) heart disease refers to any congenital 
heart disease (CHD) with a single functional ventricle 
capable of supporting cardiac output. Although its initial 
description dates to 1867, modern medicine has only 
provided solutions to manage SV heart disease relatively 
recently (1). There are different approaches to classifying 
SV anatomy, each attempting to explain the distinct nuances 
of the disease processes it encompasses (2-6). Disease 
process that can result in SV physiology include double inlet 
left ventricle (DIL V), common ventricle, univentricular 
atrioventricular connection, hypoplastic left heart syndrome 
(HLHS), hypoplastic right heart syndrome, tricuspid 
atresia, unbalanced atrioventricular canal (with significant 
imbalance), mitral atresia normal aortic root and heterotaxy

Abstract: Congenital cardiac surgery has advanced considerably over the last 4 decades with improvements 
in virtually every aspect of care. T echnical procedures are well described in literature and taught globally 
while expected results are published in databases such as the Society of Thoracic Surgery (STS) and 
European Association of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS). Outcomes have progressed from uncertain 
survival to expected program mortalities of nearly 2%. This has been driven in part by advances in surgical 
technique, but also incremental improvements in the entire patient care pathway. Single ventricles (SVs) 
remain one of our most challenging cohorts of patients and our most resource intensive. Although outcomes 
have improved in the past decades, overall morbidity and mortality exceeds that of our biventricular patients. 
Our goal is to highlight some of the impactful advances surrounding SV care, discuss some of the ideology 
that led us to our current care processes as it applies to our single center program. We also discuss areas of 
improvement in the future. We have also focused on mechanical support and transplantation as it remains 
a keystone in SV care. We hope to provide insight into how to improve the care of patients born with SV 
disease.

Methods
Relevant information for this review was obtained by 
searching the PubMed database and reviewing key papers 
identified by experienced team members which have 
influenced practice. The PubMed search strategy utilized 
was based on key words relevant to SV care throughout 
the overall care pathway and focused on contemporary 
literature from 2000 to 2024 in English. Publications prior 
to this were included when identified as being impactful 
by our team members or to provide historical context. Our 
experience in caring for SVs over the past few decades 
provided direction and insight into the areas seen as 
advancements in care as well as areas for future development 
and improvement. Key words used included “single 
ventricle care”, “single ventricle imaging”, “ICU care of 
single ventricles”, “ventricular assist device use in single 
ventricles”, “cardiac transplantation in single ventricles”. 
The care areas discussed include early care decision making, 
intensive care unit (ICU) care, surgical care, the home 
monitoring program (HMP) and transplantation. 
Advanced imaging 
Image generation
Historically three-dimensional (3D) modelling has been a 
novelty more than a required clinical tool. In the last decade 
however, its value in CHD has been recognized and it is 
now deployed in surgical programs with several reports 
of its utility (10-12). It reveals anatomical relationships 
that can elevate understanding and provide insight when 
compared with scrolling through two-dimensional (2D) 
datasets. Despite its potential, it is not universally deployed, 
and individual programs utilize this technology differently. 
Our program developed its 3D capabilities recently and 
has benefited from this resource investment which we have 
reported on in the context of double outlet right ventricle 
(RV) care (13). In 3D reconstruction, image acquisition 
from computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance 
imaging (MRI) is refined and segmented to build volumes 
revealing 3D structures ( Figure 1 ). Complex patients are 
the beneficiaries of advanced imaging and that has certainly 
been true of SV patients. In our institutional experience, 
patient families find 3D models useful in understanding 
their child’s disease and often request to keep these models 
for future reference. We have also utilized this across 
the spectrum of SV disease in both decision making and 
operative planning. We initially focused on physical printing 
and have slowly moved to a digital medium.
Printing technology
Most of our physical prints are done with hardened material 
that allows for color differentiation of anatomy with 
physical strength. With these models, when partial slices 
are created, they can maintain their structural integrity 
allowing significant portions of the ventricular wall to be 
removed ( Figure 2 ). This technique relies on our powder 
bed fusion (HP MJF580) printer. We also maintain the 
ability to 3D print softer models intended to mimic tissue 
properties utilizing a material jetting process (Stratasys 
J750 Digital Anatomy Printer). This soft material allows 
the model to be cut/sliced and manipulated in ways that 
may simulate surgical procedures. We have made use of 
this when experimenting with new surgical techniques. 
Our interventional team has used this technique along with 
digital techniques to model patent ductus arteriosus stenting 
(PDAS) in patients with tortuous anatomy ( Figure 3 ).

Our experience with predictive modelling
Predictive modeling has been used on a rudimentary 
basis in our program by creating models for size fitting 
ventricular assist devices (VADs) in SVs with success  
(Figure 5 ). This technique has been successful in fitting 
donors and recipients for heart transplantation in cases of 
size mismatch as well. In Video 1 , we see a patient donor 
heart with a donor- recipient weight ratio of 1:1 being 
modeled in the thoracic cavity of the recipient. The poor fit 
on 2D and 3D models led to our declining of the otherwise 
acceptable heart.
Future directions
As our comfort grows in this 3D space, we, like many 
programs, have become interested in virtual reality and 
augmented display systems which are a natural evolution 
of this technology. Another step forward in modelling is 
to create biophysical models to simulate cardiac mechanics 
which are in development and reported by multiple 
programs (14,15). The ability to anticipate growth, re-
modelling and valvar changes would add predictive value 
to management and would represent a generational leap 
forward in decision making. Computational modelling 
of the heart however, has been a slow work in progress 
for CHD including SVs (16,17). We have progressed 
far enough to ask the question: when will this lead to 
meaningful clinical translation? The answer has been 
soon, but not yet (18). When this technology does mature 
and enter our clinical space, it has the potential to be 
transformative for our field.

Interstage care and HMP evolution 
Introduction
SV palliation differs from other CHD management 
pathways in that it requires a series of stepwise interventions 
to achieve a more stable hemodynamic circulation. The 
result of this SV pathway, which was first developed in 
the 1980s, is not curative, but palliative, with the goal 
of survivable circulatory anatomy (19). Each anatomical 
correction results in physiology with nuances that have 
the potential to produce morbidity and mortality. This 
physiology requires attention in both an inpatient and 
outpatient setting to allow patients to progress. Attention 
to physiological deviations early on whether as an inpatient 
or outpatient has been a key step in improving our 
care of this patient population. This differs from other 
surgically repairable disease processes which often result 
in significantly improved physiology post repair in two 
ventricle systems. 
Interstage mortality
In SV palliation, the first cardiac intervention is typically 
required in the neonatal period, with the second intervention 
occurring in infancy, around 3–6 months of age. The period 
between the first and second cardiac intervention is referred 
to as the interstage period. Despite years of experience, 
this interstage period was marked by significant morbidity 
and mortality. Even in the early 2000s, interstage mortality 
after a stage 1 operation was reported as 11–15% in North 
American centers (20,21). It was known at that time 
anatomic variables such as an atrial septal defect restriction, 
valve insufficiency, aortic arch obstruction and poor 
ventricular function were linked to interstage death (22).  
These anatomic issues are responsible for physiologic 
perturbations and can be detected with conventional 
imaging and monitoring. When these issues are detected 
in a hospital setting, intervention can be undertaken to fix 
these issues. We have also recognized anatomic issues only 
accounted for some of the interstage mortality, and other 
factors were contributing, which continues to be an ongoing 
area of study. 
HMPs
After hospital discharge, the ability to monitor for 
hemodynamic changes becomes more challenging. In 
2003 Ghanayem and colleagues proposed that monitoring 
parameters such as oxygen saturations and weight gain 
in the ambulatory setting may be able to detect early 
signs of hemodynamic changes or distress and facilitate 
patient intervention before clinical deterioration (23). This 
paradigm became known as the interstage HMP (23). This 
study compared patients who had HMP to those without 
and demonstrated a significant mortality reduction with 
HMP . Despite the small number of patients, this was 
somewhat of an epiphany for the field of SV care in that 
clear evidence demonstrated mortality could be reduced 
with monitoring as outpatients. In 2006 the National 
Pediatric Cardiology Quality Improvement Collaborative 
(NPC-QIC) was created to bring care teams together with 
the goal of reducing interstage mortality through quality 
improvement initiatives. A study in 2011 demonstrated that 
SV infants followed in HMP demonstrated an improved rate 
of interstage weight gain which resulted in earlier stage 2 
operations without a mortality difference but also improved 
1-year transplant free survival (24). This data further 
promoted the adoption of these strategies in SV care.
Improving survival with home monitoring
A review in 2014 outlined how some programs were able 
to decrease interstage mortality to 0% with the average 
reduction from 15.7% to 5.7% (25).  Siehr et al.  reported 
improved interstage survival to 100% over 5 years with 
the protocol altering care in 37% of their patients (25). 
Through work of the NPC-QIC, a list of physiologic 
parameters and concerning changes, known as the “red 
flag” criteria were developed (26). These include issues with 
weight gain and feeding tolerance (weight loss >30 g/days,  
failure to gain 20 g in 3 days, intake <100 cc/day, diarrhea), 
hypoxemia (saturations <75%, cyanosis) impaired cardiac 
output (tachypnea, dyspnea, irritability, vomiting) and 
infection (temperature >38 ℃) (26). Castellanos et al. 
reported more unplanned re-interventions and an 80% 
reduction in mortality with HMP suggesting high risk

Our experience with home monitoring
Our institution’s HMP was created in 2017 and has 
continued to grow into a comprehensive, multidisciplinary 
model of care to support our infants living with SV heart 
disease. Our program, like many other HMP’s around the 
country have continued to expand, to include not only a 
cardiologist and nursing specialty, but feeding therapist, 
dietician, and social worker. The HMP team becomes 
involved with the family from the time of diagnosis 
(including during the fetal period) and remains involved 
throughout the interstage journey ( Figure 6 ). Our team 
works closely with the intensive care team prior to hospital 
discharge, to assess patients’ readiness for discharge and 
to ensure appropriate parent education is provided to 
increase rates of success in the ambulatory setting. The 
HMP becomes the patient’s medical home during this 
critical interstage period. The benefit of a dedicated team 
includes intimate knowledge and familiarity of the complex 
patients within the care team. We rely on consistent 
and direct communication with the family through the 
electronic medical record (EMR), where patient data can 
be reviewed daily and interventions can occur in a timely 
matter. Additional layers of communication, including the 
use of Emergency Care Plans in the EMR help other health 
care members identify these patients as high risk. This

Future directions
In the future, our techniques and surveillance will likely 
progress to provide a wider range of data points for analysis, 
including wearable technologies. As technology improves 
and we move towards the purported “Internet of Things”, 
data collection may be streamlined or automated. Analysis 
of this data could also be tasked to software/artificial 
intelligence (AI) driven management proactively bringing 
patients to our service. Digital HMP platforms have already 
been used with success (30). The use of 5G internet of 
things monitoring has been demonstrated in adult cardiac 
literature post revascularization and for heart failure 
monitoring with positive results (31,32). The concept of 
surveillance has already been proven successful and at our 
current state, how we apply new technology to build out 
our current HMP may result in even better outcomes. 
Conclusion
Presently, HMP represents an eloquent and effective 
solution that delivers impactful results. More importantly, 
it represents our recognition/realization that care needs to 
extend to the ambulatory setting. This area of development 
has been one of the highest yield areas of improvement 
in our program and is a critical part of our management. 
HMP is a reminder that palliation is not a cure, only a 
temporizing sequence of interventions in this population. 
With technological advancements, we see exciting 
possibilities for how HMP may be further developed in the 
future. These developments are certain to benefit patient 
care teams, families and SV patients.
Intensive care/early post-operative management
Introduction
Critical care has been a pillar in cardiac surgical care for 
decades since we endeavored to surgically manage heart 
disease. SVs are a complex subset of patients and the need 
for high acuity, focused, multisystem management in this 
population cannot be overstated. Even in the setting of a 
technically perfect surgical reconstruction, the quality of 
ICU care can determine the difference between survival 
and mortality. The progress made in ICU care can be 
broadly categorized in terms of human capital development, 
clinical sciences, and technological advancement. When 
we consider how to improve SV care, our high acuity 
management in ICU is an important part of our clinical 
pathway.
Human capital
In terms of human capital development, building an 
experienced team is one of the most important aspects 
of delivery in cardiac surgical care. Volume in caring 
for patients with specific anomalies and familiarity with 
surgical techniques allows for anticipation of post-operative 
physiology and problems early on. Well trained team 
members such as fellows, nurse practitioners, physician 
assistants, nurses and respiratory therapists are all 
independent force-multipliers who can recognize threats 
early enough to initiate appropriate management creating 
a web of safety around at-risk patients. A weak point in 
any discipline can compromise the integrity of the team 
which may manifest in outcome measures. The skill level 
of a team is tested when managing SVs as they consistently

Physiology—circulatory balance
The science of cardiac critical care has slowly progressed 
with numerous publications over the last few decades with 
some having significant impact in our perception of early 
post-operative care (35). As early as the 1990s T wedell 
and group provided key insight into how we manage the 
pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and systemic vascular 
resistance (SVR) relationship (36). The hallmark of SV 
physiology is the Qp/Qs ratio [pulmonary blood flow (PBF)/
systemic blood flow] and how we balance this ratio has 
profound impact on our post-operative hemodynamics and 
outcome. The authors commented on the use of the alpha 
blocking agent phenoxybenzamine and demonstrated value 
in preventing systemic hypoperfusion by high pulmonary 
vascular runoff post Norwood. Phenoxybenzamine use 
led to a Qp/Qs closer to 1 and a higher systemic venous 
saturation (36). This balancing act between PVR and SVR 
to maximize somatic oxygen delivery remains a focus of 
early stage 1 palliation (S1P) post operative care. Although 
the medications we utilize have evolved according to 
practice (which may be center specific), these principles 
have been maintained. There are a limited number of agents 
commonly used to augment cardiac output post cardiac 
surgery and they can be described as catecholaminergic 
inotropes, inodilators, systemic vasodilators, and pulmonary 
vasodilators (37). Of these agents, milrinone has become a 
commonly deployed agent and its value has been supported 
by studies, including those comparing it to epinephrine and 
dopamine (38,39). Inhaled nitric oxide has also been shown 
to be a safe adjunct to milrinone therapy in maintaining the 
balance of cardiac output pre-operatively (40). Although the 
cocktail of agents may expand, their use will likely reflect 
our attempt to better preserve the principles stated above. 
Goal directed targets
A study in 2007 demonstrated that goal directed central 
venous saturations were associated with early survival 
post Norwood and the absence of this regulation was 
associated with end organ failure, extracorporeal membrane 
oxygenation (ECMO) and mortality (41). This has provided 
goals for ICU teams to target immediately post-operatively. 
Focus on arterial saturations may prevent the recognition of 
systemic mal-perfusion that central venous gas monitoring 
can reveal. Insight from this helped identify venous 
saturation targets between 55–60% which is an actionable 
item for teams in the ICU who use these values as markers 
of cardiac output distribution.
Near infra-red spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring has 
also shifted thinking and proven to be a high yield strategy 
in assessing perfusion. The relationship between cerebral 
perfusion and neurodevelopment may seem logical but 
it was not well described until 2013. Hoffman and group 
determined by cerebral NIRS that low cerebral perfusion 
states are associated with reduced neurodevelopmental 
performance in survivors (42). This highlighted the need 
not only for continued cerebral NIRS monitoring, but 
target saturation levels for early ICU care. This also 
represented a shift in thinking allowing us to look beyond 
early mortality as a metric that we should be targeting. 
Cerebral and somatic NIRS was also studied as a 
predictive tool for survival and ECMO utilization in HLHS 
patients post S1P in 2017 (43). Early NIRS saturations at  
6 hours were predictive of mortality and ECMO use 
providing a target for goal directed intervention. This was 
followed by the recognition that distinct hemodynamic and 
physiologic states have different mortality risk profiles (44).  
The first 48 hours of measurements were indicative of 
outcome and suggested this early window would provide a 
rational target for interventions. NIRS measures provided 
an accurate assessment of regional hypoxia-ischemia, even 
in the absence of a global reduction in central venous 
saturations. Normal arterial-regional differences in oxygen 
extraction could now become targets for circulatory 
management. NIRS could provide insight into SVR 
providing real time data regarding circulatory deficiencies. 
Survivors tended to be associated with hemodynamic states 
with high cardiac output and high NIRs and low SVR. 
High SVR states were defined as unstable and transition to 
ECMO was associated with low cardiac output. Augmenting 
inodilator and hemoglobin levels were found to be related to 
improved hemodynamic conditions and predicted movement 
away from low cardiac output states (45). This is consistent 
with what has been observed physiologically with the use 
of inotropes such as milrinone. As a corollary to this, there 
has also been a general movement towards targeted and 
titratable therapy and away from indiscriminate prolonged 
vasodilation (38,46). 
Each of these concepts have had an impact on our ICU 
care. They highlight how technology and new techniques 
can change our perception of hemodynamic management

V ascular access
In terms of day-to-day management, we make a concerted 
effort to preserve vasculature in these patients as they will 
have re-interventions throughout their life. We preserve 
femoral arteries by using radial arterial lines in all patients 
when possible. We attempt to preserve venous access with 
the use of trans-thoracic lines (TTL) that are tunneled 
under the skin and placed in the right atrium in the 
operating room before chest closure. With TTL use, central 
lines and peripheral intravenous lines (IVs) can be removed 
on post operative day 1 and this may be beneficial in terms 
of long-term patency of the venous system and infection 
prevention. Other authors including the Pediatric Cardiac 
Critical Care Consortium (PC4) have published data on 
line infections and associated thrombosis with conventional 
line strategies (47,48). TTL ’s in our program are typically 
7 Fr cuffed double lumen lines and are removed the day of 
discharge with some lines being used for several months. 
The concept of TTLs has been explored by other authors 
as well (49-51). Our own series has been published with the 
use of TTLs where 35% of patients were SVs (52). Drawing 
from this experience, subjectively, TTL ’s have provided 
important life-lines for our SV patients in diagnostics and 
resuscitation. Although these distinct management nuances 
appear innocuous, we have observed that vessel preservation 
plays an important role in the long-term success of this 
population. 
Data growth and collaboration
With growing interest in quality improvement and expertise 
at multiple centers, the PC4 has been coordinating an 
international group of critical care physicians to share  
data (53). Our institution participates in this initiative and 
has benefited from publications regarding (but not limited 
to) cardiac arrest prevention, acute kidney injury in neonates, 
and the impact of unplanned reinterventions (54-56).  
This has been impactful in our care delivery and our 
participation in this consortium facilitates the incorporation 
of new concepts from PC4 into our units practices. 
T echnology has increased our ability to capture and 
analyze events but has also increased the volume of data we 
have had access to. We have implemented Sickbay (Medical 
Informatics Corp., Houston, TX, USA) as a strategy to 
improve our data interpretation and interrogation (57).  
Other platforms such as Etiometry T3 (T racking, 
T rajectory, T riggering) exist which provide AI driven 
analytics and decision-making assistance (58). As this 
technology matures, we hope to achieve a deeper level of 
insight into our patient monitoring.
Our ICU management is constantly being tested by the 
SV population as we work towards improved care. The 
recognition of the complexity of these patients across our 
profession led to the creation of the NPCQIC which is 
another collaborative that we engage with. We participate 
in data collection and analysis as well as utilize its insights 
to improve the care in our unit. Its primary objective was 
to function as a conduit for the exchange of information 
to improve the overall care of SV patients (59). Moving 
forward, it will be multi-institutional, data driven analysis 
tool. These initiatives and technological advancements 
together not only change our care delivery but increase the 
rate at which we learn and advance our techniques. 
Conclusion
ICU care has changed drastically over the past few 
decades. There has been an acceleration in the growth 
of data available to learn from and new ways to mine 
this information. The core principles of physiological 
management however remain, and the lessons learned in 
terms of balancing circulation and goal directed therapy are 
as applicable today as they were in the past. 
The development of organizational structures linking 
institutions allows for data sharing but also helps bring 
consistent ideology and care perspectives to different 
institutions. SV patients are the beneficiaries of these 
developments, and we hope this will continue to improve 
our care delivery moving forward.

Collaterals
SV patients are prone to developing significant 
aortopulmonary collaterals (APC) which are associated with 
lower transplant free survival (66). Collateral management 
has been problematic in the past for our institution and 
developing strategies to address this has been a work in 
progress. Our approach is to use MRI to quantify APC 
load when possible and to intervene proactively in patients 
with a burden >25% (67,68). We routinely obtain cardiac 
MRIs pre-Glenn to evaluate readiness for Glenn and other 
information we glean from this study includes ventricular 
function and atrioventricular valve regurgitation as well 
as quantitation of SVC flow (69,70). We also assess for 
delayed enhancement which can provide insight into 
myocardial health. Importantly, T2-weighted lymphatic 
imaging is included which can provide insight into the risk 
of developing pleural effusions following the Glenn. We do 
not consistently perform pre-Fontan MRIs, as our practice 
is to aggressively close collateral prior to Fontan. 
SV patients in general have repeat re-interventions 
and these additional procedures are associated with poor 
overall prognosis (71). This is representative of the risk 
of the procedure as well as the underlying substrate 
requiring the intervention, highlighting the need for skilled 
proceduralists.
PDAS
A technique that has been in development for several 
decades at our institution is the use of PDAS which has early 
experiments dating back to the 1990s (72). Benefits such as 
lower mortality, morbidity and more symmetric PA growth 
have been reported in literature (73-77). Results in the SV 
population were also promising (63). The conventional 
technique for managing duct dependent PBF has been 
aorto-pulmonary shunts (APS) which have been reported 
to have mortality rates between 2% and 19% over decades 
of observation depending on the specific cardiac diagnosis  
(78-80). Although considered a reasonable strategy, the 
inability to improve upon the observed mortality rates over 
time is suggestive that APS may have intrinsic limitations. 
PDAS, however, allows for ductal flow to be maintained as 
well as a native intima which may be less prone to thrombosis 
than a synthetic (Gortex™,  Newark, USA) shunt. After 
reviewing our experience with PDAS (75), we moved to 
PDAS as a primary strategy for all SV duct dependent 
PBF patients and have described this technique (81,82). 
This ideologic shift in practice has led to one of the most 
significant changes to our post-operative care of SV patients. 
The absence of early hemodynamic instability that often 
accompanies APS has led to smooth post-operative courses 
in both the ICU and cardiology wards. Benefits with respect 
to early extubation and reduction in ICU length of stay as 
well as hospital length of stay have been realized. Patients 
have smooth outpatient experiences and perhaps another 
benefit is the ability to perform stage 2 reconstructions on 
a virgin chest. The absence of mediastinal adhesions may 
also facilitate the use of off-pump techniques in bidirectional 
Glenn (BDG) construction which is also discussed. Other 
realized benefits include early oral feeding and a trend 
towards better neurodevelopmental outcomes (83). 
Emerging techniques based on PDAS
As a corollary to PDAS technology and its use, scenarios 
such as heterotaxy-total anomalous pulmonary venous 
return (TAPVR) vertical veins can also be managed 
allowing for corrective cardiac surgery to be delayed 
beyond the neonatal period (84). By delaying corrective 
repair, the patient may gain weight and become a more 
robust candidate for cardiac surgery. We have applied this 
to other vertical vein scenarios and this represents another 
area where we have shifted care paradigms (85). We have 
described our early experience with the use of this technique 
in TAPVR in a case series which we intend to report later 
this year.

Absence of outflow tract obstruction
In SVs without outflow tract obstruction, their pathway is 
determined by the status of their PBF (Figure 7). In patients 
with elevated PBF , they receive PA banding (PAB) to 
protect the pulmonary vascular bed and this technique for 
SVs has been described by Baslaim in 2009 (90). Patients 
with balanced circulation are allowed to convalesce until 
they reach appropriate size for their next stage. Patients 
with decreased PBF in the past received an aortopulmonary 
shunt which was either a central shunt or mBT shunt. We 
have moved away from this strategy towards the use of 
PDAS which has been described earlier in this manuscript. 
Nearly all our SV patients in this cohort receive PDAS and 
like our other SVs are followed by our HMP program. 
Stage 2 and the bi-directional Glenn procedure
Although the bi-directional Glenn is known as the stage 2 
procedure in the SV pathway following a stage 1 Norwood 
procedure, its origins date back to the 1950s when William 
Glenn described the unidirectional Glenn (91). The first 
report of the BDG followed years later in 1961 and is 
the template for how we construct our cavo-pulmonary 
connection (92). T ypically stage 2 conversions are completed 
at our center following stage 1 after PVR has fallen and 
patients have grown to roughly 6 kg. Patients receive pre-
Glenn catheterization to assess hemodynamics and anatomy 
before a multi-disciplinary review to move forward. Our use 
of MRI pre-Glenn has been discussed previously. Patients 
receiving PAB, those with balanced circulation or PDAS are 
advanced to Glenn operations utilizing the same assessment 
tools. In patients who have had previous procedures such as 
a Norwood or APS, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is used 
to facilitate the construction of the Glenn. In our patients 
with PDAS or balanced circulation, since the mediastinum 
remains virgin territory, it is possible to utilize techniques 
to construct the Glenn without CPB which is a strategy we 
have moved towards as our preferred approach. 
Off-pump Glenn
A recent change (within the past 6 years) at our institution 
is the use of OPG procedures. The technique has been 
described in the past by authors dating back as early as the 
1950s (91,93). There are also contemporary reports of use 
(94,95). LaPar and colleagues compared OPG techniques and 
identified no differences in mortality or complications (96).  
One of the attractive elements however of OPG is a 
reduction in resource utilization as well as avoidance of 
the inflammatory effects of CPB. This was the rationale 
for our adoption of the technique and our use of PDAS 
helped facilitate this transition. The absence of mediastinal 
adhesions with PDAS use allows for expeditious and safe 
isolation of necessary vessels. PDAS is discussed separately. 
Our approach to OPG has been with the use of a right 
atrium to SVC shunt along with neural clips to control the 
pulmonary arteries ( Figure 8). Vitals and NIRS monitoring 
ensure stable perfusion/cerebral perfusion with a plan to 
readily convert to CPB in the setting of hemodynamic 
instability. Once the cavo-pulmonary anastomosis is 
complete, the PDAS can be isolated with neural clips 
and removed ( Figure 9 ). Once removed, the artery can be 
reconstructed primarily or with patch material if necessary. 
In terms of recent surgical strategies, this has been our most 
successful change in the past 6 years with respect to surgical 
procedures performed in SV patients. We have observed 
a reduction in intubation time, ICU stays, and hospital 
length of stay with this technique and have reported these  
results (81). This technique has paid dividends in many

Conclusion
Throughout the spectrum of SV surgical procedures, there 
remains much to be improved upon. Given time, a fraction 
of SV palliated patients deteriorate during their stepwise 
journey, or during their life post Fontan reconstruction. 
This attrition has largely been unchanged with time and 
represents the underlying weakness of SV physiology. Our 
strategy (similar to our colleagues) in these settings has 
been to rely on mechanical support and transplantation 
techniques as an off-ramp and endpoint in therapy. We 
have relied on literature and experience to guide our

Advanced support techniques: ECMO-
mechanical support-transplantation
Introduction
Medical management for children with SV physiology has 
its limitations. Eventually, disease progression can lead to 
the failure of conventional medical strategies requiring more 
aggressive intervention. Given the anatomical complexity 
and physical size of these patients, transplantation represents 
a viable option for long term success as it removes the 
physiologic nuances associated with SV circulation. Cardiac 
allografts show somatic growth and offer an improvement 
in quality of life that comes with balanced circulation. The 
tradeoff with this strategy is the lifelong risk of infection, 
rejection and neoplasm that comes with transplantation (101).  
Discussing transplantation in the current era requires 
assessment of the entire umbrella ( Figure 7 ) of therapies 
that mechanical support and transplant provide over the 
SV pathway. In the most acute settings, ECMO offers an 
immediate solution and VADs are more appropriate for non-
emergent situations given the speed of deployment. This 
collection of strategies may be considered an off-ramp for SV 
palliation at any stage. Utilizing this umbrella and knowing 
which modality is appropriate at each setting is both an art 
and science and each are discussed here in more detail. 
ECMO
ECMO support is uncommon in the SV population but 
presents unique challenges when needed. Overall, ECMO 
utilization has been increasing over the past few decades (102).  
Understanding the physiology and risks are crucial to 
achieving safe support and many of the principles learned are 
directly applicable to longer-term device therapy. Anatomic 
heterogenicity in the SV population has required the need for 
different cannulation configurations to secure support. Veno-
venous (VV)-ECMO is typically implemented in patients 
with adequate venous access with a single dual-lumen cannula 
or multiple venous cannulas (103). Veno-arterial (VA)-ECMO 
requires more thoughtfulness depending on the anatomic 
variant and the stage of palliation. 
Flows
Mixing between the pulmonary and systemic circulations 
requires consideration and as a result, target flows may need 
to be adjusted. Collateral burdens can also play a role in 
determining adequate device flow. T ypically, cannulation 
will be central or peripheral or a combination of this. Often 
higher flows can be achieved with central cannulation as 
larger cannulas can be placed in the atrium or aorta. T eams 
also need to be cognizant of the possibility of pulmonary 
over circulation in the setting of shunt dependent 
physiology (104). Concern regarding flow through APS has 
led to some programs to clip shunts on ECMO while others 
leave them open and there have been published differences 
in outcome dependent on this decision (105).
In terms of local practice in achieving adequate support 
for this patient population, there are differences in flow 
targets. For our neonates/infants who are biventricular, 
we target flows of 110–150 cc/kg/min. We adjust flows 
for normalization of blood gases, lactate clearance, central 
venous saturation >65%, NIRS >40% and general markers 
of good perfusion. If we anticipate or encounter collateral 
burdens/shunts, we will titrate flows up accordingly. For our 
SVs, we have required flows closer to 150–200 cc/kg/min to 
meet metabolic demands and maintain perfusion according 
to the stated criteria above which is consistent with reported 
literature (104,105). When patients are advanced to stage 
2 or stage 3 palliation, we continue to use the same criteria 
and will titrate flows accordingly.

Thrombosis and bleeding
Thrombosis and bleeding are concerns in the SV population 
that can be challenging to manage and are linked to 
significant morbidity and mortality (106). Anticoagulation 
needs to be closely followed as this population is susceptible 
to thrombosis throughout all stages of palliation (107). 
Patients with synthetic grafts are also uniquely susceptible 
to thrombus formation with thrombotic complications 
reported in up to 17% of mBT shunt patients after S1P (107).  
Thrombus formation in a surgically placed shunt may not be 
easily detected and can complicate separation attempts from 
ECMO or care after ECMO. Physiologically, the disruption 
in normal coagulation can be related to abnormalities in 
levels of procoagulants and other expressed proteins and this 
has previously been described (108,109). Conversely, bleeding 
is also predicative of mortality with events observed in 86% 
of mortalities described on autopsy after extracorporeal life 
support (ECLS) (110).
These factors at least in part contribute to the high 
morbidity and mortality observed with ECMO across the 
SV population which has been reported as >50% (111). 
ECMO support duration has also been demonstrated 
to be inversely related to survival in an Extracorporeal 
Life Support Organization (ELSO) registry study where 
SV patients who required support >14 days had survival 
reported as 10% (112).
ECMO before surgery
When we begin to parse the data in terms of ECMO 
timing in the palliative care pathway, there are few studies 
evaluating ECMO use as a bridge to decision in the SV 
population. In a Boston study evaluating ECMO as a 
bridge to surgery in the general CHD population, 6 of 9 
SV patients survived to palliation and subsequent discharge 
and the 3 who died had HLHS (113). Of these 3 patients, 
2 had an intact atrial septum and one had a cystic adenoid 
malformation with all three requiring ECMO. This 
suggests that the underlying anatomic substrate whether 
it is an intact atrial septum or another factor is associated 
with outcome as opposed to simply defining these patients 
as high risk due to SV physiology. When considering 
EMCO use in SV CHD, ECMO remains a powerful tool in 
supporting patients to discharge survival (113).
Stage 1
With a focus on HLHS patients who undergo palliation 
using the traditional Norwood pathway, stage 1 is regarded 
as the most significant surgical intervention given its 
long cross-clamp times and subsequent ischemia. In this 
population, ECMO is again predictive of poor outcome 
with some programs reporting survival to discharge at 35% 
and 1 year survival at 25% post ECMO (114). Common 
reasons for support include failure to wean from bypass, 
hypoxia and low cardiac output. Successful weaning by 
others has been reported in up to 78% and in one report, 
56% were discharged home post weaning (115). For patients 
who undergo a hybrid Norwood strategy, results are poor 
with survival to discharge reported as low as 16% in those 
requiring ECMO (116). Despite the high morbidity/
mortality, to achieve our current results in SV palliation, 
access and proficiency in ECMO implementation is a 
necessity and an important part of our care algorithm (117).
Stage 2
During stage 2 support with BDG circulation, the SVC flow 
is functionally separated from the inferior vena cava (IVC) 
flow and is uniquely sensitive to PVR. T o achieve adequate 
venous drainage, cannulation of the SVC may be necessary 
along with the common atrium or IVC. BDG circulation 
is susceptible to venous obstruction, PVR perturbations, 
Glenn obstructions/stenosis and atrial level restriction. 
If a patient’s Qp:Qs ratio remains low, single venous 
cannulation in the IVC or common atrium is sufficient. 
With increasing Qp/Qs, an SVC cannula may be indicated 
to accommodate increased PBF . Mortality after ECMO use 
in BDG physiology remains high and has been reported at 
41% (118), but may improve with experience (119). Close 
attention to neurologic sequalae is needed, as the incidence 
of neurologic injury in this population remains high as well. 
Stage 3
Cannulation strategies after stage 3 (Fontan) palliation are 
somewhat normalized. Assuming patency of the Fontan 
conduit, venous cannulation can be obtained from above 
(SVC) or below (IVC). If VA-ECMO is implemented, 
arterial access can be secured in any of the traditional 
access points assuming vessel patency. As these patients 
age, access becomes more important, and it is possible to 
progressively lose vessels/vascular access due to repeated 
interventions and diagnostics in this population. With the 
pulmonary and systemic circulations in series, blood return 
to the pulmonary circulation remains a passive process

overall prognosis for a patient is likely dependent on the 
mechanism of failure. Results in patients requiring ECMO 
who are labelled “Fontan failure” remain poor. In one large 
series, Fontan survival to decannulation and discharge was

adequate flow will be dependent on patient factors such 
as the level of venous decompression and the degree of 
aorto-pulmonary (AP) collateral flow which increases 
with time. With that said, ECMO remains a viable option 
in supporting Fontan patients with outcomes dependent 
on underlying physiology more than the technology and 
techniques implemented. 
V ADs—durable mechanical support
Introduction
For SV patients requiring circulatory support while awaiting 
transplantation, aside from ECMO, we can consider its 
more durable cousin, VADs, assuming a non-emergent 
deployment scenario. When deploying VAD therapy in an 
SV we refer to this as a single ventricle assist device (SVAD). 
The typical duration of VAD support is weeks to months 
and continues until transplantation is possible. In rare cases, 
duration of support has been several years with one report 
of 2,100 days of support in a biventricular system (121). 
As device safety and utilization improves, longer support 
times for those receiving VADs is expected, similar to adults 
receiving destination therapy. The use of VAD therapy 
pre-transplant has not altered post-transplant results 
unlike the use of ECMO which is strongly associated with 
mortality when utilized pre-transplant (122). Although this 
study by Edelson and colleagues refers to pediatric heart 
transplantation in general, CHD and SVs are included in 
the study population (122). VAD utilization has also been 
shown to improve wait list mortality in the United Network 
Organ Sharing (UNOS) database (123). 
Strategies
Device therapy can come in many forms with respect 
to VAD implantation. The mechanism of support can 
be a continuous flow device such as with PediMag™ 
(Pleasanton, USA) or pulsatile device such as a Berlin

placement of an outflow graft in either the Damus-Kaye-

V AD at stage 2
When planning VAD insertion in a BDG configuration, 
the cavo-pulmonary connection can create additional issues 
that need to be addressed. Cannulation of the common 
atrium and aorta for inflow and outflow can be completed 
in similar fashion to that of stage 1 patients (128), but 
this may not provide adequate venous drainage as SVC 
flow is directed to the pulmonary arteries. Venous-venous 
collateral development can promote cyanosis which is 
problematic. Stanford has reported survival of 29% in a 
series of 7 patients who were supported with BDG’s in 
a similar fashion (132). With cyanosis being a concern, 
some have suggested utilizing a strategy of Glenn take-
down to a shunt or Fontan completion during VAD 
insertion to improve VAD support and promote transplant  
candidacy (133). Overall, acute or chronic Glenn failure 
is associated with poor outcome and the ideal solution for 
support to transplant remains unknown despite growing 
experience with VADs in this subset of SV patients. 
V AD at stage 3
Device support of a patient with Fontan physiology can 
in some cases be simpler than patients post S1P or S2P . 
With separation of the systemic and pulmonary circulations 
(aside from a fenestration or collateral flow), Qp/Qs is 
less of a concern. Collateral burdens remain an issue, but 
this can be managed with catheterization lab intervention 
and through direct collateral takedown in the operating 
room. Cannulation of the systemic ventricle for VAD 
inflow and the aorta for outflow allows augmentation of 
cardiac output while providing drainage/decompression of 
the systemic ventricle and thus lowering its end diastolic 
pressure. Patients are larger with more physical space in 
the chest compared to S1P or S2P patients and as a result 
more surgical options are available. It is important however 
to understand the nature of the heart failure and to know 
the difference between systemic ventricular failure and 
right sided (Fontan circuit) failure (134). Right sided failure 
may not benefit from systemic VAD therapy. Different 
configurations have been reported by numerous authors 
(128,135,136). Biventricular configurations have also been 
described and may be useful when it is necessary to augment 
blood flow to the lungs but are beyond the scope of our 
discussion. VAD results in patients post Fontan have yielded 
a wide range of results. An analysis from the Pedimacs and 
Intermacs databases has yielded 81% survival at 6 months 
with the first month of support responsible for the majority 
of mortality in 55 patients (137). In a similar report from 
the ACTION network with 45 Fontan patients, survival 
was 79% at 1 year (138). In the Melbourne experience, 
survival to discharge was 33% in a series of 57 patients 
throughout the entire SV cohort (139). When looking at 
individual stages, survival to discharge was similar for stages 
1, 2, and 3 with reported survival at Stage 3 VAD insertion 
being reported as 40% but with a small sample size of  
Fontans (139). With these results, it is reasonable to 
consider VAD therapy for stage 3 as more complex than in 
biventricular systems but in our current era, it is a viable 
strategy to move patients forward when necessary.

Introduction
Pediatric heart transplantation entered history with the 
first implant a few days after the first adult heart implant 
in 1967 (140). The recipient was an Ebstein’s patient and 
survived for only a few days but demonstrated proof of 
concept for transplantation to treat complex CHD. When 
structural disease cannot be repaired, replacement offers 
the opportunity for physiologic normality. The application 
of pediatric heart transplantation has grown over the 
years with the International Society for Heart and Lung

including other age categories, the number of transplants 
exceeds 10,000. The outcomes associated with these 
operations have been good with median survival in infants 
aged <1 year approaching 22 years and 18 years for patients 
aged 1 to 5 years, respectively (142). As recipients in the 
pediatric cohort ages, there is a decrease in median survival 
but this has been improving over time (141).
Donor availability
T ransplantation is a crossroads for SV patients, regardless of 
stage of palliation, as it presents a new pathway of care and 
as a result, new collection of sequala to monitor and manage 
if successful. Applying transplantation to SV disease is a 
logical step but its application has not been straightforward. 
Its utilization has been limited due to donor availability 
concerns with a pediatric waitlist mortality of 17% for the 
entire pediatric waitlisted cohort, and as high as 30–40% 
for children with CHD (143). SV patients in the Pediatric 
Heart T ransplant Study database were reviewed and had 
a waitlist mortality of 23% (144). The paucity of organs 
has led to transplantation being relegated to an alternative 
option exercised only after other therapeutic means have 
been exhausted. It is most often considered in a setting 
of failure of conventional palliation with contemporary 
literature reporting transplant free survival as a primary 
outcome measure of success. 
ABO incompatibility
One strategy that has helped alleviate donor concerns in the 
infant subgroup is the introduction of ABO incompatible 
heart transplantation in 2001 (145). In patients less 
than 12 months of age, the immature immune system is 
tolerant of ABO incompatibility and can be managed with 
immunosuppressive therapy. The initial series demonstrated 
a reduction of mortality on the wait list from 58% to  
7% (145). In a UNOS database review in 2021, it was noted 
that for the years of 2007–2018, of 2,787 patients listed 
for heart transplantation, over half were listed as ABO 
incompatible (146). They have demonstrated an increase 
in the use of this strategy over time. Monitoring these 
patients’ long term has not revealed any increased incidence 
of rejection which maintains this as a viable strategy.
SVs—challenging candidates
SV patients are challenging transplant candidates and 
typically have higher morbidity and mortality than their 
biventricular counterparts (144,147). The reason for this 
is likely multifactorial and these patients likely have more 
complex co-morbidities as well as have undergone multiple 
cardiac procedures. The implantation can be complicated 
by anatomical issues that may need to be addressed 
intraoperatively (T able 1). 
Re-operative sternotomies which can present as 
challenging dissections requiring additional operative time 
also complicate transplantation. Addressing specific disease 
processes involving the aortic arch, pulmonary arteries 
or pulmonary veins takes additional time and increases 
operative risk. Early cannulation to expedite dissection 
results in longer CPB times and patients may require 
more significant hypothermia to facilitate low flow states 
or even circulatory arrest. On occasion, modification to 
our conventional heart implantation sequence is necessary 
to accommodate the anatomical heterogeneity of the SV 
population depending on the specific lesion (148,149). The 
underlying SV pathology plays a role in outcome, as HLHS 
in itself represents a risk factor for poor outcomes in this 
population (144).
APC burden is also a risk factor for transplant free 
survival as previously noted (66). This risk however extends 
to patients who undergo transplantation as well. The 
collateral load can steal significant cardiac output from 
a newly implanted graft resulting in high-output cardiac 
failure. Krishnan and colleagues demonstrated embolization 
of APCs post-transplantation resulted in improved 
outcomes (150). In patients with Fontan physiology, pre-
operative occlusion of APCs is associated with decreased 
collateral bleeding during transplantation. Our approach 
in this population is based on factors such as the overall 
percentage burden of collaterals, but also our expected

Timing a transplantation referral
Controversy remains as to when the optimal time for 
referral for transplant should be. As patients slowly 
deteriorate and present with increasing failure symptoms, 
transplantation becomes a salvage procedure and is higher 
risk. Knowing how patients are performing requires 
frequent monitoring to assess what interventions are 
most appropriate early on. Waiting for decompensation 
or hospital admission may increase the overall risk to 
patients as opposed to pro-active interventions. HMP has 
been discussed earlier in this manuscript. Although most 
programs utilize outpatient surveillance tools in practice to 
monitor their SV patients, formal standardized techniques 
are still making their way into literature and are early in 
their development (151). With standardized screening/
surveillance processes, we can work towards optimizing 
our timing for transplant referral to ensure it is driven by 
objective data.

Fontan transplantation
With the growing number of Fontan patients now surviving 
into adulthood estimated at >90%, the disease burden of 
Fontan circulation will also grow (156). Unlike patients 
post Norwood or BDG, this physiologic state is not limited 
to months or a few years but represents the endpoint of 
palliation. This endpoint will be for life, if tolerated, or 
until transplantation. Fontan failure manifests in ways that 
are representative of both cardiac disease as well as systemic 
dysfunction. Commonly described manifestations include 
primary cardiac disease such as ventricular dysfunction, 
valvar dysfunction, arrythmias, and dysfunction of the 
Fontan connection itself (157). Other systemic processes 
include kidney, lymphatic and liver dysfunction. The 
progression of patients to liver dysfunction referred to as 
Fontan associated liver disease (FALD) is expected and 
time dependent. Although medical and interventional 
management are logical early management steps, systemic 
disease invariably advances with time. Collaterals, whether 
aorto-pulmonary or venous-venous, are important to 
manage in terms of operative planning (158). Interventions 
represent a cumulative risk in these patients throughout 
palliation and there is a peak in these interventions 
post Fontan (159). Eventually an endpoint is reached 
and an advanced heart failure consultation with cardiac 
transplantation is the next best step (157,160).
Early experience with Fontan transplantation dating back 
to the late 1990s and early 2000s demonstrated survival 
rates as low as 33% at 30 days (161). Although the cohort of 
those transplanted post-Fontan were not large, they had the 
highest mortality compared to patients transplanted at other 
stages of palliation at that point in time. SV patients in this 
same study who were transplanted after BDG construction 
demonstrated 100% survival (161). Contemporary studies 
with larger samples of Fontan transplant patients are now 
reporting 1 year survival between 75% and 89% (162-164). 
Timing does play a role in outcome as patients transplanted 
less than a year from Fontan had significantly reduced 
survival (155). These early conversions likely represent 
patients who fail early and are intrinsically higher risk. 
This suggests transplantation is a reasonable alternative 
to managing Fontan patients who are failing with SV 
physiology and perhaps there is a more optimal time to 
intervene on them to eliminate this difference in outcome. 
Long term, it may be reasonable to say that once a patient 
has achieved a stable Fontan circulation, management 
should focus not only on optimizing the SV circulation but 
planning for success with transplantation. 
Our experience with Fontan transplantation has grown 
with time and we focus on key areas to optimize our chance 
of success. Although timing for referrals may represent 
the most important aspect of this process, when we move 
forward with transplantation, we need to optimize both 
the donor and recipient physiology. Our preference is to 
oversize donors whenever possible. In recipients, we target 
optimizing anatomy for the donor heart whenever possible. 
Arch obstructions are relieved with patching or with an 
extended aortic reconstruction. Pulmonary arteries are 
patched to ensure unobstructed flow to both lungs. Stents 
in the aorta and pulmonary arteries are removed whenever 
possible. The SVC to caval connection is re-established with 
attention to prevention of focal stenosis. Aorto-pulmonary 
collaterals encountered in the operating room are taken 
down aggressively. The most significant challenges we 
have faced in the past in this specific population have been 
related to PA architecture, significant collateral burdens 
and the time it takes to perform these reconstructions. As 
our experience has grown with this population, attention 
to these details has been an important part of the learning 
process and has yielded positive results.

Donation after circulatory death (DCD)
A technique that may help address the donor availability 
problem in pediatric transplantation is the advancement of 
DCD. It’s estimated that with current donor criteria, this 
technology could increase available donor pools by up to 
30% (170). The pediatric population may see an increase 
of up to 25% (171) and adults 17–56% based on reports  
(172-178). Unlike conventional transplantation which relies 
on donation after brain death (DBD), with DCD, the organ 
is procured after the patient dies by conventional circulatory 
death criteria. Though this technology was initially 
employed in small children, it is currently being utilized 
more in adults and efforts have been made to expand this 
into pediatric heart transplantation more extensively. 
The first series of 3 DCD implants at Denver Children’s 
Hospital were performed between 2004 and 2007 (179). 
The adult programs have demonstrated similar survival for 
these organs to traditional DBD over 5 years demonstrating 
its short to mid-term durability (180). Pediatric programs 
have also demonstrated similar survival and ICU stays 
with DCD compared to DBD, though the 10-year survival 
from the initial Denver cohort was only 50% (181). As 
this technology proliferates and experience grows, its 
incorporation into our care pathway may help mitigate the 
ongoing donor pool concerns. 
There are, however, aspects of DCD that differ from 
conventional DBD that make its widespread adoption 
tricky. The identification of good candidates and protocols 
surrounding who can be utilized as a donor needs to be 
addressed (182). The determination of what is considered 
acceptable donors will likely be center specific as with DBD 
donors. Our institution has developed internal criteria 
for DCD acceptance which is still in evolution ( T able 2). 
T ypically, when a candidate is identified, there will be a time 
frame and circumstance for when death is expected. If a 
patient does not fall within that criterion, the procurement 
may be abandoned. Contemporary adult studies have 
reported 66% conversion of potential to actual DCD 
donors, which is similar to pediatrics (183). There exists the 
subset of patients who do not experience circulatory death 
on schedule preventing harvesting as well as those who do 
but are deemed poor grafts after re-perfusion. Messer and 
colleagues discarded 11% of hearts after ex vivo perfusion 
and Chew and colleagues discarded 21% grafts (184,185). 
This unfortunately adds costs to the overall procedure and 
this cost can be compounded by the fact that teams may 
have to be flown to a location remote from the recipient 
for a procurement. It is also worth noting some of the 
disposable equipment costs several tens of thousands of 
dollars (186). When organs are not successfully procured, 
it may be difficult to justify the costs incurred to an 
increasingly budget conscious healthcare system. When 
an organ is procured, explanation is done in an accelerated 
manner given that with circulatory death, the heart is 
subject to ongoing ischemia. Critical steps such as sternal 
entry, and exposure need to be done expeditiously for organ 
preservation. Skills such as this in procurement may take 
time to percolate through the pediatric cardiac surgery 
community. There are 2 basic techniques for organ recovery, 
namely direct procurement (our conventual technique with 
DBD) and regional perfusion where circulation is restored 
to allow for conversion to a DBD scenario (187) (Figure 10).

patients. As our experience grows, we hope to report on this 
in the future.

Xenotransplantation
As a corollary to the use of DCD expansion of the donor 
pool, there are more ambitions approaches from the past 
that have been resurrected. Although often considered to 
be in the realm of science fiction, xenotransplantation offers 
a practical solution to many of our perceived problems. 
The potential for unlimited donors along with reduction 
in travel and ischemic times is attractive. The concept 
however is not new and was reported by Bailey in 1984 at 
Loma Linda (published 1985) after 20 successful days of 
implantation of a baboon heart in an HLHS patient (188). 
Although the patient did not survive, it was successful 
proof of concept despite its poor public reception. Almost  
40 years later, the interest continues with recent work 
in pig to primate studies using cardiac xenografts that 
demonstrated graft survival over 945 days with heterotopic 
implantation (189). This concept has also been furthered 
by the University of Alabama with baboon recipients 
receiving porcine hearts (190). The newer generation 
xenotransplantation efforts have been deploying 
genetically engineered donors as a potential solution to 
the immune intolerance that we currently see with human 
allotransplantation. T echniques similar to this have been 
tested in pig to human transplantation into brain dead 
humans (191). Initial experiments revealed that in the acute 
time frame, there was no evidence of antibody mediated 
rejection or cellular rejection (191). There is still much to 
discover and develop before xenotransplantation is ready for 
humans and added to our care algorithm. For now, looking 
at recent steps forward, we can say with confidence that this 
technology is viable and may someday bridge the deficit we 
have in organ donor pools. 
Conclusion
If we are able expand our donor pool, the impact on the 
SV population will be quantifiable and profound. Perhaps 
a more interesting question to ask is whether increasing 
donor availability will lead to expansion of indications for 
transplantation within SV care. With the large number of 
Fontan survivors, it is conceivable that early transplantation 
may become a recommendation in the future, along the 
entire SV pathway. The answer to an academic question 
such as this may lie in our ability to prove DCD’s cost 
effectiveness. Regardless of the answer, DCD remains a hot 
topic in transplantation and has the potential for significant

in programs but also has become a prime example for

ICU. Delivering high fidelity results requires a life-long 
investment in patient surveillance and care to advance them 
through life. Small and seemingly innocuous technological
