ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy 
of three- port thoracoscopic surgery for patent ductus 
arteriosus (PDA) ligation in children. A retrospective analysis 
was conducted on 50 pediatric patients with PDA (median 
age: 30 months; median weight: 12.00 kg; median ductal 
diameter: 2.50 mm) treated with three- port thoracoscopic 
surgery in the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery at 
Kunming Children’s Hospital from January 2021 to June 
2024. All 50 patients successfully underwent surgery

meticulous handling during ductus arteriosus ligation is 
essential to minimize complications and residual shunts.
INTRODUCTION
Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is one of the 
most common congenital heart diseases, 
with an incidence of approximately 0.3%–
0.8% in term infants and a female- to- male 
predominance of 2:1.1 The clinical and phys-
iological manifestations of PDA depend on 
both the size of the vessel and the relative 
systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance. 
If left untreated, PDA may lead to pulmonary 
overflow, congestive heart failure, pulmo-
nary hypertension, and an increased risk of 
infective endocarditis; this warrants prompt 
intervention on diagnosis. 2 With the rapid 
advances in minimally invasive surgery, 
thoracoscopic PDA ligation has emerged as 
a primary treatment option. This retrospec-
tive study analyzed 50 pediatric patients who 
underwent three- port thoracoscopic PDA 
ligation to evaluate the safety and efficacy 
of this technique. We also provide region- 
specific benchmark data on hospitalization 
costs.
METHOD
Patients
We analyzed the clinical data of patients diag-
nosed with PDA who underwent three- port 
thoracoscopic PDA ligation at the Depart-
ment of Cardiothoracic Surgery, Kunming 
Children’s Hospital, between January 2021 
and June 2024. Inclusion criteria were as 
follows: (1) confirmed PDA diagnosis by 
echocardiography (ductal diameter ≥1.5 and 
≤8 mm); and (2) three- port thoracoscopic 
PDA ligation as the sole surgical interven-
tion. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) 
concomitant major cardiovascular anomalies 
(e.g., complex congenital heart defects or 
pulmonary hypertension) and (2) multiple 
organ dysfunction or systemic conditions that 
contraindicated surgery.
Surgical technique
While under general anesthesia and with 
one- lung ventilation, patients were placed in 
a 90° right lateral decubitus position. A three- 
port thoracoscopic approach was used and 
consisted of a 5 mm trocar inserted at the fifth 
intercostal space of the left midaxillary line 
as the camera port and two additional 5 mm 
trocars placed at the fourth intercostal space 
of the left anterior axillary line and posterior 
axillary lines as working ports. An intraop-
erative pneumothorax pressure was main-
tained at 4–6 mm Hg. The PDA was dissected 
via electrocautery and doubly ligated with 
a silk suture and 1–2 Hem- o- lok clips. After 
the procedure, a chest tube was placed, and 
successful closure was confirmed via bedside 
echocardiography prior to final chest closure.
Data extraction
Patient demographic data, operative time, 
intraoperative blood loss, duration of post-
operative mechanical ventilation, duration of 
chest tube placement, postoperative hospital

RESULTS
Baseline characteristics
Our study included 50 pediatric patients (23 male, 
27 female) with a median admission age of 30 months 
(range: 11 days–13.5 years), a median weight of 12.00 kg 
(range: 2.70–52.00 kg), and a median ductal diameter of 
2.50 mm (range: 1.50–6.40 mm). Concomitant cardiac 
anomalies were observed in 13 patients (12 atrial septal 
defect (ASD) and 1 ventricular septal defect (VSD)). 
The patients with ASD and VSD exhibited no significant 
shunt flow on echocardiography and received no concur-
rent repair due to their age and small defect size.

DISCUSSION
All 50 pediatric patients successfully completed three- port 
thoracoscopic surgery without conversion to open thor -
acotomy or mortality. Postoperative evaluation revealed 
residual shunts in three (6.00%) patients, all of whom 
were in the early phase of the study. Preoperative anal-
ysis revealed that these patients had significantly larger 
ductal diameters, with two patients exceeding the 75th 
percentile (P75) for ductal diameter in our cohort. This 
finding suggests that for patients with larger ductal diam-
eters, (1) intraoperative manipulation requires more 
meticulous techniques and (2) immediate postoperative 
assessment of closure should be intensified. This should 
use transesophageal echocardiography where needed to 
confirm complete closure and minimize residual shunts. 
At our institution, we manage postoperative small residual 
shunts in patients with no hemodynamic significance by 
adopting a conservative approach with regular clinical 
and echocardiographic follow- up. Reintervention is only 
considered in cases where the shunt is accompanied by 
left heart enlargement or other clinical symptoms. The 
available options for reintervention include either percu-
taneous transcatheter closure or repeat thoracoscopic 
ductus arteriosus ligation.
Based on our institutional experience with thoraco-
scopic PDA ligation, the following technical consider -
ations are recommended to optimize surgical outcomes. 
First, surgical exposure is enhanced through strategic 
patient positioning (increased right lateral tilt) and 
selective lung suspension to ensure optimal visualiza-
tion of the ductal region. Second, during the procedure, 
the electrocautery hook should be used with caution, 
maintaining a safe distance from the recurrent laryngeal 
nerve and controlling the extent of coagulation to avoid 
thermal injury to both the nerve and the thoracic duct. 
Third, after complete dissection of the vessel, a double 
closure is achieved by ligating it with a silk suture and 
placing a Hem- o- lok clip. This is especially important for 
cases with large ducts as it can improve the reliability of 
ligation. Fourth, real- time hemodynamic monitoring of 
infants with large PDAs, including post- ligation lower 
extremity blood pressure assessment, helps mitigate 
potential complications. While our current practice 
includes routine chest tube placement to prevent pneu-
mothorax or hydrothorax, recent literature suggests that 
the absence of tube thoracostomy may reduce infection

With respect to advances in medical technology and 
increasing societal demands, minimally invasive tech-
niques have developed rapidly. Indeed, since its first 
reported use in 1991, thoracoscopic PDA ligation has 
undergone continuous technical refinement.4 A compar-
ative study by Stankowski et al. demonstrated that thora-
coscopic PDA ligation offered significant advantages 
over conventional open thoracotomy. These include 
three small incisions of a few millimeters and avoidance 
of the large incisions, rib retraction, and intercostal 
muscle damage associated with traditional surgery. 
This significantly reduces postoperative pain, increases 
patient and parental satisfaction with surgical scars, and 
shortens the duration of intensive care unit stay (ICU stay 
for non- low- birth- weight infants is reduced from 6.3 to 
2.5 days). It also greatly reduced chest drain placement 
(8.6% vs. 89.7%) and overall complication rate (17.1% 
vs. 55.2%). Moreover, thoracoscopic ligation of the PDA 
carries a very low risk of chest wall deformity and offers 
satisfactory therapeutic outcomes for low- birth- weight 
infants.5 Sen et al. reported that thoracoscopic ligation 
produces cosmetically pleasing incisions, rapid postoper-
ative recovery, short hospital stay, and therapeutic effects 
worthy of clinical application and promotion. 6 With the 
maturation of percutaneous transcatheter closure in 
recent years, some authors believe that this technique 
is the preferred method for treating PDA. 7 8 Moreover, 
studies have shown that this procedure can be safely 
performed in both preterm and very- low- birth- weight 
infants.9 However, this procedure has several drawbacks. 
First, during the procedure, there is long- term radia-
tion exposure. Second, the occluder will remain in situ 
long term, which may have an impact on the child and 
their parents both psychologically and in their daily life. 
Although percutaneous transcatheter closure of a PDA 
guided by ultrasound has been performed, the surgical 
technique is highly challenging and uptake is slow. 10 
Moreover, for some special types of PDA not suitable for 
transcatheter closure, surgery remains the only choice.7
With the iterative development of minimally invasive 
techniques, robotic- assisted technology has provided a 
new technical pathway for the treatment of PDA. The first 
report of robotic- assisted ligation of a PDA was published 
in 2002. 11 Compared with conventional thoracoscopic 
surgery, robotic surgery offers several advantages. These 
include enhanced three- dimensional visualization, 
robotic arms that precisely mimic human hand move-
ments with a greater range of motion, and automatic 
tremor filtration to ensure more stable and precise oper-
ation.12 A recent study by Liang et al. has confirmed that 
robotic- assisted thoracoscopic surgery offers advantages 
in common pediatric thoracic diseases. Outcomes such 
as chest tube retention, duration of chest tube drainage, 
and length of hospital stay are all superior to tradi-
tional thoracoscopic surgery. 13 With the application 
and development of this technology, day case manage-
ment can be adopted. Under conditions of similar ther -
apeutic effects, hospitalization time can be reduced. 14 
Parallel, the evolution of robotic- assisted technology is 
progressing toward even less invasiveness and greater 
precision. In the management of select pediatric urolog-
ical conditions, evidence suggests that single- port robotic 
surgery is both safe and feasible, demonstrating compa-
rable perioperative outcomes to multi- port approaches, 
with potential advantages in reducing blood loss and 
shortening hospital stay. 15 To address the challenge of 
limited working space in children, technical modifica-
tions, such as the “single- port plus one” technique, have 
emerged. These approaches maintain safety and effi-
cacy while yielding superior cosmetic results. 16 Looking 
ahead, the adaptation of one- port or two- port robotic- 
assisted surgery for PDA ligation holds promise, poten-
tially offering an ultra- minimally invasive alternative for 
pediatric patients. However, robotic- assisted surgery has 
disadvantages. These include high cost, limitations in 
performing surgery on smaller children, fewer surgeons 
proficient with the technology, and a lack of consensus 
guidelines for some aspects; widespread application 
remains challenging.17 Thoracoscopic surgery continues 
to be the mainstream minimally invasive approach for 
PDA treatment.
This study has limitations. First, this was a single- center 
retrospective study with a small sample size, which affects 
objectivity and generalizability. Second, the range of chil-
dren’s ages and weights was wide. No stratified analysis was 
conducted, making it difficult to assess the differences in 
surgical outcomes among children at different develop-
mental stages. Although subgroup analyses based on age 
and ductal diameter were attempted, significant differ -
ences in baseline characteristics (such as body weight 
and anatomical dimensions) were observed between 
the subgroups, indicating that they were not directly 
comparable. Therefore, statistical comparisons were not 
performed to avoid potential bias. Third, the follow- up 
period for some children was short. At only 6 months, this 
was insufficient to assess long- term complications.Further 
multicenter, large- sample randomized controlled trials 
with long- term follow- up that assess cost- effectiveness are 
required to make evidence- based decisions.
In summary, this study confirmed the safety and effi-
cacy of three- port thoracoscopic ligation of the PDA in 
children. This surgical approach is minimally invasive, 
has a significant therapeutic effect, and has a low compli-
cation rate. This represents an essential treatment option 
for children with PDA. Moreover, with the application of 
emerging technologies, this minimally invasive treatment 
system for PDA will continue to improve. This technique 
has the potential to provide personalized treatment for 
children.
