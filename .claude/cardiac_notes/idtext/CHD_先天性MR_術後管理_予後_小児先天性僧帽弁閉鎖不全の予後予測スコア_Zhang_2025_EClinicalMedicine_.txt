Summary
Background Paediatric patients who underwent surgery for mitral regurgitation (MR) have a high risk of recurrence 
or death; however, no prediction tool has been developed to risk-stratify this challenging subpopulation.
Methods In this multicentre cohort study, paediatric patients undergoing surgery for congenital MR in Shanghai 
Children’s Medical Center in January 1st, 2009–December 31st, 2022 were included for analysis while those had a 
combination with infective endocarditis, anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery, rheumatic 
valvular disease, connective tissue disease, or single ventricle were excluded. A Cox regression model predictive of 
the primary outcome (a composite of mortality or mitral valve [MV] re-operation) was derived and converted to a 
point-based risk score. This score was externally validated in a cohort of patients undergoing MR surgeries 
between January 1st, 2009 and December, 31st, 2022 in eight independent hospitals in China. The Harrell’s c 
index and Hosmer–Lemeshow test was used to quantify the discrimination and calibration of the risk score.
Findings In total, 2640 patients (female: 57% [n = 1505]) with a median age of 0.99 (IQR, 0.47–2.60) years were 
included. The primary outcome occurred in 262 patients (16.6%) over a median of 5.7-year follow-up in the 
derivation cohort (n = 1581; median age of 1.03 years [IQR, 0.47–2.67]) and in 130 (12.3%) over a median of 7.1- 
year follow-up in the validation cohort (n = 1059; median age of 0.93 years [IQR, 0.46–2.51]). The score variables 
included preoperative variables (age, the presence of primary MR, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide, left 
ventricular ejection fraction, and left atrium z score), perioperative changes in z scores of LA and left ventricular 
end-diastolic dimension, and the procedural variable (use of MV annuloplasty or not). This risk score, ranging 
from −22 to 10, yielded strong discrimination (Harrell’s c index: 0.85, 95% CI, 0.83–0.87) and exhibited good 
calibration of postoperative 10-year primary outcome (Hosmer–Lemeshow; χ
2 
= 9.85; P = 0.33) in the derivation 
cohort, which maintained in the validation cohort (Harrell’s c index: 0.86, 95% CI, 0.83–0.89; Hosmer– 
Lemeshow; χ
2 
= 23.80; P = 0.64). In addition, a simplified stratification of the score model (low-risk: −22 to −5, 
intermediate risk: −4 to 0, high risk: >0) showed good performances in predicting the primary outcome in both 
derivation and validation ( P s < 0.001).

Introduction
Recurrent significant mitral incompetence after surgi -
cal repair of paediatric mitral regurgitation (MR) is not 
negligible, with approximately 50% of the patients 
requiring reoperations within 10 years, which accu -
mulates to an underlying burden and participating in 
increased morbidity and cardiovascular risks.
1,2 
Identi -
fying patients who are at high risk of developing 
recurrent MR is therefore crucial in tailoring manage -
ment and may allow timely intervention to prevent 
irreversible cardiac remodelling.
3 
Congenital MR, either 
in isolation or combined with other congenital heart 
defects, is the most common aetiology of paediatric 
MR.
4 
Individualised risk estimation in this subpopula -
tion is challenging due on the one hand to the wide 
range of pathologies affecting leaflets and subvalvar 
elements of the MV, and on the other hand to the weak 
stratification capabilities of MR severity graded by 
echocardiogram.
5
Optimisation of risk assessment has been recom -
mended in guidelines
6,7 
as a fundamental step in the 
clinical-decision process in the MR setting. However, data 
specifically focused on the paediatric patients is very scarce 
largely because of the disease rarity and lack of studies 
involving enough sample size to explore the relevant 
prognostic factors. As such, this subpopulation is under -
represented in the current guidelines. There have been 
few attempts to develop a specific risk score model for 
paediatric MR but the model is derived from a single- 
center study and does not differentiate between mitral 
stenosis and regurgitation.
1 
Moreover, it has not been 
validated in a separate and independent cohort, precluding 
the clinical applicability to a broader patient population.
We designed this study to try to overcome the 
aforementioned limitations. The primary aim of this 
study was to explore the predictors of mortality and MV 
re-operation, in a nationwide multicenter cohort of 
paediatric patients with congenital MR, and further 
quantify their impacts to propose a risk score to 
enhance stratification, whilst assessing whether poste -
rior annuloplasty is an effective method to improve 
repair durability.
Research in context
Evidence before this study
We searched PubMed and Web of Science for articles 
published in English using the keywords “MR”, “paediatric”, 
“risk factors”, “MV surgery” from January 1, 2004 to January 
31, 2024. 44 studies explored risk factors in paediatric 
congenital MR, among which, 10 were multi-centred. These 
studies mainly focused on different etiologies, surgical 
techniques or clinical features, revealing younger age at 
surgery , emergency surgery , left ventricular outflow 
obstruction, genetic syndromes, lower ejection fraction, and 
concomitant surgery , etc. were associated with higher risks of 
post- operative mortality or recurrence of MR needing re- 
intervention of MV, while none of them carried out an 
integrated score system with good discrimination power in 
predicting surgical outcomes in such population. Therefore, 
the extent to which variables could be conducted in risk 
stratification in children undergoing MR surgery remains 
unclear .
Added value of this study
We have developed and validated the first prediction model 
in the paediatric MR setting, to our knowledge, that 
facilitated shared decision-making and allowed for 
individualised treatment pathway . Contemporarily , freedom 
from death or reoperation for recurrent MR in paediatric 
patients was 79.6% at postoperative 10 years. This point- 
based risk score incorporating 8 clinical, echocardiographic, 
and laboratory parameters was superior to the grading of MR 
severity by echocardiogram, which demonstrated better 
discrimination for outcomes after surgery for paediatric MR.
Implications of all the available evidence
The novel scoring system is useful in optimising risk 
stratification, supporting shared decision-making process, 
and tailoring counselling in the paediatric patients with MR. 
Further evidence from large prospective studies or registries 
is needed to confirm our findings.

Methods
Study design
PaediatRIc Mitral vAlve Regurgitation registrY (PRI -
MARY) was initiated by Shanghai Children’s Medical 
Center (SCMC) in March 2018. This is an ongoing pro -
spective, multicenter, observational registry conducted in 
China, aiming to provide contemporary data on the clin -
ical profiles and prognosis of patients (<18 years of age) 
with a diagnosis of MR who were referred for surgery.
Participant and setting
For the present study, outcomes were analysed for pa -
tients with congenital MR referred for surgery at 9 
tertiary hospitals between January 2009 and December 
2022. Prospective inclusion of patients has commenced 
since March 2018, and cases were retrospectively 
collected before that time point. We specifically exclude 
patients who had MR in combination with infective 
endocarditis, anomalous left coronary artery from the 
pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), rheumatic valvular dis -
ease, connective tissue disease, or single ventricle 
because these patients may develop adverse outcomes 
resulting from other comorbidities besides MR. For 
example, there is a likelihood that postoperative death 
in patients with ALCAPA may result from the severe 
myocardial infarction/ischemia.
This study adhered to the TRIPOD guideline
8 
wherein an external validation should use participant 
data collected by other investigators in other hospitals 
(geographically different from the included hospital in 
derivation cohort). Thus, the data from SCMC defined 
the derivation cohort in this analysis and data from the 
other 8 sites (Supplemental Table S1) was evaluated as 
an external validation cohort and not included in the 
process of risk score development. Ethical approval 
from the local ethics committees were obtained from all 
participating centres prior to joining the registry. 
Written informed consent was obtained from the pa -
tient’s parents or caregivers. Ethics approval was ob -
tained for this study from Shanghai Children’s Medical 
Centre review board (SCMCIRB-K2021096-1).
Data collection and MR aetiology
Data were collected independently by each of the 
participating centres using their local electronic data -
bases, including patient demographic information (age, 
sex, body surface area), preoperative N-terminal pro- 
brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), associated 
congenital heart defects (CHDs), surgical notes, peri -
operative information (rhythm disturbance, complete 
atrioventricular block, haemorrhage, low cardiac 
output, length of hospital stay), echocardiographic pa -
rameters at the index hospitalisation (preoperative and 
predischarge) as well as at the last follow-up. NT- 
proBNP was routinely tested in a structured way using 
Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany.
9 
SCMC 
served as the data coordinating center. To ensure the 
completeness and accuracy of the data, the department 
of clinical research in SCMC kept regular telephone 
contact with the investigators and performed central -
ised verification of the electronic case report forms. In 
addition, comprehensive data audits were performed in 
randomly selected sites.
Congenital MR was subcategorized into type-I: val -
var (annular or leaflet abnormality), type-II: subvalvar 
(chordae tendineae or papillary muscle abnormality), 
and type-III: mixed aetiology (a combination of valvar, 
leaflet, and subvalvar abnormality) based on a com -
bined evaluation of preoperative echocardiography and 
operative inspection.
10 
Specifically, atrioventricular 
septal defect (AVSD) was considered as type-I MR 
considering the deformed left component of the atrio -
ventricular valve as well as the leaflet abnormality (e.g., 
cleft and bridging leaflet).
Echocardiographic measurements
All of the echocardiographic imaging was transmitted 
to the core laboratory of SCMC, and were reanalysed by 
a single expert investigator (W.L.P) who was blinded to 
the clinical outcomes.
Severity of MR was graded as mild, moderate, and 
severe using an integrative approach including quanti -
tative, semi-quantitative, and qualitative measure -
ments.
11 
Baseline evaluation of MR included quantitative 
(effective regurgitant orifice area and regurgitant volume 
by proximal isovelocity surface area method) and semi- 
quantitative (vena contracta width). Left ventricular 
(LV) size and function were described by LV end- 
diastolic dimension (LVEDD; parasternal view), LV 
end-systolic dimension (LVESD; parasternal view), and 
LV ejection fraction (LVEF). Left atrial (LA) size was 
described by LA diameter (LAD; major-axis length from 
apical 4-chamer view at ventricular end-systole). Evalua -
tion of the atrioventricular valves included lateral mitral 
and tricuspid valve diameter (apical 4-chamber view at 
cardiac diastole), mitral inflow velocity, and tricuspid 
regurgitation jet peak velocity.
Outcomes and follow-up
The primary composite outcome was mortality or 
mitral valve (MV) re-operation. We separately examined 
the outcomes of death, MV re-operation (including re- 
repair and MV replacement [MVR]), and subsequent 
MVR after failure of the index repair. Follow-up started 
on the date of the initial operation and ended at the 
time of primary endpoints or January, 2024, whichever 
occurred first. Data from those who lost follow-up were 
censored at their last follow-up visit.
Statistics
R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) 
was used for statistical analysis and figures construction. 
All tests were two-tailed, and significant level was set as 
P < 0.05.

mean value, usually recognised as the thresholds for

13 
The Harrell’s c 
index and time-dependent area under the receiver 
operating characteristic curve (AUC) were used to 
assess the predictive performance of the model. A 
Harrell’s c index of >0.7 was considered acceptable for a 
sufficient model fit. The calibration of the model was 
evaluated by the calibration curve
14 
at different time 
points (postoperative 1, 5, and 10 years) and Hosmer– 
Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. The primary outcome 
of MV reoperation or death was plotted on Kaplan– 
Meier curves and between-group comparisons were 
made using the log-rank test. Considering that death 
has a competing risk with MR re-operation (including 
replacement), Nelson–Aalon cumulative risk curve an -
alyses were used to estimate incidence of MR re- 
operation as well as mitral valve replacement, and 
between-group comparisons were made using Gray’s 
test. Short-term prediction of the primary outcome was 
made within a prespecified time window at the land -
mark time point (postoperative 3 months).
15 
A stratified 
bootstrapping method
16 
with 1000 iterations was used 
to partially address the issue regarding heterogeneity in 
hospital sizes among the participating centres. A com -
bination of the derivation and validation cohort was 
used to further assess the discrimination and calibra -
tion of the final risk score model; meanwhile, we eval -
uated the incidence of primary outcomes in the entire 
cohort of patients stratified by the presence of AVSD 
and age <1 year vs. ≥ 1 year. Missing data of NT-pro- 
BNP was found in 2.7% of the patients in earlier era 
and multiple imputation was used.
Model derivation
Univariable Cox regression analysis was used to iden -
tify the predictors of mortality or reoperation. The 
candidate variables included in the univariable analysis 
were derived from a review of the literatures
17–19 
or 
regarded as clinically relevant. Variable with P < 0.1 in 
univariable analysis were included in the multivariable 
analysis using Cox backward stepwise multiple model -
ling. All hazard ratios (HRs) for the continuous vari -

Risk score
To further support clinical use of the final model, we 
created a simple, easy-to-use, integer risk score using 
the method described by Sullivan and colleagues.
20 
Detailed process was shown in the Supplemental 
Method Material . Briefly, continuous variables were 
converted into category variables wherein several cate -
gories were used to create ordinal variables for the 
continuous linear predictors while restricted cubic 
spline with five knots at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th, 
and 95th percentiles was used to determine the optimal 
categorisation thresholds in continuous non-linear 
predictors. Thus, for each predictor, a point of 0 was 
assigned to the reference category and the regression 
coefficients of other categories were then scaled and 
rounded to the nearest integer. Subsequently, a risk 
score for each patient can be calculated by summing the 
risk points of all predictors. Thereafter, patients were 
stratified into quartiles on the basis of their risk scores, 
and risk categories were then defined as low-risk 
(quartile 1), intermediate-risk (quartile 2–3), and high- 
risk groups (quartile 4).
21 
Moreover, to optimise the 
scoring system, we compared the risk score derived 
from data-driven cut points versus clinically meaning -
ful cut points.

thrive (36.4%), and 637 with dyspnoea (24.1%), while 
thirty-five percent of the patients (n = 927) were 
asymptomatic at the initial surgery. Approximately,

variables. Model 3 comprised 5 predictive variables 
(Harrell’s c index > 0.6). Among the three models, 
model 1 showed better discrimination than model 2 
(Harrell’s c index: 0.93, 95% CI, 0.91–0.94 vs. 0.92, 95% 
CI, 0.90–0.93; P = 0.0042) and model 3 (Harrell’s c in -
dex: 0.93, 95% CI, 0.91–0.94 vs. 0.80, 95% CI, 0.78–0.83; 
P < 0.001). The eight variables in model 1 included six 
continuous variables, among which preoperative LA z 
score had a non-linear relationship with the outcomes 
while the other 5 (age at operation, preoperative NT- 
proBNP and LVEF, perioperative changes in z scores 
of the LVEDD [ΔLVEDD z score], and perioperative 
changes in z scores of the [ΔLA z score]) had linear 
relationship with the outcomes (Supplemental 
Figure S2).
Optimisation and assessment of the risk score
The eight variables in model 1 were used to generate 
the risk score (Table 4). Considering that clinically 
meaningful cutpoints of NT-proBNP (>0.45 ng/ml) and 
LVEF (<50%) have been well established in the current 
guidelines,
6,7 
we subsequently developed two risk scores 
derived from data-driven cut points versus clinically 
meaningful cutpoints. The risk score derived from data- 
driven cut points showed a satisfactory prognostic value 
in predicting the primary outcome during the follow-up 
period (Fig. 2), outperforming that derived from the 
clinically meaningful cut points (Harrell’s c index: 0.85 
[95% CI, 0.83–0.87] vs. 0.74 [95% CI, 0.70–0.78]; 
Supplemental Figure S3). Calibration curves demon -
strated adequate agreement between actual and pre -
dicted probabilities of primary outcomes at 
postoperative 1, 5, and 10 years (Supplemental 
Figure S4). Based on the distribution of patients’ risk 
score, the scoring system was categorised into three 
groups: low-risk group (−22 to −5 points), intermediate- 
risk group (−4 to 0 points), and high-risk group (>0 
points). There were significant between-group differ -
ences in the primary outcome when stratified by 
different risk levels (low, intermediate, high; 
Supplemental Figure S5).

Comparison between derivation and validation 
cohort
The derivation and validation cohort were consisted of 
1581 and 1059 patients, respectively. The distribution 
of demographic and procedural characteristics was 
similar between the 2 cohorts except for larger pre -
operative LVEDD ( P < 0.0001), and a higher prevalence 
of primary MR ( P = 0.0051) in the validation cohort 
( Table 1 ). There was no difference in the primary 
outcomes in the patients with primary MR between 
the derivation and validation cohort ( Supplemental 
Figure S9 ).
Sensitivity and subgroup analyses
We combined derivation and validation cohorts to 
further increase the sample size in order to facilitate 
additional analyses. In the entire cohort, a total of 392 
patients (death: 162; MV reoperation: 307) had primary 
outcome, among whom 77 patients died after MV re- 
operation. Time-to-event primary outcome as well as 
each secondary outcome was plotted in Kaplan–Meier 
or Nelson–Aalon curve as appropriate ( Supplemental 
Figure S10 ). The scoring system had superiority in 
predicting death or MV reoperation compared to MR 
severity ( Supplemental Figure S11 ). Landmark analysis 
showed no difference in the primary outcome within 3 
months post index operation among the low-, inter -
mediate-, and high-risk groups ( Supplemental 
Figure S12 ). There was no difference in the primary 
outcomes in patients stratified by the presence of AVSD 
or not and age < 1 year vs. ≥ 1 year ( Supplemental 
Figure S13 ).
Our scoring system also showed good discrimina -
tion for secondary outcomes of death, MV re-operation, 
and MVR after failure of the index repair 
( Supplemental Figure S14 ). Subgroup analysis was 
performed according to the adopted repair techniques, 
AVSD vs. non-AVSD, and primary vs. non-primary MR, 
and the risk score continued to exhibit high predictive 
performance within each subgroup ( Supplemental 
Figures S15–S17 ).
Discussion
To our knowledge, PRIMARY is the largest multicenter 
study of paediatric patients receiving MR surgery. The 
present work documents follow-up outcomes up to 14 
years and is a necessary first step towards developing a 
paediatric-specific score system to predict adverse out -
comes. Our findings highlight that reoperation for 
recurrent significant MR is not uncommon and in -
creases with time. We demonstrate that trough inte -
gration of clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory 
features, it is possible to precisely provide prognostic 
information regarding MV reoperations and mortality, 
which may be useful for optimisation of individualised 
risk evaluation.

One strength of this study is that we first developed 
and externally validated an integrated risk score to 
predicate MR re-operation or mortality. Despite that 
recent guidelines for MR in general recommend opti -
mising risk assessments for improved prognostication, 
there has been no attempt at deriving and validating a 
risk score in the paediatric setting probably because the 
availability of such a prediction score warrants large co -
horts of patients with comprehensive clinical data. The 
present work overcomes this limitation and relies on 
multi-institutional collaboration to obtain separate co -
horts for the derivation and validation process. Moreover, 
development of this data-driven scoring model corre -
sponds to the congenital heart community that un -
derscores the priority to find suitable criteria for 
paediatric MR assessment because some clinically 
meaningful cut points described in the guidelines are 
largely derived from the adult MR population and 
whether they are suitable for the paediatric patients re -
mains unclear.
5 
However, we should clarify that this 
scoring model is a useful method in stratifying patients 
at risk of the primary outcomes during a relatively late 
follow-up period (<1 year postoperatively) but not at very 
early postoperative period (<3 months postoperatively).

Knowledge gaps remain in predicting occurrence of 
adverse complications after MR intervention as cardiac 
remodelling response to volume overload can vary 
significantly among individuals, which has an impact 
on outcomes and can be further complicated by pa -
tient age, mechanism of MR, and associated comor -
bidities.
1 , 22 , 23 
This risk score model, which integrated 8 
clinical, echocardiographic, and laboratory parameters, 
allowed for an improved discrimination and calibra -
tion, outperforming MR severity based on echocardi -
ography. Our scoring system helps to describe 
complex disease patterns with simple explanations and 
appears to be a useful mean to transfer and interpret 
clinical data, which has implication for providing in -
formation to surgeons, cardiologists, and patients 
regarding personalised risk prediction and tailoring 
management, and facilitating the shared decision- 
making process which has been increasingly regar -
ded as a critical component of high-quality and value- 
based care.
24
Plausible speculations for the significant difference 
in the primary outcome between the derivation and 
validation cohort warrants discussion. First, only one 
hospital constituted the derivation cohort while other 8 
hospitals constituted the validation hospital. And the 
patient number of each hospital in the validation cohort 
was far less than that of the hospital in the derivation 
cohort. Hence, there may be a case-by-case bias because 
paediatric MR surgery is complicated and requires 
individualised strategy. Second, there may be variation 
in management protocols and surgical techniques in 
different hospitals. Third, it should be clarified that the 
significant differences in baseline characteristics (pre -
operative LVEDD and primary MR) between the 2 co -
horts might less contribute to the outcome difference 
because (1) preoperative LVEDD was not identified as a 
risk factor, and this may be attributed to the fact that 
reverse LV remodelling could occur after operation
QT
; 
(2) a comparative analysis in patients with primary MR 
between the two cohorts revealed no difference in the

acknowledge that such discrepancy in outcome between 
the derivation and validation cohort may result in an 
overestimation of the predictive performance of the 
scoring model.
In general opinion, patients with AVSD are known 
to have an increased risk of reoperation for left atrio -
ventricular valve and this rate probably increase with 
time.
26–28 
Although AVSD is not identified as a risk 
factor for the primary outcome, we still cannot rule out 
the possibility that presence of AVSD potentially 
contribute to the adverse outcomes. A plausible expla -
nation for the failure to identify the AVSD phenotype as 
a risk factor may be that it could have collinearity with 
other variables (e.g., LA size, NT-proBNP). Age of 0–2 
year was considered to be the highest risk age category, 
which helps to support that notion that younger age is 
associated with poorer outcomes. Interestingly, the 
failure to identify age of <1 year as a risk factor may be 
due to the fact that there were a considerable number of 
patients with age <1 year had secondary MR in com -
bination with VSD, ASD, and/or PDA wherein better 
prognosis can usually be expected.
Some may argue the appropriateness of surgery in 
this series because nearly one-third of the patients were 
asymptomatic at the initial surgery and twenty-four 
percent of the patients had only mild MR. Several 
clarifications should be made. First, this study trended 
towards younger patients. Notably, these patients may 
not accurately report the clinical symptoms by them -
selves. Instead, the information was materially obtained 
from their parents/caregivers. Thus, there may be a risk 
of reporting bias due to parental interpretation, espe -
cially when the symptoms were based on subjective 
judgements (e.g., feeding difficulties, growth restric -
tion). Therefore, we did not merely rely on the symp -
toms for decision-making. Second, a majority of the 
patients with mild MR had AVSD wherein valvuloplasty 
(e.g., cleft closure) was routinely performed. The 
remaining patient with mild MR had elevated NT- 
proBNP level, enlarged left heart which may suggest 
impaired cardiac function and cardiac remodelling. The 
surgeries were performed after a thorough communi -
cation with their parents. Notably, no significant dif -
ference in the primary outcomes when stratified by MR 
degree in this study also partially supports the notion 
that a combination of clinical symptoms, echocardio -
graphic parameters, and blood biomarker should be 
taken into account for decision-making.
Despite that bootstrap method was used to mitigate 
the issue of heterogeneity in terms of the sample size 
between the derivation and validation cohort, such 
discrepancy still potentially influenced the robustness 
of the risk score model. Further studies are definitively 
required to assess the generalisability of this model. On 
the other hand, such heterogeneity in sample size may 
also reflect a real-world situation. For example, 
paediatric MR is a rare and complex congenital heart 
disease that usually requiring individualised treat -
ment.
29,30 
Plausibly, there is a high likelihood that 
regionalisation of the treatment care may occur in this 
clinical setting and these patients were more willing to 
receive treatment in high-volume centres.
This risk score model also allows for prognostication 
of MVR after failure of valve repair. As a general prin -
ciple, valve surgery in paediatric MR focuses on the 
possibility of repair before eventually committing to 
replacement given the disadvantages of prosthetic 
valves, i.e., obligatory lifelong anticoagulation, need for 
subsequent repeated replacements, and increased 
mortality risk (10%–36%).
30,31 
In this regard, our find -
ings have implications in two aspects: first, the heart 
team should comprehensively take LVEF, preoperative 
LA diameter, and NT-proBNP into account to ensure an 
optimal timing of surgery to decrease the incidence of 
subsequent MVR. Secondly, structured surveillance 
should be warranted in patients who are at high risk of 
repair failure and require inevitable valve replacement.
The present study supports that posterior suture 
annuloplasty should be included as an essential 
component to the repair strategy across all risk cate -
gories, which appears to increase the repair durability. 
Plausible advantages of annuloplasty include (1) stabi -
lisation of the annulus and restoration of its physio -
logical systolic relationships and (2) optimising annular 
dimension/shape to facilitate effective coaptation and 
decrease the stress on the leaflets.
32,33 
Nevertheless, we 
cannot exclude the possibility that those who did not 
receive annuloplasty may have intrinsic unfavourable 
anatomic features and at high risk of repair failure.
Our findings should be interpreted with caution. 
First, although parameters derived from standard two- 
dimensional echocardiography can be easy to obtain 
and appears to be useful, novel imaging metrics 
reflecting cardiac remodelling derived from speckle 
tracking echocardiography or cardiac magnetic reso -
nance may offer better prognostic as well as diagnostic 
value in MR setting.
28 
Second, the data in this study 
originates from tertiary centres where patients may be 
referred for MV surgery following local, site-specific 
policies, introducing selection bias. Prior to prospec -
tive validation, a broader validation in diverse patient 
demographics is needed to assess the performance of 
our models, and is crucial to mitigate the risk of sys -
temic biases. Third, although important variables were 
included in the model, it may not be applicable to 
paediatric patients with MR of other etiologies (i.e., 
rheumatic heart disease, endocarditis, connective tissue 
disorders) because calibration and precision could vary 
by phenotype. Fourth, surgeon level of expertise in MV 
surgery varies among different institutions and may 
influence the results. Lastly, discrepancy in sample size 
between the derivation and validation cohort may be 
due to the selection bias. In addition, other residual and
