Percutaneous cardiac catheterisation is an essential modality
in the diagnosis and management of children with congenital
cardiac disease. An increasing array of percutaneous pro-
cedures, alone or in conjunction with minimally invasive
open interventions, are being performed with the goal of
postponing, augmenting, or replacing full open surgical
intervention via sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass
(CPB). As a result, more physiologically complex patients are
presenting to the cardiac catheterisation laboratory (CCL) for
multidisciplinary procedures. This trend is likely to continue.
Deﬁning risk within medicine remains a challenge, and
‘acceptable’ risk is constantly evolving, inﬂuenced by pa-
tients’ expectations, improving outcomes and institutional
oversight.1 Currently, the incidence of cardiac arrest is esti-
mated to be between 0.8% and 1.6% of all procedures in the
CCL, with bradycardia and pulseless electrical activity or
asystole the most likely precipitating rhythm. 2 Managing
cases in the CCL requires the anaesthetist to have a thorough
understanding of the patient’s anatomy and physiology, the
procedure being performed, potential complications and a
plan for dealing with them in advance. Given the number of
team members involved (interventionalists, radiographers,
cardiac physiologists, theatre staff, and often cardiac sur-
geons and perfusionists), a detailed brieﬁng and communi-
cation are essential before commencing any procedure in the
CCL.

Considerations before the procedure
Preoperative assessment should focus on current cardiovas-
cular function with emphasis on signs and symptoms of left
and right cardiac failure, baseline oxygen saturation, and cur-
rent medications. Noninvasive cardiac imaging such as trans-
thoracic echocardiogram (TTE) should be reviewed for
structural and functional heart defects and estimation of pul-
monary pressures where appropriate. The potential risk for a
particular procedure can be difﬁcult to estimate because of
large variability between procedures and patients. The same
factors make evidence-based decision making difﬁcult. Oper-
ator bias may inﬂuence which patients are offered interven-
tional treatment over surgery and can potentially lead to the
exclusion of other possible treatment options. 3 Analysis of
registries of congenital cardiac catheterisations such as
Congenital Cardiac Catheterisation Project on Outcomes (C3PO)
and Improving Paediatric and Adult Congenital Treatment
(IMPACT) has allowed the development of scoring systems to
categorise risk, based on haemodynamic vulnerability, type of
procedure and age. The Catheterisation Risk Score for Paedi-
atrics (CRISP) can be used to predict the risk of adverse events in
higher risk patients ( Table 1). The CRISP score divides patients
into ﬁve risk groups with a risk of adverse events of 1% in CRISP
1( S c o r e0e2) patients and 36.8% in CRISP 5 (Score 15 e21) pa-
tients.4 Patients with pulmonary hypertension, for example are
at signiﬁcant risk. O’Byrne and colleagues found a rate of
catastrophic adverse events, deﬁned as death, cardiac arrest or
new requirement for extracorporeal life support (ECLS), of 1.4%
in these patients with risk increasing with severity of pulmo-
nary hypertension.5
We advocate the use of a CCL-speciﬁc WHO checklist,
which has been shown to be associated with decreased radi-
ation exposure, fewer procedural complications, faster turn-
around and improved staff experience. This includes
establishing a vascular access plan, requirement for anti-
coagulation and any additional equipment that may be
required before the start of the case. 6
Previous vascular puncture sites should be noted as this
may have consequences for arterial and venous access sites
for both the anaesthetist and interventional cardiologist in
addition to required puncture sites for the forthcoming pro-
cedure (e.g. site required for surgical vascular cutdown, cen-
tral venous access in the neck).
Anaesthesia
The exact conduct of anaesthesia is not prescriptive and de-
pends on a number of variables, much of which has been
covered in greater detail previously in this journal.
7,8 Key
factors in determining anaesthetic technique are the indi-
vidual patient’s anatomy and the nature of the planned
intervention. Choice of anaesthetic agents must account for
their effects on systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance
(PVR) and how this may alter ﬂow and shunt in the individual
patient. Both i.v. and gas induction can be used safely in
children with congenital heart disease (CHD). General anaes-
thesia (GA) and positive pressure ventilation may be avoided
by using sedation but this may result in hypoventilation, hy-
percapnia and increased PVR.
Catheterisation procedures usually cause minimal painful
stimulation after placement of the vascular access sheath and
TOE probe. Inﬁltration of local anaesthetic at puncture sites or
regional blockade can be used to attenuate pain from these
interventions.
Anticoagulation may be necessary and depends on the
proposed intervention and the anatomical considerations of
the child. Typically, heparin 50e100 units kg/C01 is required once
vascular access is established. Activated clotting time (ACT)
may be used to guide heparin dosing with a target of>200 s, but
it must be noted ACT does not solely reﬂect the effect of hep-
arin. Antibiotics may be required. Use of protamine sulfate is
not standard; however, it may be indicated where there is dif-
ﬁculty achieving haemostasis and the ACT remains increased.9
The most suitable postoperative destination for the patient
should be decided as early as possible to allow adequate time
to allocate resources on the general wards or critical care unit.
Requirements for analgesics requirements are variable, and
generally consist of a combination of local/regional anaes-
thesia, paracetamol, and supplemented with non-steroidal
drugs, opioids, or both when required. Extubation under
deep anaesthesia may be considered to minimise the effects
of coughing and straining on the puncture sites, and has been
safely performed with and without the use of dexmedetomi-
dine in paediatric patients with a myriad of cardiac lesions. 10

the CCL and ergonomics have led to incorporation of

Radiation safety
For procedures using ionising radiation, all staff should use
lead aprons with a thyroid shield, leaded glass screens and
appropriate eyewear. General radiation safety best practice
applies: minimise exposure time, maximise distance from
radiation source, use appropriate radiation protection, wear a
dosimeter and minimise patient exposure where possible.
Children are more susceptible to radiation tissue injury and
receive a higher cumulative dose over their lifetime from
multiple interventions. 11 Cineﬂuoroscopy involves some of
the highest radiation doses encountered in theatre, and staff
should limit their proximity to the radiation source.
Background noise and the distances between practitioners
places impediments to clear communication between anaes-
thetists, cardiologists, radiographers, physiologists and
nursing staff. In some centres wireless headsets are used to
aid communication. Percutaneous interventions can result in
signiﬁcant haemodynamic changes that may be anticipated
and mitigated by clear and open communication. Where CPB
or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support is
anticipated, positioning of perfusion equipment and staff in
addition to potential vascular access sites should be accoun-
ted for during the preparation phase.
General complications
The widening remit of catheterisation has led to sicker and
smaller children undergoing intervention. Complication rates
increase with decreasing patient weight. Infants <2k gh a v e
the highest risk of severe adverse events with 12% in one
study suffering a catastrophic adverse event (one leading to
death, emergent surgical intervention or ECMO). 12 Adverse
events are more frequent in interventional relative to diag-
nostic catheterisations. 13 Data from the paediatric perioper-
ative cardiac arrest registry shows up to 25% of arrests occur
in children with CHD, with 17% of those arrests occurring in
the CCL. 14 Lasa and colleagues 2 analysed a multicentre reg-
istry of cardiac arrest in the paediatric CCL and found that 41%
had a diagnosis of cardiac disease whereas more than 50%
were under 1 yr old. An analysis of the IMPACT registry
looking at paediatric catheterisation in children with and
without CHD found a rate of major adverse events of 7.2%. 15
Complications of catheterisation can be classiﬁed as set out
in Table 2 and further divided into minor or major (requiring
further intervention or surgery). Vascular complications
account for up to a third of all adverse events with arterial
thrombus the most common. Medical management of
thrombus is usually successful; however, issues with vessel
patency can persist in some patients, which may have sig-
niﬁcant impact on future interventional procedures.

Diagnostic catheterisation
Catheterisation allows for the elucidation of cardiac anatomy,
haemodynamics, function and responsiveness to speciﬁc in-
terventions. Where pulmonary and systemic pressures are
being measured, the interventionalist may request that the
patient’s gas exchange approximates as closely as possible to
their physiology when awake (i.e. inspired oxygen close to
room air, normocapnia, breathing spontaneously or venti-
lated to optimise functional residual capacity.) This also
minimises the dissolved oxygen when calculating cardiac
output.
16
Three-dimensional (3D) rotational angiography can be per-
formed on-table to generate 3D images of cardiac anatomy and
surrounding structures. This can assist preoperative planning,
allow for the creation of 3D models or to map structures for
intervention in the CCL (e.g. 3D imaging of coarctation of aorta).
During image acquisition, the patient typically requires a
breath hold, rapid ventricular pacing, or both. 17
Endomyocardial biopsy may be performed for surveillance
of rejection in patients who have had a cardiac transplant.
This procedure, although invasive, carries low morbidity and
may detect evidence of rejection in up to 20% of patients. 18

Device embolisation is rare (1 e2%) and also usually occurs
within 24 h of placement. 20
Septostomy
In cardiac conditions with inadequate mixing of blood (e.g.
transposition of the great arteries with a restrictive atrial
septum) or with hypertensive atrial pressures (e.g. left atrial
hypertension in a patient on veno-arterial ECMO), an ASD can
be created to form an atrial shunt. This can be achieved via
balloon atrial septostomy (the Rashkind procedure), with a
Brokenbrough needle or radiofrequency catheter. Occasion-
ally, septal puncture may be incorporated as part of another
procedure when it is necessary to access the left side of the
heart, for example anterograde aortic valvuloplasty, pulmo-
nary vein access. Complications include pericardial effusion
and haemorrhage.

Procedures on vessels
Pulmonary vasculature
Pulmonary artery balloon angioplasty, stenting, or both is one
of the most common procedures performed in the CCL for
CHD.
22 Improvement in pulmonary blood ﬂow may help bal-
ance cardiac output to the pulmonary and systemic circula-
tion or relieve pressure on the right heart. Stenting may be
indicated where the vessel diameter permits use of a stent,
which can be further dilated up to adult size or, in select cases,
a smaller stent can be used as part of a palliation strategy.
Balloon angioplasty alone may allow for intervention where
vascular anatomy precludes stenting. Where vascular steno-
sis is secondary to external compression, beneﬁt may be
temporary. The IMPACT registry showed 14% of pulmonary
artery interventions involved a complication, with 9%
involving a major adverse event such as vessel rupture, stent
embolisation, signiﬁcant haemodynamic instability and
death. Vessel rupture may result in bleeding into lung inter-
stitium, bronchi or the pleural space, and may be life threat-
ening. Use of a low-pressure balloon or covered stent may
help to control haemorrhage but surgical control may be
required. Stent embolisation can occur in up to 6% of cases.
The stent may be retrievable endovascularly or may require
surgical intervention. 22
Ductus arteriosus
Depending on the underlying physiology, patients may pre-
sent to the CCL for closure of a ductus arteriosus (in the event
that the duct is causing too much systemic to pulmonary
shunt) or a procedure to keep it open (if the circulation re-
quires a duct to provide blood from the systemic to pulmonary
circulation or vice versa). Persistent ductus arteriosus (PDA) in
the preterm neonate has been linked with signiﬁcant
morbidity including increased rates of necrotising enteroco-
litis, chronic lung disease and retinopathy of prematurity.
23 In
cases where medical management has failed, there may be a
role for percutaneous closure over open surgical repair. The
utility of this approach is limited by the patient’s size and
anatomy although closure in infants weighing <1 kg are now
widely reported. For complications relating to vascular access,
treatment success and device embolisation are all more
common in lower-weight patients. 17
In patients with duct-dependent lesions the maintenance
of ductus patency is paramount to bridge the patient until
deﬁnitive repair or surgical palliation can be carried out. These
cases may carry high risk owing to small patient size and the
nature of their cardiac anatomy (e.g. critical aortic stenosis).
Over time PDA stents may become less effective because of
intimal narrowing necessitating upsizing, replacement or
surgical intervention. The potential exists for the duct to
spasm or inadvertently occlude during the procedure neces-
sitating an emergency open cardiac procedure to provide
stability for pulmonary or systemic blood ﬂow.
Coarctation
Angioplasty and stenting can be used to treat coarctation of
the aorta either as a primary treatment or in the setting of
post-surgical recurrence of coarctation. Typically, the inter-
ventional approach is ﬁrst line for older children and adoles-
cents; however, increasing technological advances have
allowed its use in younger children. Angioplasty alone may
not be sufﬁcient because of the elasticity of the aorta and
potential for aortic aneurysm formation. Using a balloon-
expandable stent allows for future upsizing to an adult’s
aortic diameter although their use is limited by the size of the
femoral vessels needed to allow passage of a large sheath. 24

valves. Once the gradient across the stenotic valve has been

complete occlusion of ﬂow across the valve will occur

Aortic stenosis may require intervention in the neonate

arteriosus. Balloon valvuloplasty is comparable with surgical
valvotomy and can decrease the gradient across the aortic

ischaemia. As a result, these patients are at high risk for car-
diac arrest.
Pulmonary valvuloplasty may be indicated to improve
pulmonary blood ﬂow in cases of congenital pulmonary ste-
nosis and pulmonary atresia with intact interventricular
septum. Pericardial effusion, arrhythmia and myocardial
ischaemia can occur. Mortality can be as high as 6%.

Valve replacement
Percutaneous pulmonary valve replacement (PPVR) has
become increasingly useful in the management of right ven-
tricular outﬂow tract (RVOT) dysfunction, particularly after
transannular patch repair of tetralogy of Fallot. Chronic pul-
monary regurgitation has traditionally been managed by
surgical repair; however, PPVR offers the potential to inter-
vene earlier before RVOT diameter has dilated beyond the
suitability of available devices. Pulmonary valve replacement
is indicated where there is symptomatic moderate to severe
pulmonary regurgitation, RV pressures >2/3 systemic, or
both.
27 Limitations to its use are based on RVOT diameter and
the patient’s size. Transjugular or perventricular access may
be required to accommodate the large delivery system,
although lower proﬁle delivery mechanisms are being devel-
oped.28 Alongside complications related to vascular access,
these patients are particularly at risk of RVOT tear, compres-
sion of coronary arteries because of aberrant coronary anat-
omy and endocarditis.
Percutaneous mitral valve replacement has also been
used successfully in children with signiﬁcant mitral valve
disease.29
Hybrid procedures
Hybrid procedures use a combination of interventional and
open surgical techniques to create treatment options that are
less invasive than primary surgical repair ( Figs. 1 & 2).
Norwood procedure
Traditionally, ﬁrst-stage palliation in functional single
ventricle lesions involves a systemic-to-pulmonary shunt,
creation of a neo-aorta and creation of a common atrium via
atrial septectomy. In addition to the stress of surgery, the
neonatal brain is also subjected to the sequelae of CPB and
deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. The alternative ‘hybrid ’
combined surgical and percutaneous intervention offers the
possibility of providing circulatory stability via a less invasive
procedure. Indeed, the avoidance of CPB, cardioplegia and
surgical alterations in cerebral blood ﬂow during aortic arch
reconstruction in these neonates has been hypothesised to
improve neurological outcomes. 30 The hybrid stage I proced-
ure involves transcatheter PDA stent placement to maintain
pulmonary blood ﬂow, an atrial septostomy to ensure
adequate mixing and surgical branch pulmonary artery
banding to prevent excess pulmonary blood ﬂow. This
approach comes at the cost of committing the infant to a
comprehensive stage II operation at 4 e6 months. The stage 1
and 2 procedures (the Norwood and the bidirectional cav-
opulmonary anastomosis) are combined into a single inter-
vention with a signiﬁcantly more precarious immediate
postoperative course. Furthermore, neonates undergoing the
hybrid repair are more likely to require further intervention
before a Fontan procedure because of their smaller pulmonary
arteries.
31 There is signiﬁcant inter-institutional variation in
practice, which, when combined with a trend toward higher
risk neonates being offered a hybrid stage 1 procedure, makes
direct comparison between hybrid stage 1 and the Norwood
procedure difﬁcult.

Fontan procedure
The total cavopulmonary circulation can be prepared in
advance by creating a fenestrated lateral tunnel with a patch
to occlude the superior vena cava (SVC) end such that the SVC
and right pulmonary artery are separated. Once the patient is
suitable for a total cavopulmonary connection, a trans-
catheter approach can be used to open the superior end of the
lateral tunnel via perforation of the patch followed by balloon
dilation and stenting. Although this adds complexity to the
stage II operation, it allows for avoidance of resternotomy for
the stage III procedure. 32
Special circumstances
Extracorporeal life support
Catheterisation of the patient during ECLS carries signiﬁcant
complication because of the patient’s morbidity, difﬁculty in
transport and anticoagulation status. Veno-arterial ECMO
does not completely unload the left heart and as a result
distension of the left side can result in back pressure on the
pulmonary circulation, increased left ventricular end-
diastolic pressure (LVEDP), increased wall stress and
myocardial dysfunction, which will impair any attempts to
wean the patient from ECLS. Transeptal puncture of the inter-
atrial septum can decompress the left atrium, which in turn
may aid recovery.
Boscamp and colleagues
33 have shown catheterisation in
patients on ECMO to be high yield with 78% of patients in their
case series undergoing intervention. Of those patients who
did not merit intervention in the CCL, defects requiring sur-
gical intervention were identiﬁed in 40%. The majority of
patients required transseptal puncture to decompress the left
heart.33
Conclusions
Anaesthesia in the CCL presents signiﬁcant challenges,
although these can be overcome with adequate preparation,
knowledge of the patient’s speciﬁc physiology and good
interdisciplinary communication. Continuing innovation in
interventional cardiology will result in smaller and sicker
patients coming to the CCL.
