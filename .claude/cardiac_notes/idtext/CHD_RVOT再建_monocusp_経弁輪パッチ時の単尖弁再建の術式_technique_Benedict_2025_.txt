INNOVATION
Novel technique of monocuspid reconstruction of pulmonary valve 
in children requiring transannular patch for right ventricular outflow 
tract relief during tetralogy of Fallot surgery
Raj Benedict1 · Rhazane Nalluri2 · Vasudev Vemala3 
Received: 24 December 2023 / Revised: 16 September 2024 / Accepted: 17 September 2024 / Published online: 28 October 2024 
© Indian Association of Cardiovascular-Thoracic Surgeons 2024
Abstract
Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) repair involves the placement of a transannular patch (TAP) to relieve right ventricular outflow 
tract (RVOT) obstruction. TAP results in free pulmonary regurgitation (PR) after surgery. PR is responsible for most of the 
long-term complications in patients with operated TOF. To overcome this problem of PR we describe a novel method of 
monocusp reconstruction of the pulmonary valve using autologous pericardium in children requiring TAP. Newly created 
monocusp pulmonary valves reduce PR, help in faster recovery from surgery, and reduce long-term issues associated with 
repaired TOF.
Keywords Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) · Transannular patch (TAP) · Monocusp valve · Pulmonary regurgitation (PR)
Introduction
Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is one of the most common 
cyanotic congenital heart diseases (CHD) in clinical prac-
tice. TOF is surgically repaired by closing the ventricular 
septal defect (VSD) and relieving right ventricular outflow 
tract (RVOT) obstruction. Surgical correction has evolved 
with time, advances in imaging, and our understanding of 
the long-term complications of repaired TOF.
Earlier surgical techniques were based on aggressive 
relief of RVOT obstruction. They involved extensive resec-
tion of the RVOT and placement of a large transannular 
patch (TAP) across the pulmonary valve [ 1]. This resulted 
in good intermediate survival outcomes but was associated 
with increased long-term complications [1 ]. Placement of 
large TAP resulted in increased late morbidity and mortality 
[2, 3]. Chronic pulmonary regurgitation (PR), although well 
tolerated in childhood, is responsible for most long-term 
complications in repaired TOF [2, 3].
Free PR also affects immediate postoperative recov -
ery, leads to prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and 
increases perioperative morbidity and mortality. Long-term 
free PR results in volume overload and remodeling of the 
right ventricle (RV), which can lead to progressive RV dila-
tion and RV failure [ 2, 3]. To overcome this problem of 
PR, in this study, we describe a novel yet simple method of 
monocuspid reconstruction of the pulmonary valve using 
autologous pericardium in children requiring TAP to relieve 
RVOT obstruction (Figs. 1 and 2).
Methods
Informed consent was obtained from parents prior to the 
surgical procedure and pulmonary valve reconstruction, 
and the expected pros and cons of this new technique were 
explained. All procedures were performed in accordance 
with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on 
human experimentation (institutional and national) and the 
Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2000. Ethical 
clearance was taken for the novel technique from the insti-
tutional review board (Ethical approval certificate number: 
XXX/IEC/123/2020, Dated: 22–12-2020).

The patch was sized to fit from the PA confluence (or 
beyond, if LPA plasty was planned) to the end of the proxi-

in mind the length and width of the TAP needed. The size of 
the monocusp was determined by the pre-calculated RVOT

the size of the ventriculotomy.
1. The pericardium was harvested as a single patch for 
monocusp and TAP with prior planning of the line of 
division (Fig. 3a), which was demarcated by the change 
in the width of the patch. The proximal rectangular part 
of the patch was used for TAP (from A to D1 in Fig. 3c), 
while the distal part of the patch (from D2 to edge C2–B2 
in Fig. 3d) was marked for use as a monocusp.
2. The harvested pericardial patch was treated with 0.6% 
glutaraldehyde solution for 10 min, and only the proxi-
mal rectangular portion of the patch (A1 to D1 in Fig. 3a) 
was soaked and washed. The part of the patch to be used 
for monocusp remained untreated (from D2 to C2–B2 in 
Fig. 3a).
3. Distal-site anastomosis (A1 in Fig. 3g) was performed 
with utmost care to prevent bleeding. Sometimes, we 
had to go on low flows for the initial few stitches of the 
anastomosis. The suture line was continued towards the 
annulus and paused on both sides, and rubber shods were 
placed on the two ends of the suture.
4. Rewarming was started, the cross-clamp was released, 
and the rest of the procedure was performed on the 
beating heart.

5. The monocusp was rotated 180° clockwise (Fig.  3e) 
and sutured in such a way that the smooth side was 
towards the RVOT (B2–C2 in Fig.  3f) using a three-
layered sandwich technique (Fig.  3g) starting from the 
assistant’s side towards the surgeon’s side to create a good 
cylindrical hood for the TAP. During reconstruction of 
the pulmonary valve, the monocusp and pericardium for 
TAP were fixed with stay sutures for stability and orienta-
tion (Fig. 3g).
6. The patch was tailored such that it filled up synchro -
nously with the RV ejection. Immediately after the proce-
dure on the operating table, a monocusp was sometimes 
seen moving within the patch, which could be confirmed 
on epicardial or transesophageal echocardiography.
7. The patient was weaned from CPB and hemostasis was 
secured. RV and left ventricle (LV) pressures (PRV/PLV 
ratio) were calculated. The PRV/PLV ratio in our series 
of patients with monocusp ranged from 0.35 to 0.75. The 
low diastolic value of RV pressure indirectly provides a 
measure of the competence of the newly created mono-
cusp valve.
8. Transesophageal echocardiography or epicardial echo-
cardiography was used to confirm adequate ventricular 
function and competency of the monocusp valve. If the 
distal free edge of the monocusp prolapsed beyond the 
annulus of the PA into the RV, it was minimally plicated 
in the middle to achieve competence.

Indications
We attempted to perform this technique in all the cases of 
TOF that required TAP. Our cohort of patients was mainly 
infants, with the youngest being five months of age. How -
ever, we did not use this technique in newborns. We prefer 
this technique in post-BT shunt cases where PA pressure 
tends to be high and PR tends to be high in the immediate 
postoperative period.
Contraindications
We successfully performed monocuspid reconstruction of 
the pulmonary valve in all cases. In cases where there was 
a coronary crossing RVOT, monocuspid reconstruction was 
difficult to perform and was a major contraindication for this 
procedure. In redo sternotomy cases (post-pericardiotomy), 
our technique was not possible because we did not obtain 
an adequate quantity of native pericardium for monocuspid 
reconstruction. The native pericardium retains flexibility and 
mobility, unlike the glutaraldehyde-treated pericardium.
Review of literature
Multiple monocuspid and bicuspid reconstruction tech-
niques have been reported in the literature. Sung et al. [ 4] 
described the monocusp reconstruction technique by aug-
mentation of the pulmonary cusp using bovine pericardium 
during TAP. In their technique, most patients had a bicuspid 
pulmonary valve, and the anterior leaflet was augmented. 
The divided pulmonary valve was repaired to maintain 
hinge mechanism for better pulmonary valve competence. 
Few studies have shown that the long-term durability of the 
pericardium is unpredictable, and reconstruction has been 
attempted using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). The use of 
PTFE increases the cost and is not applicable to all pulmo-
nary valve anatomies. Unlike the Sung et al. [4 ] technique, 
we preferred using untreated native pericardium for mono-
cusp reconstruction as we have had good results and found 
less of early calcification. Furthermore, in our reconstruc-
tion, divided pulmonary valve repair was not imperative in 
each case. In our repair, the pulmonary annulus had better 
growth potential and was an advantage over the long term.
Unlike the Ozaki template [5], we selected a rectangular 
shape to obtain a simplified and reproducible model. Three-
layered sandwich anastomosis, viz. pericardium of TAP, 
pericardium of monocusp, and right ventricular epicardium 
along the ventriculotomy, makes monocuspid reconstruction

Follow‑up
In the immediate postoperative period, PR was nil or mild, 
which facilitated early extubation and fast tracking from the 
ICU. PR assessment was performed in the immediate post-
operative period and during follow-up at 6-month intervals. 
In the short-term follow-up (2.5 years from the first case), 
we found PR to be mild or moderate with good mobility of 
the monocusp valve; most importantly, there was no sig -
nificant RVOT obstruction. None of the patients required 
re-intervention. The follow-up data are presented in Table 2. 
Our study was a small series with a short follow-up duration, 
and the results are encouraging. Long-term follow-up can 
validate our technique and the durability of the monocusp 
in maintaining pulmonary competence.
Conclusion
Chronic PR is responsible for most long-term complica -
tions of TOF surgery. This novel monocuspid reconstruc-
tion technique using untreated pericardium and three-
layered sandwich anastomosis for pulmonary valve repair 
in TOF patients shows promise in addressing pulmonary 
regurgitation. Key advantages include cost-effectiveness, 
reproducibility, and broad applicability to all pulmonary 
valve anatomies. The initial results are encouraging, with 
patients showing minimal regurgitation and satisfactory 
valve mobility with no obstruction on follow-up. This inno-
vative approach represents a significant advancement in TOF 
repair, potentially improving the outcomes and quality of life 
of affected children.
