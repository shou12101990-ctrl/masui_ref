repair: A focus on posterior leaflet augmentation

ABSTRACT
Objective: To evaluate mid- and long-term outcomes of posterior leaflet augmen-
tation with fresh autologous pericardium for congenital or acquired mitral regur-
gitation in pediatric populations, and to identify predictors of valve-related 
reintervention.
Methods: A retrospective review was conducted on all patients who underwent 
mitral valve repair, including posterior leaflet augmentation, at Bambino Ges� u
Children’s Hospital between 2011 and 2024. Patients with atrioventricular septal 
defects or single-ventricle physiology were excluded. Demographic, operative, 
and echocardiographic data were analyzed. Freedom from reintervention was 
estimated using Kaplan-Meier analysis and risk factors were identified by Cox pro-
portional hazards modeling.
Results: Twenty-eight patients (median age, 3.8 years; interquartile range, 
0.6-9.2 years; 57% men) underwent posterior leaflet augmentation using fresh 
autologous pericardium. Congenital mitral dysplasia was the most common 
etiology (50%), followed by rheumatic disease (29%). Mean preoperative mitral 
regurgitation grade was 3.9 ± 0.6, decreasing to 1.4 ± 0.5 postoperatively, and 
remained stable during follow-up (mean residual mitral regurgitation, 
1.7 ± 0.7 mm Hg). Mean transmitral gradient increased from 2.5 ± 1.8 mm Hg to 
4.2 ± 2.1 mm Hg, remaining stable over time (4.9 ± 2.2 mm Hg at last follow-
up). During a median follow-up of 7.7 years (interquartile range, 6.6-10.5 years), 
9 patients (32%) required reintervention, all consisting of mitral valve replace-
ment. Freedom from reintervention was 84.9% at 1 year, 75.9% at 5 years, and 
54.2% at 10 years. A postoperative mean gradient>5 mm Hg was the only indepen-
dent predictor of reintervention (hazard ratio, 7.81; 95% CI, 1.9-31.4; P = .004).
Conclusions: Posterior leaflet augmentation with fresh autologous pericardium 
provides a durable, growth-preserving, and physiologic repair for pediatric mitral 
valve disease predominantly presenting with severe regurgitation. A postoperative 
transmitral gradient>5 mm Hg identifies patients at higher risk for late mitral valve 
replacement, emphasizing the importance of accurate intraoperative tailoring and 
early echocardiographic follow-up. (JTCVS Techniques 2026;36:102264)

PERSPECTIVE
Posterior leaflet augmentation with fresh autolo-
gous pericardium offers a growth-preserving and 
durable repair for pediatric mitral valve disease 
predominantly presenting with severe regurgita-

mizing patch geometry and confirming its role as 
a bridge to definitive valve replacement in 
growing patients.

Congenital mitral valve disease (MVD) encompasses a 
wide spectrum of structural abnormalities that severely in-
fluence valve mobility and competence. 1 Among these, 
dysplastic forms characterized by restricted leaflet motion 
are particularly frequent in pediatric populations. 2,3 In this 
setting, the posterior leaflet is often hypoplastic, thickened, 
or adherent to the posterior wall of the left ventricle, result-
ing in limited excursion and reduced coaptation surface. 
Such anatomical conditions make conventional repair 
strategies, derived from adult experience, unsuitable for pe-
diatric use. 3-6 Posterior leaflet augmentation (PLA) using 
fresh autologous pericardium offers an alternative, 
physiologic approach. By enlarging the posterior leaflet, 
this technique improves the coaptation area and restores 
mobility while maintaining the potential for valve growth 
and remodeling during follow-up. 7-9 However, long-term 
data on outcomes and predictors of reintervention after 
PLA in congenital mitral dysplasia remain limited. The pre-
sent study aims to evaluate mid- and long-term results of 
PLA in children and adolescents with congenital MVD 
and to identify risk factors for valve-related adverse events, 
focusing on postoperative residual stenosis.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Ethical Statement
The study conforms to the ethical principles of the 
Declaration of Helsinki and is in compliance with the 
current Italian regulations. The institutional review board 
was notified February 5, 2024. Individual consent waived 
due to retrospective design and anonymity.
Study Population and Follow-up
We retrospectively reviewed all consecutive patients who 
underwent MV repair with PLA at Bambino Ges�u 
Children’s Hospital between February 2011 and December 
2024. All procedures were primary MV repairs; no patient 
had previously undergone MV repair. Patients with 
complete or partial atrioventricular septal defects and 
single-ventricle physiology were excluded from analysis. 
A total of 28 patients were identified. Clinical, operative, 
and echocardiographic data were obtained from
institutional databases. Echocardiographic follow-up was 
performed before discharge, at 6, 12, and 24 months, and 
annually thereafter. End points of the study were survival 
after MV repair and cumulative incidence of reoperation.
Echocardiographic Evaluation
Transthoracic and intraoperative transesophageal echo-
cardiography were used to assess the integrity of MV repair. 
Quantitative assessment of MV stenosis was estimated 
based on the mean continuous-wave Doppler gradient 
across the valve and stratified in the following manner: 
grade I with mean gradient <5 mm Hg, grade II with 
mean gradient from 5 to 10 mm Hg, and grade III with a 
mean gradient of 10 mm Hg or more. The MV regurgitation 
was semiquantitatively assessed based on the jet 
regurgitation and was classified as: grade I = mild, grade 
II = mild-moderate, grade III = moderate, and grade 
IV = severe.
Timing of Surgery
Indications for surgery varied according to the etiology, 
anatomy, patient age, size of the MVannulus, need for intra-
venous drugs and the clinical status. 5,6 Neonates and infants 
with severe mitral regurgitation underwent surgery only if 
they were symptomatic despite maximal medical therapy. 
Patients with isolated mitral cleft underwent operation 
based on the grade of regurgitation, regardless of symp-
toms. Surgical indications for reoperation were dictated 
by symptoms, the amount of medications required, or the 
need for mechanical ventilation. No specific thresholds 
for either pulmonary arterial pressure or transmitral 
gradient triggered a surgical indication, if few or no symp-
toms were present. According to our institutional policy, 
posterior leaflet augmentation with fresh autologous peri-
cardium is reserved for patients in whom restricted motion 
of the posterior leaflet is the main mechanism of mitral 
regurgitation. Typical substrates include congenital MV 
dysplasia with short, thick, and partially muscularized chor-
dae predominantly involving the posterior leaflet and its 
subvalvular apparatus, selected postischemic lesions with 
posterior papillary muscle involvement, and small children 
with severe mitral regurgitation in whom increasing the 
global coaptation surface of the valve is preferred to isolated 
annuloplasty.
Surgical Technique
After median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass was 
instituted with aortic and bicaval cannulation under moder-
ate hypothermia (28 ◦ C). Myocardial protection was 
achieved using intermittent antegrade cold blood cardiople-
gia. Access to the MV was gained through a left atriotomy 
in the interatrial Waterston’s groove in children and adoles-
cents, or by a transseptal approach extending into the dome 
of the left atrium in neonates and infants. 10 Beginning in

curvature of the posterior leaflet and prevent excessive 
tissue redundancy, the free-edge side of the patch was 
made slightly longer than the annular side, thereby avoiding 
the aortic cusp effect. 8

was determined according to both preoperative echocardio-
graphic measurements and intraoperative inspection, using 
the following formula:

where APL = anterior leaflet free-edge length (measured in

Illustrative Case (Patient 11)
A 6-month-old infant (weight, 4.8 kg) with severe 
congenital MV dysplasia and restricted posterior leaflet mo-
tion was referred for surgery due to persistent heart failure 
and inability to wean from mechanical ventilation. The 
mitral annulus measured 13 mm. To determine the appro-
priate patch size, the maximal height of the anterior leaflet 
(15 mm) and the posterior leaflet (11 mm) were measured 
echocardiographically in mid-systole at the expected coap-
tation point. Applying the formula above, we obtained a 
coaptation surface of at least 7 mm: 11
H-patch = (15 mm -- 11 mm)+(7 mm -- 3 mm) = 
4 mm+4 mm = 8 mm
A pericardial patch with a maximal height of 8 mm 
(+2 mm for suture line) was fashioned and gently tapered 
toward both commissures (Figure 1). As shown in 
Figure 1, the pericardial patch assumes a sail-like configu-
ration that creates a new, extended line of leaflet closure and 
coaptation. The functional coaptation surface is provided by 
the patch itself, whereas the native posterior leaflet free 
margin falls into the left ventricle, forming a smooth and 
continuous transition between the native and augmented tis-
sues. Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography 
demonstrated satisfactory leaflet mobility and coaptation,
with a mean transmitral gradient of 4 mm Hg. The pericar-
dial patch was sutured to the annulus and native leaflet edge 
using a continuous 6-0 polypropylene suture reinforced by 
interrupted stitches at both commissures to prevent 
dehiscence.
The result of the repair was evaluated by saline injection 
through the valve and confirmed by intraoperative transeso-
phageal and/or epicardial echocardiography. Surgical revi-
sion was considered in case of a suboptimal immediate 
postoperative result—defined as mitral regurgitation 
grade ≥III and/or mean continuous wave Doppler gradient 
>10 mm Hg—although decision making was individual-
ized to each case. Associated repair procedures are summa-
rized in Table 1; artificial expanded polytetrafluoroethylene 
chordae were used exclusively in patients with associated 
anterior leaflet prolapse.
Follow-up
All patients underwent postoperative examinations once 
during the first month, every 6 months during the first 
year, and at least yearly thereafter, including a clinical 
assessment, an electrocardiogram, and a 2-dimensional 
echocardiogram with color Doppler study performed by 
the referring cardiologist. Clinical and echocardiographic 
mitral functional status and the number of reoperations at 
the most recent follow-up were obtained from local records. 
The end of the follow-up period was set at December 31, 
2024. Median follow-up was 7.7 years (interquartile range 
[IQR], 6.6-10.5 years).
Statistical Analysis
Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± SD or me-
dian (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables are pre-
sented as frequencies and percentages. Freedom from 
reintervention was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier 
method with 95% CI, and curves (with their corresponding 
numbers at risk) were truncated when the number of pa-
tients at risk dropped below predefined thresholds, in accor-
dance with recommendations. Differences between groups 
(postoperative gradient ≤5 vs >5 mm Hg) were evaluated 
using the log-rank test. A Cox proportional hazards model 
was used to identify independent predictors of reinterven-
tion, and results are reported as hazard ratios with 95% 
CI. All analyses were performed with SPSS Statistics 
version 21 (IBM Corp).

9.2 years), and the median body surface area was 0.65 m 2

Risk Factors for Reintervention
On univariate analysis, an immediate postoperative mean 
transmitral gradient >5 mm Hg emerged as the only factor 
significantly associated with the risk of reintervention 
(P = .004). Patients discharged with a gradient >5 mm
Hg had a hazard ratio of 7.81 (95% CI, 1.9-31.4) for subse-
quent reoperation compared with those with gradients
≤5 mm Hg (Figure 2). No other variable, including age, 
annulus size indexed to body surface area, or postoperative 
mitral regurgitation grade, was significantly associated with 
valve-related reintervention. In multivariate Cox analysis, 
the postoperative gradient remained the sole independent 
predictor of late repair failure.
Kaplan-Meier analysis stratified by postoperative 
gradient (Figure 3) confirmed a markedly reduced freedom 
from reintervention in patients with gradients >5 mm Hg. 
The 2 survival curves diverged early, within the first years 
after repair, and remained separated throughout follow-up. 
The median time to reintervention was approximately 
5 years, indicating that most events occurred within the first 
decade after repair. After an initial mild postoperative in-
crease, the mean transmitral gradient remained stable dur-
ing follow-up in a majority of patients. Only a few 
showed a progressive rise above 5 mm Hg, which correlated 
with later reintervention. Overall, gradients remained low 
and steady over time, confirming the durability and physio-
logical adaptation of the repair (Figure 4).

heterogeneity of lesions and the need to ensure valve growth 
over time. 1-3 Among these conditions, restricted motion of 
posterior leaflet represents among the most common 
morphologic substrates, often leading to incomplete 
coaptation and residual regurgitation after conventional 
repair. 2-8 Our experience demonstrates that PLA using 
fresh autologous pericardium provides durable valve 
competence and stable hemodynamics, confirming its 
reproducibility and long-term effectiveness in pediatric 
settings. 6
In our experience, PLA represents the optimal surgical 
option for Carpentier type III lesions, characterized by 
restricted motion of the posterior leaflet with preserved 
excursion of the anterior leaflet. 1,2 These anatomic 
conditions are common in congenital mitral dysplasia, 
where posterior leaflet hypoplasia or adherence to the left 
ventricular wall results in inadequate coaptation despite a 
normally mobile anterior leaflet. Although the technique 
was originally developed for rheumatic mitral disease, its 
application in the setting of congenital dysplasia with pos-
terior restriction has proven particularly effective, restoring 
leaflet motion and achieving a physiological closure line. 7,8 
This observation is consistent with the lesion profile of our 
series, in which the majority of patients (71%) were classi-
fied as Carpentier type III, and highlights the importance of 
tailoring the augmentation strategy to the leaflet imbalance 
typical of congenital forms.
In our cohort, mean mitral regurgitation grade decreased 
from 3.9 ± 0.6 preoperatively to 1.4 ± 0.5 after surgery and 
remained stable throughout follow-up, whereas the mean
transmitral gradient increased modestly to 4 to 5 mm Hg 
and showed no progressive rise. These findings are consis-
tent with those of Wierup and colleagues, 12 who reported 
stable gradients (4.4 ± 1.7 mm Hg) and mild residual mitral 
regurgitation in 82% of patients after leaflet expansion with 
fresh pericardium and subpartial annuloplasty. Together, 
these data confirm that fresh autologous pericardium 
ensures flexibility, adaptation to growth, and long-term 
coaptation without calcification or stiffening. 9,13
In our series, severe mitral regurgitation was the 
predominant lesion; in mixed cases, increased Doppler 
gradients often reflected high mitral inflow with complete 
valve opening rather than true anatomical stenosis. 
Conversely, patients with predominant anatomical mitral 
stenosis represent poor candidates for posterior leaflet 
augmentation, in whom patch size is critical and alternative 
repair strategies are usually preferred.
A residual mean transmitral gradient>5 mm Hg detected 
intraoperatively identifies patients at risk for late reinterven-
tion. This value reflects early residual obstruction rather 
than progressive late stenosis and likely depends on patch 
oversizing or inadequate contouring. In most patients, the 
gradient remained stable at follow-up, whereas only those 
with an early high gradient developed late dysfunction. 
This finding emphasizes the importance of precise 
intraoperative evaluation of valve opening and gradient 
immediately after weaning from bypass. In contrast to other 
approaches that combine leaflet augmentation with partial 
or complete annuloplasty, our technique deliberately avoids 
any form of annuloplasty. 12,14-19

This strategy aims to preserve annular growth potential 
and minimize the risk of postoperative mitral stenosis, 
which could otherwise increase with annular support. 
Because the PLA procedure itself tends to mildly reduce 
the valvular orifice, the addition of annuloplasty would 
further enhance the risk of obstruction, particularly in small 
children. The key to reducing obstruction lies in accurate 
sizing and shaping of the pericardial patch. The patch is de-
signed so that its free-edge side is slightly longer than the 
annular side to prevent the aortic cusp effect and maintain 
a smooth leaflet motion and wide coaptation area. 8 Func-
tionally, the sail-like configuration of the pericardial patch 
defines a new coaptation plane that replaces the native pos-
terior leaflet margin as the effective closure surface. This 
geometry promotes laminar flow and prevents residual pro-
lapse or turbulence along the coaptation line, explaining the 
stable gradients observed during long-term follow-up.
An important refinement in our strategy has been the 
abandonment of glutaraldehyde-treated pericardium. In 
children, chemical fixation promotes calcification and 
may impair annular and leaflet growth, leading to late 
restrictive physiology. The use of fresh autologous pericar-
dium preserves tissue pliability, allows integration with 
native structures, and supports physiological remodeling 
during growth. 9,13 Clinically, PLA should be regarded as 
a growth-preserving and temporizing repair, particularly 
valuable in small or symptomatic patients. When reinter-
vention becomes necessary, the procedure serves as a bridge 
to definitive valve replacement, allowing time for somatic 
and annular growth and reducing the risks associated with 
early prosthetic implantation. 20,21
Limitations
The main limitations of this study are its retrospective 
single-center design and the relatively small cohort size. 
However, the long and complete follow-up and the homoge-
neity of surgical technique strengthen the reliability of the 
findings. Future multicenter studies and biomechanical an-
alyses may better define optimal patch geometry and its 
adaptation to growth.
CONCLUSIONS
PLA with fresh autologous pericardium provides a 
durable, growth-preserving, and physiologic repair for 
pediatric MVD predominantly presenting with severe 
regurgitation. Avoiding annuloplasty minimizes the risk 
of iatrogenic stenosis, whereas meticulous patch tailoring 
reduces obstruction and preserves coaptation. A residual 
mean transmitral gradient >5 mm Hg detected intraopera-
tively identifies patients at risk for late reintervention. In 
this context, PLA should be regarded as a safe and effective 
bridge to definitive valve replacement, allowing somatic 
and annular growth and optimizing long-term outcomes.
