SUMMARY
Situs inversus totalis (SIT) with congenitally corrected 
transposition of the great arteries (ccTGA) represents 
a rare and complex congenital combination that 
significantly complicates anaesthetic management. 
We report the anaesthetic management of an early 
childhood (2–5 years), 13 kg child diagnosed with SIT 
and ccTGA undergoing elective adenotonsillectomy. 
Individualised perioperative strategies were 
implemented, including modified ECG monitoring, 
arrhythmia preparedness and explicit mg/kg drug 
dosing. Intraoperative management was uneventful, and 
the patient had a stable recovery. This case highlights 
anaesthetic considerations and monitoring adaptations 
in non- cardiac paediatric surgery involving mirror- image 
congenital cardiac anatomy.
BACKGROUND
Situs inversus totalis (SIT) is a congenital condi-
tion in which thoracic and abdominal organs are 
arranged in a mirror- image configuration. While 
SIT alone may be clinically benign, its association 
with complex congenital heart disease (CHD) such 
as congenitally corrected transposition of the great 
arteries (ccTGA) significantly complicates anaes-
thetic care. In ccTGA, atrioventricular and ventricu-
loarterial discordance places the morphologic right 
ventricle (RV) in the systemic position, predisposing 
it to dysfunction. These pathophysiological features 
demand precise planning of anaesthetic techniques 
and vigilant perioperative monitoring.
CASE PRESENTATION
An early childhood (2–5 years), (weight 13 kg) was 
scheduled for elective adenotonsillectomy due to 
obstructive sleep symptoms. She had a neonatal 
diagnosis of SIT and ccTGA, with associated peri-
membranous VSD, subvalvular pulmonary stenosis

On physical examination, the cardiac apex was 
palpable on the right side, consistent with mirror- 
image thoracic orientation. Oxygen saturation at

The 12- lead ECG showed inverted P waves and 
predominantly negative QRS complexes in lead I,

ccTGA. Accurate interpretation required mirror-

DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS
Preoperative evaluation of this paediatric patient 
initially raised diagnostic uncertainty due to the 
presence of dextrocardia on chest radiography 
(figure 1). This finding could reflect simple situs 
inversus with structurally normal heart, mirror- 
image dextrocardia or more complex congenital 
malformations such as complete TGA (1 ) or ccTGA. 
Electrocardiography was challenging to inter -
pret because reversed lead orientation mimicked 
conduction abnormalities (figure 2).
Echocardiography provided decisive infor -
mation: atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial 
discordance with preserved systemic ventricular 
function confirmed ccTGA rather than complete 
TGA, in which severe cyanosis would be expected. 
Abdominal imaging further demonstrated mirror- 
image positioning of the viscera, excluding isolated 
cardiac malposition and establishing SIT.
Thus, careful integration of imaging and clin-
ical findings allowed clinicians to exclude isolated 
dextrocardia and complete TGA and to establish 
the final diagnosis of SIT with ccTGA—a distinc-
tion critical for tailoring perioperative anaesthetic 
management and anticipating haemodynamic 
vulnerabilities. This is because in ccTGA, systemic

TREATMENT
The patient was classified as ASA Physical Status III. Endocar -
ditis prophylaxis (ampicillin 50 mg/kg IV) was administered. 
ECG leads and defibrillator pads were applied in mirror- 
image configuration. Anaesthesia was induced with midazolam 
0.12 mg/kg, fentanyl 1.5 µg/kg, propofol 3.1 mg/kg and rocuro-
nium 1.15 mg/kg. Airway was secured with a cuffed ETT, and 
anaesthesia maintained with sevoflurane 2.0%–2.5% in 40% O₂/
air. Volume- controlled ventilation was used with tidal volume 
7  mL/kg, Ppeak  ≤18  cmH₂O and EtCO₂ maintained between 
35–40 mm Hg. Emergency drugs were prepared in advance and 
immediately available (table 1).
An experienced paediatric cardiac anaesthesiologist supervised 
the induction and maintenance, and haemodynamic parameters 
remained stable throughout the procedure. Sugammadex 3 mg/
kg was administered for reversal, and extubation was performed 
fully awake.
OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP
The patient was observed for 90 min in the recovery unit, then 
transferred to the ward. No arrhythmia or haemodynamic insta-
bility occurred. The postoperative course was uneventful, and 
the patient was discharged the following day.

mirror- image anatomy and systemic right ventricular physiology

(figure 3). In ccTGA, systemic RV function depends on careful 
management of preload and afterload, while avoiding myocar -
dial depression and rhythm disturbances. 2 SIT adds complexity 
to monitoring setup, resuscitation orientation and surgical field 
positioning.3 Electrocardiographic interpretation may be erro-
neous if mirror- image lead placement is not used. Adopting 
reversed ECG and defibrillator pad configuration ensures accu -
rate rhythm monitoring. 4 Similarly, anaesthetic induction must 
anticipate conduction abnormalities; therefore, arrhythmia 
management drugs should be immediately available.5 In our case, 
multidisciplinary preparation and paediatric intensive care unit 
(ICU) readiness supported a safe perioperative course. Although 
sevoflurane is generally well tolerated in CHD, systemic RV 
physiology warrants avoidance of hypotension and hyper -
carbia.6 Ventilation strategies should maintain normocapnia 
and normoxia, while extubation should occur fully awake to 
minimise haemodynamic stress. 7 Perioperative goals are there-
fore directed toward preserving haemodynamic stability: main-
taining stable preload and afterload; avoiding hypoxaemia and 
hypercarbia that could increase pulmonary vascular resistance 
and titrating anaesthetics to minimise cardiodepressant effects 
(figure 4). Contemporary educational reviews synthesise these 
principles for children with CHD undergoing non- cardiac proce-
dures and highlight practical operating room considerations for 
trainees.4 The American Heart Association (AHA) scientific 
statement specifically emphasises vigilant rhythm surveillance, 
avoidance of physiological perturbations that intensify cardio-
pulmonary interactions and clear communication between 
anaesthesia, surgery and cardiology. 5 The 2024 ACC/AHA 
perioperative guideline, though adult- focused, reinforces risk 
stratification, multidisciplinary optimisation and individualised 
decision- making that can be appropriately adapted to complex 
paediatric cases.8
Risk in paediatric CHD is lesion, physiology and procedure- 
dependent; both narrative syntheses and cohort analyses indi-
cate substantial heterogeneity in perioperative outcomes, 
underscoring the value of structured preoperative assessment 
and planning. 6 9  Consistent with these recommendations, our 
plan incorporated cardiology consultation, paediatric ICU 
readiness, availability of a senior paediatric cardiac anaesthesi-
ologist and explicit arrhythmia management strategies. In SIT 
with dextrocardia, surface electrocardiography may be misin-
terpreted if mirror- image lead placement is not used; practical 
sources for CHD anaesthesia recommend systematic reorienta-
tion of monitoring and resuscitation equipment to the patient’s 
mirror anatomy. In our case, we adopted mirror image place -
ment of ECG leads and defibrillator pads and observed no 
arrhythmias, aligning with pragmatic guidance for non- cardiac 
procedures in CHD. 4  5  8Adenotonsillectomy introduces airway 
reactivity and bleeding/aspiration considerations that influence 
anaesthetic planning. Current paediatric ear, nose and throat 
(ENT) anaesthesia summaries support steady ventilation strate-
gies and deliberate timing of extubation to minimise haemody -
namic swings and airway events. 7 In CHD with systemic right 
ventricular physiology, these concerns strengthen the rationale 
for fully awake extubation with intact protective reflexes, facili-
tating reliable rhythm assessment and rapid detection of haemo-
dynamic instability.4 5
From an educational perspective, this case offers several 
important lessons for anaesthesiology trainees. It highlights the 
need for precise setup and interpretation of ECG monitoring 
in mirror- image anatomy, emphasises the value of transparent 
weight- based drug dosing to ensure reproducibility and safety, 
and demonstrates how established anaesthesia guidelines can 
be effectively translated to routine paediatric ENT procedures 
involving complex congenital anatomy. 4  5  8 Given the rarity of 
non- cardiac anaesthesia reports describing the coexistence of 
SIT and ccTGA, this experience underscores the importance of 
individualised, multidisciplinary planning to achieve safe periop-
erative outcomes. Relevant published reports are summarised in 
table 2.
The current case represents a unique educational scenario 
combining mirror- image anatomy (SIT) with systemic right 
ventricular physiology (ccTGA) during a routine ENT proce-
dure, emphasising the need for adapted monitoring and multi-
disciplinary planning.

Patient’s perspective
Written by the patient’s mother, translated by the authors from 
Turkish.
We were very anxious when we heard about our child’s heart 
condition and the need for surgery. The anaesthesiology team 
explained everything clearly, which helped us feel more secure. 
The surgery and recovery went smoothly, and we are grateful 
that our child quickly returned to normal daily life.
Learning points
 ► Mirror‑ image anatomy requires adjusted ECG and defibrillator 
pad placement.
 ► Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries 
mandates vigilant rhythm and afterload management to 
prevent systemic right ventricle stress.
 ► Weight‑ based drug dosing enhances reproducibility and 
safety.
 ► Emergency drugs should be prepared in advance with senior 
anaesthesiologist supervision.
 ► Awake extubation and intensive care unit monitoring 
optimise outcomes in complex congenital heart disease.
