ABSTRACT
Introduction : Conventionally, the surgical approach for Taussig–Bing anomaly (TBA) and transposition 
of the great arteries with aortic arch obstruction (AAO) has often involved a two‑stage 
strategy. However, in contemporary practice, most centers now favor a one‑stage repair. 
Despite this trend, the choice between one‑stage and two‑stage strategies may still arise 
in specific anatomical cases, such as those with interrupted AA (IAA) or in low‑weight 
infants.
Patients and 
Methods
: We conducted a review of our institutional experience with single‑stage correction 
in 24 consecutive cases of TBA associated with AAO. Each patient underwent a 
single‑stage correction that included an arterial switch operation (ASO) along with 
ventricular septal defect (VSD) closure through baffling of the left ventricle to the 
neo‑aorta and AA repair. Early mortality or reoperation was defined as death or 
the need for reoperation occurring prior to hospital discharge or within 30 days 
following ASO. Reintervention was classified as either a reoperation or a transcatheter 
procedure.
Results : The study comprised 24 cases. Among them, 6 (25%) patients had TBA with aortic 
coarctation, 15 (62.5%) patients had TBA with hypoplastic AA (HAA), 2 (8.3%) patients 
presented with TBA and IAA, and one patient had TBA with HAA alongside partial 
anomalous pulmonary venous connection. There were two early postoperative 
deaths (8.3%). Recoarctation occurred in 2 (8.3%) cases, both of which involved 
TBA with AA hypoplasia. Both patients successfully underwent balloon dilation of 
the coarctation after 285 and 312 days, respectively. One (4.2%) patient developed 
subaortic stenosis and underwent a modified Konno procedure after 233 days. 
The total number of reinterventions was 5  (20.8%), which included three cardiac 
catheterization procedures (two successful balloon dilations of the coarctation and 
one unsuccessful balloon dilation of the supravalvular stenosis), along with two 
surgical reinterventions.

INTRODUCTION
Aortic arch obstruction (AAO) can occur alongside various 
other cono-truncal anomalies.[1,2] In cases of double outlet 
right ventricle featuring a subpulmonary ventricular 
septal defect (VSD) and malposed great arteries (Taussig–
Bing anomaly [TBA]), the occurrence of AAO can be as high 
as 50%.[3,4] Conversely, its association with transposition 
of the great arteries (TGAs) is notably uncommon.
Patients presenting with TGA or TBA combined with 
AAO pose significant surgical challenges. Conventionally, 
the two-stage surgical strategy for TBA and TGA with 
accompanying AAO has been a common method. 
However, in recent times, most centers have shifted their 
preference to a one-stage repair. Nonetheless, in specific 
anatomical cases, such as those with interrupted AA (IAA) 
or low-weight infants, the debate between one-stage and 
two-stage strategies remains relevant today.[5-14]
With advancements in neonatal cardiac surgery, a 
single-stage repair that incorporates arterial switch 
operation (ASO) along with simultaneous arch repair 
has emerged as the favored technique,[10,15,16] particularly 
after demonstrating improved outcomes and reduced 
mortality and morbidity,[5,11]
In this report, we share our experience with a single-stage 
approach involving complete intracardiac repair and 
extensive simultaneous AA repair for patients with TBA 
and AAO. Our aim is to evaluate the outcomes of this 
single-stage correction and identify potential risk factors 
for mortality and complications.
PATIENTS AND METHODS
We conducted a review of our institutional experience 
involving single-stage correction with simultaneous arch 
repair in 24 consecutive cases of TBA associated with 
AAO, covering the period from January 2018 to January 
2024. All patients underwent a single-stage correction 
that included an ASO along with VSD closure through 
directing the left ventricle to the neo-aorta and AA repair.
Early death or reoperation was defined as death or the 
need for reoperation occurring before hospital discharge 
or within 30 days following ASO. Late death was classified 
as death occurring after discharge and more than 
30 days post-ASO. Reintervention was defined as either 
a reoperation or a transcatheter procedure.
Low cardiac output was diagnosed by elevated blood 
lactate levels or a rapid increase in blood lactate, 
decreased central venous oxygen saturation, an increased 
arterial to central venous oxygen saturation difference, 
decreased urine output, an increased peripheral skin 
temperature to core body temperature difference, and 
a low echocardiographic Doppler-derived cardiac index, 
in addition to a high inotrope requirement.
We used pRIFLE criteria for the diagnosis of acute 
kidney injury (AKI). The pRIFLE criteria classify AKI 
into three severity levels based on changes in estimated 
creatinine clearance (eCCl) and urine output: risk, 
eCCl decrease by 25% or urine output <0.5  mL/kg/h 
for 8 h; injury, eCCl decrease by 50% or urine output 
<0.5 mL/kg/h for 16 h; and failure, eCCl decrease by 
75% or eCCl <35 mL/min/1.73 m 2 or urine output 
<0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 h or anuria for 12 h.[17]
All patients were monitored with a 5-lead 
electrocardiogram, systemic oxygen saturation, end-tidal 
CO2, near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), and rectal 
temperature monitoring.
The aorta was cross-clamped at a core temperature 
of 30°C during systemic cooling, and myocardial 
protection was achieved through infusions of the 
histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutaratecardioplegic solution 
into the aortic root.
In the early stages of the series, we employed hypothermic 
circulatory arrest (HCA) for cases that required extensive 
arch repair. At 18°C, extracorporeal perfusion was 
stopped, and the AA was reconstructed under HCA. 
However, with more experience, we transitioned to 
selective antegrade cerebral perfusion. This involved 
direct cannulation of the brachiocephalic artery, 
followed by arch dissection, snaring of the great vessels 
and descending aorta, and cooling between 26°C and 
28°C. Cerebral perfusion was monitored using NIRS, with 
the flow from the cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) machine 
adjusted to 40%–50%.
The procedure was performed via a median sternotomy. 
The pericardium was opened, and cannulation of the 
ascending aorta and right atrium was carried out to 
Conclusions : The single‑stage repair involving VSD closure, AA reconstruction, and ASO is an 
applicable and safe option for patients with combined TBA and concomitant AAO. We 
recorded reinterventions for recurrent coarctation, supravalvular, and subvalvular aortic 
stenosis. Long‑term follow‑up is essential, and early percutaneous interventions may 
help lower the rates of repeat surgeries.

centimeter above the aortic valve, while the pulmonary 
artery was transected below its bifurcation. The coronary 
arteries were then implanted into their new positions 
without distortion or traction. The ascending aorta and 
pulmonary artery were switched using the Lecompte 
technique, where the ascending aorta was anastomosed 
to the neo-aorta, and the previous coronary position was 
repaired with the distal pulmonary artery and patches.
For coarctation of the aorta (COA) or IAA, end-to-side 
anastomosis is the most performed operation. However, 
in patients with limited coarctation or IAA, this method 
can lead to high tension and poor growth potential, 
increasing the risk of postoperative bleeding and 
recoarctation. Therefore, in these cases, we employed 
a modified end-to-side anastomosis with anterior patch 
augmentation with a bovine pericardial patch.
Statistical analysis
Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for 
the Social Sciences (SPSS, version 20.0, Chicago, IL, 
USA). All results were presented as frequencies (n), 
percentages (%), ranges, and means ± standard 
deviation (mean ± standard deviation). The survival 
rate and freedom from reoperation were calculated, with 
statistical significance indicated by P < 0.05.
RESULTS
The study included 24 cases, with a majority being 
males ( n = 15, 62.5%) and females comprising 
37.5% ( n = 9). The ages of the patients ranged 
from 17 days to 26 months, with a mean age of 
116.6 days ± 179.93 days. Their weights varied from 
2.4–6 kg, averaging 3.65 kg ± 0.797.
Among the patients, six (25%) had TBA with COA, 
15 (62.5%) had TBA with hypoplastic AA (HAA), 
two (8.3%) had IAA, and one patient had TBA with HAA 
and partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection 
(PAPVC) [Table 1].
All patients presented with a history of hypoxemia, with 
oxygen saturations ranging from 55% to 91% (mean 
75.57% ±9.78). Unusual coronary anatomy was noted in 
6 (25%) patients, characterized by either a single coronary 
artery or an intramural course. Preoperative subvalvular 
left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) 
was reported in another child (4.2%), with a maximal 
pressure gradient of 57 mmHg. None of the children 
exhibited valvular pulmonary stenosis or aortic 
stenosis, although 1 (4.2%) child had mild preoperative 
pulmonary regurgitation (PR) [Table 1].
A total of 23 patients underwent a single-stage ASO, VSD 
closure, and repair of the AA, while one patient received 
the same procedure along with repair of the partial 
anomalous venous connection [ Table 2 ]. Regarding 
the surgical technique for arch repair, 6 patients 
underwent a direct end-to-end anastomosis of the arch, 
while 18 (75%) patients had a modified end -to-side 
anastomosis with anterior patch augmentation using a 
bovine pericardial patch.
Postoperatively, nine patients experienced low cardiac 
output, which was successfully managed in the pediatric 
intensive care unit (PICU). In addition, 5 (20.83%) cases 
developed chest infections. One patient was diagnosed 
with postoperative pulmonary hypertension and received 
medical treatment for this condition. Four  (16.7%) 
cases developed renal failure attributed to low cardiac 
output and required peritoneal dialysis. Furthermore, 
5 (20.8%) cases presented to the PICU with an open 
chest, which was subsequently closed during their stay 
in the PICU. One patient experienced postoperative 
unilateral diaphragmatic paralysis, which was managed 
conservatively without the need for surgical plication. 
Three patients required extracorporeal membrane 
oxygenation, with one of them successfully weaned off 
after 3 days. The duration of stay in the PICU ranged 
from 4 to 24 days, while the total hospital stay varied 
from 7 to 31 days [Table 3].
The CPB time ranged from 120 to 390 min (mean 
254.4 min ± 71.86), and the aortic cross-clamp time 
varied from 120 to 255 min (mean 158.9 min ± 55.36). 
Twelve patients with modified end-to-side anastomosis 
with anterior patch augmentation required HCA, which 
includes 10 patients, 45.5% in the survival group, and 
the two mortalities [Table 4].

There were two early postoperative mortalities (8.3%), 
one boy and one girl, with mortality occurring between 
1 and 50 days postoperation (mean: 15.5 days ± 23.1). 
No significant relationships were found between age, 
weight at the time of operation, aortic cross-clamp time, 
diagnosis, and the risk of mortality or morbidity. The CPB 
time was longer in the two mortality cases, ranging from 
225 to 390 min, but this was not statistically significant 
compared to the survivor group. Both deceased patients 
had TBA with HAA; one had an intramural coronary 
artery, while the other had concomitant subvalvular 
LVOTO (pressure gradient ~56 mmHg) [Table 4]. The 
cause of death for the 2 cases was low cardiac output 
associated with AKI.
Follow-up duration ranged from 1 to 5 years, with no 
midterm or late mortalities reported. The survival rate at 
follow-up was 91.7%. Recoarctation occurred in 2 (8.3%) 
cases, both involving the TBA with AA hypoplasia, 
who were repaired using a modified end -to- side 
anastomosis with anterior patch augmentation using 
a bovine pericardial patch. These cases underwent 
successful balloon dilation of the coarctation after 285 
and 312 days, with no residual gradient, and they have 
not required any repeat surgical interventions since. 
One (4.2%) patient developed subaortic stenosis during 
the follow-up period and underwent a modified Konno 
procedure after 233 days. Another patient (4.2%) 
developed supravalvular aortic stenosis; interventional 
cardiac catheterization was performed with balloon 
dilation, but the gradient did not change significantly. 
Surgery was conducted after 271 days with patch 
augmentation of the ascending aorta. In total, there were 
five reinterventions (20.8%), including three cardiac 
catheterization interventions  (two successful balloon 
dilations of the coarctation and one unsuccessful balloon 
dilation of the supravalvular stenosis), in addition to two 
surgical reinterventions [Table 5].
In addition, another child (4.2%) developed supravalvular 
right ventricular outflow obstruction with a pressure 
gradient of 42  mmHg. This child is currently under 
regular follow-up, and no interventions have been 
performed yet. Thirteen (54.2%) children experienced 
neo-aortic incompetence (neo-AI). Among these, 2 (8.3%) 
cases were classified as trivial neo-AI, 10 (41.7%) cases 
as mild neo-AI, and 1 (4.2%) case as moderate neo-AI. 
Furthermore, 9 (37.5%) patients developed neo-PR, 
with 3 (12.5%) cases being trivial, 5 (20.8%) cases mild, 
and 1 (4.2%) case moderate neo-PR. Residual VSD was 
observed in 4 (16.7%) children, all of which were small 
and restrictive in nature. Importantly, none of the 
patients required reinterventions during the follow-up 
period [Table 5].
DISCUSSION
TBA with concomitant AAO presents a rare and complex 
challenge for congenital heart surgeons. It is notable 
that AAO occurs in over half of TBA patients undergoing 
ASO.[18]
The combination of TBA with AAO is among the most 
technically challenging anatomical subgroups for 
ASO. Factors such as aortopulmonary size mismatch, 
side-by-side great vessels, and septal malalignment 
contribute to right ventricular outflow tract obstruction 
(RVOTO), complicating surgical repair.[8,19,20]

A two-staged repair, involving primary reconstruction 
of the AA and excision of ductal tissue (with or without 
pulmonary artery banding) through left thoracotomy, 
followed by an arterial switch and intracardiac repair, 
is often the preferred surgical approach for TGA with 
IAA. This approach remains the treatment of choice 
for patients with right ventricular (RV) hypoplasia 
or significant comorbid conditions like necrotizing 
enterocolitis or brain injury.[20]
Konstantinov et al.[21]  supported this method to 
minimize the duration of CPB time necessary for the 
subsequent ASO and VSD closure. However, mortality 
rates associated with the two-staged repair are relatively 
high, [5,22] primarily due to tubular hypoplasia of the 
AA. The negative effects of pulmonary artery banding 
can lead to high incidences of postoperative neo-aortic 
regurgitation, systemic ventricular dysfunction, and 
distortion of branch pulmonary arteries. [23,24] But the 
requirement for a second -stage operation could arise 
earlier than anticipated if banding is not performed due 
to deteriorating congestive cardiac failure.[23]
In our study, we included 24 cases: 6 (25%) patients had 
TBA with COA, 15 (62.5%) had TBA with HAA, 2 (8.3%) 
had TBA with IAA, and one (4.2%)  had TBA with HAA 
and PAPVC [Table 1].
With advancements in neonatal cardiac surgery, 
single-stage repair for TGA with IAA has become the 
preferred surgical plan, leading to decreased operative 
mortality rates. This approach has several benefits, 
including the early restoration of normal physiological 
circulation and the avoidance of complications associated 
with pulmonary artery banding.[25]
In our study, there were no midterm or late mortalities, 
and the survival rate at follow-up was 91.7%. However, 
there were two early postoperative mortalities (8.3%). 
The CPB time was longer in these two cases, ranging 
from 225 to 390 min, but this was not statistically 
significant compared to the survivor group. Both 
mortality cases involved TBA with HAA, with one child 
having an intramural coronary artery and the other 
having concomitant subvalvular LVOTO (pressure 
gradient ~56 mmHg) [Table 4]. The presence of 
intermural coronaries and concomitant subvalvular 
LVOTO likely contributed to the complexity of these cases 
and may explain the cause of mortality.
Literature indicates that postoperative complications are 
often linked to high reintervention rates and may include 
recurrent coarctation, supravalvular aortic stenosis, 
neo-aortic valve regurgitation, obstruction of the right 
heart system, coronary stenosis, and other complications 
such as arrhythmia, bleeding, left phrenic nerve 
paralysis, left recurrent laryngeal nerve impairment, 
chylothorax, low cardiac output, renal failure, brain 
injury, and sepsis. Diagnostic tools such as chest X-rays, 
electrocardiograms, echocardiographic assessments, and 
diagnostic catheterizations can aid in decision-making 
during postoperative care.[25]
In our study, recoarctation developed in 2 (8.3%) cases, 
both of which were TBAs with AA hypoplasia. Both 
patients underwent successful balloon dilatation of the 
coarctation after 285 and 312 days, respectively, with 
no residual gradient, and to date, they have not required 
any repeat surgical interventions [Table 5].
Except for Mohammadi et al.,[26]  who identified 
transpulmonary VSD closure as a significant risk factor 
for impaired valve function, most studies have regarded 
aortic valve regurgitation as an infrequent postoperative 
complication that necessitates repeat surgery.[5,22,23]
Right-sided obstruction, which includes RVOTO, stenosis 
of the pulmonary valve, and issues with the pulmonary 
trunk and its branches, is a primary indication for 
reintervention.[27] In our current study, only 1 (4.2%) 
child developed supravalvular right ventricular outflow 
obstruction with a pressure gradient of 42 mmHg. 
Fortunately, this child is currently under regular 
follow-up, and no interventions have been performed 
yet.
Some studies have shown that no significant risk factors 
for postoperative RVOTO were found in patients with 
TBAs and AAO, despite the presence of various coronary 
anomalies, nonanteroposterior great arteries, and 
preoperative organic subaortic stenosis.[9,23]
In addition, 1 (4.2%) patient developed subaortic 
stenosis during the follow-up period and underwent 
a modified Konno procedure after 233 days. Another 
patient (4.2%) developed supravalvular aortic stenosis; 
interventional cardiac catheterization was performed 
with balloon dilatation, but the gradient did not change 
significantly. Surgery was subsequently performed after

Postoperative supravalvular aortic stenosis has been 
reported as a contributing factor to operative mortality 
following ASO, potentially due to the size discrepancy 
between the dilated neo-aortic root and the undersized 
ascending aorta. In such cases, a wide patch augmentation 
of the entire arch is often required.[28]
CONCLUSIONS
A single-stage repair involving VSD closure, reconstruction 
of the AA, and ASO is a viable approach for patients with 
combined TBA and concomitant AAO. This surgical 
method has demonstrated safety, with low mortality 
rates and low risk for reintervention during short-term 
follow-up. Although reinterventions for issues such as 
recurrent coarctation, supravalvular aortic stenosis, 
right heart system obstruction, and coronary stenosis 
have been reported, long- term follow- up is essential. 
Additionally, early percutaneous interventions may help 
reduce the rates of repeated surgeries.
